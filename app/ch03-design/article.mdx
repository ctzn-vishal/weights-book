## The cascade, estimated the way NCHS estimates it

The target population is U.S. civilian noninstitutionalized adults 18 and older, excluding pregnant women, in August 2021–August 2023. The definitions follow NCHS Data Brief 511 <Cite id="nchs_db511" /> exactly. Blood pressure is the average of up to three oscillometric readings taken in the mobile examination center (MEC). Hypertension means a systolic pressure of 130 mm Hg or more, a diastolic pressure of 80 or more, or a yes to "Are you now taking any medication prescribed by a doctor for your high blood pressure?" Awareness is a yes to having ever been told of hypertension by a doctor or other health professional. Treatment is the medication question. Control means systolic below 130 and diastolic below 80. Awareness, treatment, and control are shares of adults with hypertension, not of all adults, and not of those at the previous stage. Among adults with hypertension, <Fact id="ch3.htn_aware" /> had been told so by a health professional, <Fact id="ch3.htn_treat" /> were taking medication for it, and <Fact id="ch3.htn_control" /> had blood pressure below 130/80. With prevalence, these form the hypertension cascade.

The August 2021–August 2023 files hide two traps. The examination weight is `WTMEC2YR`; `WTMECPRP` belongs to the pre-pandemic file. And the medication item changed: `BPQ_L` replaced `BPQ040A` and `BPQ050A` with `BPQ150` and a new skip pattern, so only a yes counts as treated. Under that rule, <Fact id="ch3.htn_unknown_med" /> adults with normal readings and unknown medication status stay in the denominator as not hypertensive, and the analytic sample is <Fact id="ch3.htn_n" /> adults, <Fact id="ch3.htn_n_hyp" /> of them with hypertension, the brief's counts exactly.

## Three standard errors for one number

The prevalence estimate is a weighted ratio, the Hájek estimator:

$$
\hat p = \frac{\sum_i w_i y_i}{\sum_i w_i},
$$

where $$y_i$$ is 1 for hypertension and $$w_i$$ is `WTMEC2YR`. With correct inclusion weights, $$\hat p$$ is a consistent estimator of one specified finite-population quantity: the share of the target population with hypertension. The weights define what the formula estimates. They do not say how far the estimate would move if the survey were run again.

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

A naive standard error treats the examined adults as a simple random sample. NHANES instead draws a few dozen primary sampling units (PSUs), counties or groups of counties, and examines many people in each. Will the design-based standard error be smaller than the naive one, about the same, or larger? By how much?

</Callout>

Three standard errors answer three different questions about repetition. The **naive** standard error, $$\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/(n-1)}$$, imagines repeated simple random samples of the same size and ignores the weights. The **weights-only** standard error is the sandwich (HC1) formula for a weighted mean: it charges for unequal weights but still treats each respondent as an independent draw. The **full-design** standard error uses Taylor linearization. Replace each respondent by a linearized value $$z_i = w_i(y_i - \hat p)/\sum_j w_j$$, add those values within each PSU to get a total $$z_{hj}$$ for PSU $$j$$ in stratum $$h$$, and compare PSUs within strata:

$$
\widehat{\Var}(\hat p) = \sum_{h} \frac{n_h}{n_h - 1} \sum_{j=1}^{n_h} \left(z_{hj} - \bar z_h\right)^2 .
$$

PSUs enter as the units of independent information. Strata enter only through the within-stratum comparison, which keeps differences between strata out of the variance; that is where stratification's precision gain comes from <Cite id="lumley2010,heeringa2017" />. The weights-only formula is the same expression with every respondent as its own PSU and a single stratum.

<Figure n="3.2" caption="The full-design standard error is well above both simpler ones, and by how much depends on the estimand.">
  <Chart id="ch3.three_ses" />
</Figure>

For prevalence, the three standard errors are <Fact id="ch3.se_prev_naive" />, <Fact id="ch3.se_prev_w" />, and <Fact id="ch3.se_prev_d" /> percentage points (Figure 3.2). The ratio of the design variance to the naive variance is the design effect, $$\DEFF$$ <Cite id="kish1965" />: here <Fact id="ch3.deff_prev" />. It factors exactly into two pieces. The weighting factor, the squared ratio of the weights-only to the naive standard error, is <Fact id="ch3.wfac_prev" />. The clustering-and-stratification factor, the squared ratio of the design to the weights-only standard error, is <Fact id="ch3.cfac_prev" />. For prevalence, the <Fact id="ch3.htn_n" /> examined adults carry about as much information as <Fact id="ch3.neff_prev" /> adults drawn at random.

The slogan "weights get the point estimate, the design gets the uncertainty" needs a correction in each half. The weights help define the estimator, and they also shape its variance twice over: unequal weights inflate it, and the weights are themselves built from the sample, since nonresponse and calibration adjustments are estimates. The repeated-sampling variance therefore depends on the sampling design and on how the weights were constructed. Taylor linearization applied to the final weights alone treats them as fixed. A calibration-aware linearization can carry the construction step, but it needs the auxiliary variables and control totals behind the adjustments; replicate methods that repeat the weighting steps in every replicate carry it without them.

Degrees of freedom are the other half of the story. The public August 2021–August 2023 file has <Fact id="ch3.nhanes_psu" /> masked PSUs in <Fact id="ch3.nhanes_strata" /> strata, and the usual Taylor rule, PSUs minus strata, gives <Fact id="ch3.nhanes_df" /> degrees of freedom: the number of independent within-stratum comparisons behind the variance estimate. With so few, the 97.5th percentile of t is <Fact id="ch3.t_crit_df" /> rather than <Fact id="ch3.t_crit_z" />. Together with the larger standard error, the design-based interval is <Fact id="ch3.ci_width_ratio" /> times as wide as the naive one.

```python
import polars as pl
import svy

# nh: one row per MEC-examined participant (WTMEC2YR > 0) with 0/1 columns
# htn and analytic built as defined above. PSU codes repeat across strata.
nh = nh.with_columns((pl.col("SDMVSTRA") * 10 + pl.col("SDMVPSU")).alias("psu"))
design = svy.Design(stratum="SDMVSTRA", psu="psu", wgt="WTMEC2YR")
sample = svy.Sample(nh, design=design)
prev = sample.estimation.mean("htn", where=svy.col("analytic") == 1, deff="wr")
```

```r
des <- svydesign(ids = ~SDMVPSU, strata = ~SDMVSTRA, weights = ~WTMEC2YR,
                 nest = TRUE, data = nh)
svymean(~htn, subset(des, analytic), deff = "replace")
```

The two calls return the same estimate, standard error, design effect, and degrees of freedom to machine precision; this chapter's build checks both against a hand-written Taylor routine.

### The Moulton bridge

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Suppose two people in the same PSU are barely more alike than two strangers, an intraclass correlation well under one percent. Can that nearly double a variance?

</Callout>

Economists know the clustering component under another name. For a regressor that is constant within clusters, with equal cluster sizes $$\bar m$$ and equicorrelated errors, <Cite id="moulton1990" narrative /> showed that conventional variances understate the truth by the factor

$$
1 + (\bar m - 1)\rho .
$$

NHANES examines about <Fact id="ch3.moulton_mbar" /> analytic-sample adults per PSU, so an intraclass correlation of only <Fact id="ch3.moulton_rho" /> reproduces the clustering factor of <Fact id="ch3.cfac_prev" />. The answer to the prediction is yes: with clusters that large, a correlation that small nearly doubles the variance. Read the formula as a bridge, not an identity. The Moulton factor is a special-case regression analogue of the clustering component of a design effect: it assumes equal cluster sizes, leaves out the stratification gain, and says nothing about the weighting factor. [Chapter 6](/ch06-clustering) takes up clustering in regression.

The separate 2017–2018 cycle stays out: the pre-pandemic file already contains its participants under new identifiers, so pooling the two would count them twice. The medication question changed wording and skip pattern between the periods, and the ledger records that caveat.

## Age standardization: a third kind of weight

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Crude prevalence is higher among men than among women by <Fact id="ch3.sex_gap_crude" /> percentage points, and women in the population are older on average. After both groups are put on one age distribution, will the gap shrink or grow?

</Callout>

Survey weights $$w_i$$ repair representation: they make the sample stand for the population that existed in 2021–2023, with its own age structure. Standardization weights $$s_a$$ do something else. They replace that age structure with a fixed standard, which redefines the population in which the comparison is made:

$$
\hat p^{\text{adj}} = \sum_{a} s_a \hat p_a, \qquad s_a = (0.4203,\ 0.3572,\ 0.2225),
$$

where $$\hat p_a$$ is the survey-weighted prevalence in the age groups 18–39, 40–59, and 60 and older, and $$s_a$$ is each group's share of the 2000 U.S. standard population, the NCHS convention. The $$s_a$$ are constants, so the variance comes from linearizing the three $$\hat p_a$$ jointly under the design.

Crude prevalence is <Fact id="ch3.htn_prev" />; age-adjusted prevalence is <Fact id="ch3.htn_prev_adj" ci /> (Figure 3.6). For men the two are <Fact id="ch3.htn_prev_men" /> and <Fact id="ch3.htn_prev_men_adj" />; for women, <Fact id="ch3.htn_prev_women" /> and <Fact id="ch3.htn_prev_women_adj" />. The sex gap widens from <Fact id="ch3.sex_gap_crude" ci /> to <Fact id="ch3.sex_gap_adj" ci /> percentage points. The crude comparison sets an older female population against a younger male one; standardization removes that difference in age composition. Neither number is "the" prevalence. The crude share describes the actual population, and the adjusted share describes a hypothetical one with the standard's age distribution, which is what makes groups and periods comparable. The pre-pandemic crude and adjusted values, <Fact id="ch3.pre_prev" /> and <Fact id="ch3.pre_prev_adj" />, show the same wedge.

## Domains: subset the design, not the data

Awareness, treatment, and control are domain estimates. They describe adults with hypertension, a subgroup whose sample size is itself random. The textbook rule is to declare the design on the full sample and then restrict the estimate, never to delete rows first. What the rule buys is easiest to see in the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) 2023 <Cite id="ipums_nhis" />, which has far more PSUs than NHANES: <Fact id="ch3.nhis_psu" /> PSUs in <Fact id="ch3.nhis_strata" /> strata and <Fact id="ch3.nhis_df" /> design degrees of freedom. The analysis uses the sample-adult weight `SAMPWEIGHT` and a (`STRATA`, `PSU`) key built within the year, because PSU numbers repeat across strata. For self-reported hypertension, <Fact id="ch3.nhis_hyp" ci /> of <Fact id="ch3.nhis_n" /> sample adults, the design effect is <Fact id="ch3.nhis_deff" />.

Start with the defensible-looking mistake: filter to the domain, then declare the design on what remains. For a dense domain, cost-related delay in medical care among adults with hypertension (<Fact id="ch3.dense_est" ci /> of <Fact id="ch3.dense_n" /> adults), the delete-rows-first standard error is only <Fact id="ch3.dense_se_gap" /> smaller than the correct one. For a sparse domain, uninsured adults in poor health with <Fact id="ch3.sparse_n" /> respondents, the correct standard error of the same share is <Fact id="ch3.sparse_se_full" /> percentage points and the delete-rows-first one is <Fact id="ch3.sparse_se_sub" />, which is <Fact id="ch3.sparse_short" /> too small. For the weighted count of adults who delayed care, the shortfall is <Fact id="ch3.sparse_total_short" />. The proportion itself, with an effective sample size of <Fact id="ch3.sparse_neff" />, would fail the NCHS presentation standards <Cite id="parker2017" />; it appears here only for its standard error.

The mechanism is collapsed strata, not subsetting as such. The domain's members sit in <Fact id="ch3.sparse_psus" /> PSUs spread over <Fact id="ch3.sparse_strata" /> strata. Once the other rows are gone, <Fact id="ch3.sparse_single" /> of those strata contain a single PSU, and the within-stratum comparison has nothing to compare. Declared on the full design, the same strata keep all their PSUs. PSUs with no domain members contribute totals of zero, which is how the formula learns that the domain's size varies from one sample to the next. That source of variation matters most for totals.

<Figure n="3.7" caption="In a sparse domain, deleting rows first makes the standard error too small or too large, depending on a rule the analyst may never have chosen.">
  <Chart id="ch3.lonely_rules" />
</Figure>

The software has to be told what a lonely PSU contributes, and each answer is a modeling decision (Figure 3.7). Under *certainty* or *remove*, it contributes zero, as if that PSU had been selected with certainty. Under *adjust*, it is centered at the grand mean rather than at its stratum mean. Under *average*, the other strata's variance is scaled up to stand in for it. For the sparse domain these rules give <Fact id="ch3.sparse_se_sub" />, <Fact id="ch3.sparse_se_adjust" />, and <Fact id="ch3.sparse_se_average" /> percentage points, against the correct <Fact id="ch3.sparse_se_full" />. R's default, `survey.lonely.psu = "fail"`, stops with an error, and `svy` raises `SingletonError` rather than guessing. Both defaults are right: they force the decision into the open.

```python
# Wrong: delete rows, then declare. svy refuses to guess.
sub = svy.Sample(adults.filter(pl.col("sparse") == 1), design=design)
sub.estimation.mean("delay")            # raises SingletonError
sub.singleton.handle("certainty")       # an explicit rule you must report
# Right: keep every PSU and restrict the estimate.
svy.Sample(adults, design=design).estimation.mean("delay", where=svy.col("sparse") == 1)
```

How sparse is too sparse? A controlled experiment gives a gradual answer, not a threshold (Figure 3.8). Keep a random subset of sample adults, shrinking it from everyone down to <Fact id="ch3.thin_n_02" /> respondents, and compare the two standard errors for self-reported hypertension across 200 random domains of each size. Down to domains of about one percent of the sample, the median ratio of the delete-rows-first standard error to the correct one stays within a percent of one (<Fact id="ch3.thin_ratio_1" />). Below that the understatement grows gradually as more strata lose PSUs, and it becomes serious only in very small domains: at <Fact id="ch3.thin_n_02" /> respondents, a median of <Fact id="ch3.thin_single_02" /> strata are left with one PSU, the median ratio falls to <Fact id="ch3.thin_ratio_02" />, and <Fact id="ch3.thin_share_02" /> of the domains get a standard error more than 10 percent too small.

<Figure n="3.8" caption="As the domain shrinks and strata lose PSUs, the delete-rows-first standard error drifts gradually below the correct one; the understatement becomes serious only in very small domains.">
  <Chart id="ch3.thinning" />
</Figure>

Two reporting rules follow. First, put the PSUs retained and the number of single-PSU strata next to every domain standard error; for every NHANES domain in this chapter, all PSUs contain domain members and no stratum is lonely. Second, state the degrees of freedom separately from the variance. NCHS counts only the PSUs and strata that contain domain members, and R and `svy` report the same count, <Fact id="ch3.sparse_df" /> for the sparse domain, although the full design has <Fact id="ch3.nhis_df" />. The variance formula and the reference distribution are two decisions, and a report should state both.

"Complex survey" names a family, not a property. ## Interlude: weighting repairs representation, not measurement

Weights can correct for who answered. They cannot correct what was measured. Three surveys ask adults whether a health professional ever told them they had high blood pressure: BRFSS 2023 gives <Fact id="ch3.brfss_bphigh" ci />, NHIS 2023 gives <Fact id="ch3.nhis_hyp" />, and NHANES's own interview gives <Fact id="ch3.nhanes_selfreport" />. Those three agree within a few percentage points. NHANES's measured definition gives <Fact id="ch3.htn_prev" />, and the older 140/90 threshold applied to the same readings gives <Fact id="ch3.nhanes_htn140" /> (Figure 3.10). The gap between self-report and measurement is not a representation error that better weights could close. The surveys estimate different constructs: a remembered diagnosis on one side, a blood pressure reading plus medication use on the other. BRFSS asks this item only in odd years, and 2023 omits Kentucky and Pennsylvania, so its population is adults in 48 states and the District of Columbia.

NHANES shows the same lesson inside one survey. In 2017–2018, NCHS measured the same examinees with both the old auscultatory protocol and the new oscillometric device, in random order <Cite id="nhanes" />. For those <Fact id="ch3.instr_n" /> adults, with identical weights, mean systolic pressure was <Fact id="ch3.instr_sbp_diff" ci /> mm Hg higher by auscultation. ## Replicate weights, briefly

NHANES releases masked strata and PSUs, so Taylor linearization is the producer's method. Other surveys ship replicate weights instead: the American Community Survey provides 80 alternative weight columns and the CPS ASEC provides 160. Replicate weights are the survey world's pre-packaged repeated sampling. Re-estimate once with each column, and the spread of the replicate estimates, scaled by a method-specific constant, approximates the sampling variance. Balanced repeated replication, Fay's method <Cite id="fay1995" />, the jackknife, successive difference replication, and the bootstrap are distinct procedures with different constants and degrees of freedom, and calling them all "a bootstrap" hides exactly the constants an analyst must get right. When the producer repeats the nonresponse and calibration steps inside each replicate, the replicate variance also captures the weight-construction variability that Taylor linearization with fixed final weights leaves out. [Lab 3](/ch03-lab-replicates) works through the American Community Survey case, including a `svy` default that silently ignores declared replicate weights.

<Callout kind="note" title="A new case">

Two groups' 95 percent intervals overlap. Does that settle whether the difference between the groups is statistically significant?

<Details summary="One answer">

No. For two independent estimates with equal standard errors, the standard error of their difference is $$\sqrt{2}$$ times one of them, not twice, so two intervals can overlap while the interval for the difference excludes zero. When the estimates share PSUs or respondents, their covariance changes the answer again. Estimate the difference itself and give it a standard error under the design, as the period comparison in this chapter does with two independent samples and as [Lab 3](/ch03-lab-replicates) does with replicate weights.

</Details>
