## Is our state worse than the one next door?

A reporter calls, or a dean does, with the same question: is our state worse than the one next door? The state uninsured rates came out this morning, your state sits a few places below its neighbor in the table, and you are the person on campus who works with the American Community Survey (ACS). You know that both rates are estimates. What you have to decide before you answer is whether the survey can tell the two places apart, and the answer is on the public-use file, in 80 weight columns most users never open.

Each September the Census Bureau releases the previous year's ACS, and within a day the state uninsured rates are a league table. The 2024 table for working-age adults reads: Texas first, Georgia second, Oklahoma third, Arizona tenth, Massachusetts last. Texas has the highest share of adults aged 19 to 64 without health insurance, <Fact id="ch3lab.top_rate" />, and Massachusetts the lowest, <Fact id="ch3lab.bottom_rate" />, against a national rate of <Fact id="ch3lab.us_rate" ci />. A governor's office in a state that dropped three places wants to know why. An advocacy group in a state that climbed two has the press release drafted.

Every step down the table is a claim about neighbors: this state's rate is higher than the next one's. There are 50 such neighboring pairs. With textbook standard errors, <Fact id="ch3lab.adj_sig_naive" /> of them differ at the 90 percent level. With the standard errors the ACS was built to support, <Fact id="ch3lab.adj_sig_sdr" /> do. The estimated rates have not moved. The evidence for separating neighboring places has, and a story about who moved up or down is mostly a story about pairs the data cannot tell apart.

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Only a handful of neighboring pairs survive the design-based test. Before reading on, guess where in the table they sit: at the two ends, in the crowded middle, or scattered throughout. And guess whether a state's sample size or its rate decides the matter.

</Callout>

The rates describe adults aged 19 to 64 in the civilian noninstitutionalized population: everyone except residents of institutions such as prisons and nursing homes, and members of the Armed Forces. This is the Medicaid-relevant age group. Children have their own Medicaid and CHIP eligibility rules, nearly everyone 65 and older has Medicare, and the adults in between are the ones whose coverage depends most on each state's Medicaid choices. The data are the ACS 2024 1-year public-use sample from IPUMS <Cite id="ipums_usa" />, restricted to the Census Bureau's universe for health insurance: <Fact id="ch3lab.us_n" /> adults aged 19 to 64, from <Fact id="ch3lab.n_min" /> in Wyoming to <Fact id="ch3lab.n_max" /> in California. Every test in this lab is a per-comparison test at the 90 percent level, the Census Bureau's convention for ACS comparisons. A set of such tests does not certify a whole ranking. It says which places the data can tell apart.

## The naive test

The quickest way to attach uncertainty to each rate is the textbook standard error for a proportion from a simple random sample, $$\SE_{\text{iid}} = \sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$$, followed by a test for every pair of states. At the 90 percent level, that analysis finds <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_sig_naive" /> of the <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_total" /> pairs significantly different, including <Fact id="ch3lab.adj_sig_naive" /> of the 50 pairs that sit next to each other in the ranking.

The analysis looks defensible and is wrong. It treats the ACS as a simple random sample of independent people. The ACS samples addresses and records everyone living at each one, so members of a household, who often share an insurance status, enter the sample together. Its weights also vary widely across respondents, and unequal weights add variance that the iid formula never sees.

## Replicate weights: repeated sampling, pre-packaged

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

The naive standard error for Texas is <Fact id="ch3lab.top_se_iid" /> percentage points. Is the design-based standard error about the same, a little larger, or more than half again as large?

</Callout>

A standard error answers a repeated-sampling question: draw another sample under the same design, weight it the same way, and how far does the estimate move? With the public-use file come 80 alternative sets of person weights (REPWTP1 to REPWTP80 in IPUMS). Each starts from a systematic perturbation of the sample's base weights and then goes through the full weighting again, with the nonresponse adjustments and the calibration to population controls redone for every replicate. Re-estimating a statistic with each set shows how far it moves.

That is why replicates travel with the data. The final weight is itself an estimate, and only the producer can repeat its own weighting on a perturbed sample. The spread of the replicate estimates therefore includes the variance that weight construction adds, and the variance that calibration removes. An ordinary standard-error formula applied to the final weight column cannot recover either, because the column alone does not reveal how it was built.<SideNote>A calibration-aware linearization could, but it needs the calibration's control totals and the weighting areas they apply to, which are not on the public file.</SideNote>

The ACS uses successive difference replication (SDR), developed at the Census Bureau for state and county income and poverty estimates <Cite id="fay1995" />. It is one of several replication procedures, alongside balanced repeated replication (BRR), Fay's variant of BRR, the jackknife, and the bootstrap. They form replicates differently and convert the spread into a variance with different constants; calling them all "the bootstrap" blurs procedures that are not interchangeable. For the ACS <Cite id="census_acs_dm" />:

$$
\widehat{\Var}(\hat\theta) = \frac{4}{80}\sum_{r=1}^{80}\bigl(\hat\theta_r - \hat\theta\bigr)^2, \qquad \text{df} = 79,
$$

where $$\hat\theta$$ is computed with the final weight $$w_i$$ (PERWT) and $$\hat\theta_r$$ with the replicate weight $$w_i^{(r)}$$ (REPWTP$$r$$). The ACS publishes 90 percent margins of error (MOE), $$1.645 \times \SE$$.

For Texas the replicate standard error is <Fact id="ch3lab.top_se_sdr" /> percentage points, more than half again the naive <Fact id="ch3lab.top_se_iid" />. Across states the ratio of the two runs from <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_min" /> in Oklahoma to <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_max" /> in Minnesota, with a median of <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_median" />.<SideNote>The ratio belongs to the estimate, not to the survey, as [Chapter 3](/ch03-design) argues for the design effect. For the national rate it is <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_us" />, larger than for any state. Two states' replicate deviations are only slightly correlated, <Fact id="ch3lab.cross_corr_mean" /> on average, but the national estimate adds up every pair: those covariances supply <Fact id="ch3lab.us_cov_share" /> of its variance and make its standard error <Fact id="ch3lab.us_se_over_indep" /> what independent state estimates would give.</SideNote> A standard error that uses the weights but still treats people as independent closes much of that gap, yet it misses in both directions: depending on the state, the replicate standard error is between <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_wo_min" /> and <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_wo_max" /> the weights-only one. ## Which differences are distinguishable

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Two states' 90 percent margins of error overlap. Can the difference between them still be statistically significant at the 90 percent level?

</Callout>

It can. A difference between two states is itself an estimate, and it gets the same treatment: compute it on every replicate and take the SDR spread of the replicate differences.

$$
\widehat{\Var}(\hat\theta_a-\hat\theta_b) = \frac{4}{80}\sum_{r=1}^{80}\Bigl[\bigl(\hat\theta_{a,r}-\hat\theta_{b,r}\bigr)-\bigl(\hat\theta_a-\hat\theta_b\bigr)\Bigr]^2
$$

For independent estimates with equal standard errors, the standard error of the difference is $$\sqrt{2}$$ times one of them, not twice, so overlapping intervals are not a test. The replicate formula also carries the covariance between the two estimates without being asked. For two different states the covariance is small: swapping in the independence formula $$\sqrt{\SE_a^2 + \SE_b^2}$$ changes the verdict for <Fact id="ch3lab.indep_flips" /> of the <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_total" /> pairs. It matters when one estimate contains the other. California minus the nation has a replicate standard error of <Fact id="ch3lab.state_us_se_rep" /> percentage points; treating the two as independent gives <Fact id="ch3lab.state_us_se_ind" />.

With the replicate standard error of each difference, <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_sig_sdr" /> pairs are distinguishable, against <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_sig_naive" /> in the naive analysis. The totals hide where the change is. Most pairs sit far apart in the ranking and differ under any method, while a league table's claims are about neighbors. Of the 50 adjacent pairs, <Fact id="ch3lab.adj_sig_sdr" /> are distinguishable, not <Fact id="ch3lab.adj_sig_naive" /> (Figure 3L.2). One place apart, <Fact id="ch3lab.gap1_sdr" /> of pairs are distinguishable (<Fact id="ch3lab.gap1_naive" /> under the naive test), and every pair <Fact id="ch3lab.gap_all_sdr" /> or more places apart is distinguishable (under the naive test, every pair <Fact id="ch3lab.gap_all_naive" /> or more apart).

<Figure n="3L.2" caption="Neighbors in the ranking are rarely distinguishable; naive standard errors make many more of them look different.">

</Figure>

The pairwise tests also give each state a range of ranks compatible with them (Figure 3L.3). The highest compatible rank is one plus the number of states whose rate is significantly higher; the lowest is 51 minus the number whose rate is significantly lower. Texas is first and Massachusetts last under every test. That answers the opening prediction: the distinguishable neighbors are at the ends, where the rates are far from the pack. Indiana, the median state, is compatible with any rank in <Fact id="ch3lab.median_rank_range" /> and is distinguishable from <Fact id="ch3lab.median_distinct" /> of the other 50 (the naive analysis says <Fact id="ch3lab.median_distinct_naive" />). Only <Fact id="ch3lab.sure_top" /> states remain in the top ten under every ordering compatible with the tests; <Fact id="ch3lab.maybe_top" /> are in it under at least one. These ranges summarize a set of per-comparison tests. They are not simultaneous confidence intervals for ranks, which would need a joint method.

These are per-comparison tests, the ACS convention, and across <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_total" /> comparisons some false positives are expected. Holding the chance of any false positive to 10 percent with a Bonferroni correction leaves <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_sig_bonf" /> distinguishable pairs.

## Worse at what? The same states, a second table

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Restrict the same file to adults aged 55 to 64, the group closest to Medicare, and rank the states again. Will the order stay roughly the same, and will more or fewer neighboring pairs be distinguishable?

</Callout>

The dean's question has a hidden clause: worse at what, for whom? A ranking belongs to an estimand. Change the population and the table changes, and it changes by more than the uncertainty in either table would suggest. Among adults 55 to 64 the national uninsured rate is <Fact id="ch3lab.old_us_rate" ci />, and the same code, universe, and replicate variance give a second table from <Fact id="ch3lab.old_n_total" /> respondents. Texas is still first and Massachusetts still last. In between, <Fact id="ch3lab.old_moved5" /> states move five or more places and <Fact id="ch3lab.old_moved10" /> move ten or more (Figure 3L.4). Delaware and New Jersey move the most, <Fact id="ch3lab.old_move_max" /> places each. Delaware is <Fact id="ch3lab.mover_rank_all" />th among all working-age adults, with an uninsured rate of <Fact id="ch3lab.mover_rate_all" ci />, and <Fact id="ch3lab.mover_rank_old" />th among adults 55 to 64, at <Fact id="ch3lab.mover_rate_old" ci />. Its rank ranges in the two tables, <Fact id="ch3lab.mover_range_all" /> and <Fact id="ch3lab.mover_range_old" />, barely touch. That is not sampling noise moving one number. It is two different questions with two different answers, and a state can be middling at one and near the bottom at the other.

The second table is also less able to separate its own neighbors. The smallest state sample falls to <Fact id="ch3lab.old_n_min" />, in the District of Columbia, and the largest coefficient of variation among the 51 rates is <Fact id="ch3lab.old_cv_max" />, close to the level at which producers suppress estimates. The replicate standard error is a median <Fact id="ch3lab.old_ratio_median" /> the naive one, and it distinguishes <Fact id="ch3lab.old_pairs_sig_sdr" /> of the <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_total" /> pairs, down from <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_sig_sdr" /> in the working-age table, and <Fact id="ch3lab.old_adj_sig_sdr" /> of the 50 adjacent pairs. The naive test still claims <Fact id="ch3lab.old_pairs_sig_naive" /> pairs and <Fact id="ch3lab.old_adj_sig_naive" /> adjacent ones; here its error is spread over the whole table rather than concentrated among neighbors. So the honest answer to the dean has two parts: state which population the ranking describes, and then say whether the two places differ under the replicate test in that table, which for neighbors they usually do not.

## Re-rank on every replicate

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Wyoming ranks ninth and Arizona tenth among working-age adults. If you recompute the ranking with each set of replicate weights, which of the two stays in the top ten more often?

</Callout>

<ReplicateRanking id="ch3lab.state_replicates" />

Each replicate yields all 51 state rates, so it yields a complete ranking. The widget's top-k view re-ranks the states on every replicate and counts how often each lands in the top ten; its uncertainty view marks which states differ from any state you pick. One adjustment comes first. A single SDR replicate deviation $$\hat\theta_r-\hat\theta$$ carries only a quarter of the sampling variance (the formula multiplies the sum of 80 squared deviations by $$4/80$$), so the widget stretches every deviation by $$\sqrt{0.05 \times 80} = 2$$ before ranking.

Arizona stays in the top ten on <Fact id="ch3lab.r10_top_freq" /> rescaled replicates; Wyoming on only <Fact id="ch3lab.r9_top_freq" />. The two rates are equal to one decimal place, but Wyoming's rests on <Fact id="ch3lab.r9_n" /> respondents and Arizona's on <Fact id="ch3lab.r10_n" />: the sample size, not the rate, decides. Tennessee, eleventh, makes the top ten on <Fact id="ch3lab.r11_top_freq" />. Across all replicates, <Fact id="ch3lab.rep_top_all" /> states are in the top ten every time and <Fact id="ch3lab.rep_top_any" /> at least once: the same states the pairwise tests identify.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Three cautions on replicate counts">

Replicates approximate the spread of estimates across repeated samples; they are not posterior draws, and a top-ten frequency is not the probability that a state belongs in the top ten. Stretching each deviation by two matches the variance of each state's rate; it does not make the re-ranked replicates a validated sampling distribution for a rank, which is not a smooth function of the rates. And 80 replicates make a coarse distribution, whose frequencies move in steps of one-eightieth. Read the counts as a sensitivity summary under structured perturbations, not as probabilities of membership.

</Callout>

## Doing it yourself

With the replicate weights joined on SAMPLE, SERIAL, and PERNUM, the hand computation takes three lines:

```python
# one state's adults 19-64: y = uninsured (0/1), w = PERWT, W = REPWTP1-80 (n x 80)
theta = (w * y).sum() / w.sum()
theta_r = (W * y[:, None]).sum(axis=0) / W.sum(axis=0)
se = np.sqrt(4 / 80 * ((theta_r - theta) ** 2).sum())  # MOE = 1.645 * se
```

In R, the `survey` package supplies the SDR constant <Cite id="lumley2010" />, and `svyby` with `covmat = TRUE` keeps the replicate covariance for contrasts:

```r
des <- svrepdesign(data = d, weights = ~PERWT, repweights = "REPWTP[0-9]+",
                   type = "successive-difference", mse = TRUE)
est <- svyby(~uninsured, ~STATEFIP, des, svymean, covmat = TRUE)
svycontrast(est, c("48" = 1, "13" = -1))  # Texas minus Georgia
```

Setting `mse = TRUE` centers the squared deviations on the full-sample estimate, as the Census formula does. The second table is `subset(des, AGE >= 55 & AGE <= 64)` followed by the same two lines; subsetting a replicate design keeps all 80 weight columns, so the domain's variance is right without further thought.

<Callout kind="warning" title="svy ignores declared replicate weights unless told otherwise">

With replicate weights declared, the default `mean()` call in `svy 0.28` silently ignores them. For Texas it returns a standard error of <Fact id="ch3lab.svy_default_se" /> percentage points on <Fact id="ch3lab.svy_default_df" /> degrees of freedom, a linearization that treats every person as an independent draw. Passing `method="replication"` and `variance_center="estimate"` returns <Fact id="ch3lab.svy_rep_se" /> percentage points on 79 degrees of freedom, identical to the hand computation. Assert the degrees of freedom:

```python
s = svy.Sample(d, design=svy.Design(
    wgt="PERWT", rep_wgts=svy.SdrWgts(prefix="REPWTP", n_reps=80)))
est = s.estimation.mean("uninsured", method="replication",
                        variance_center="estimate")
assert est.to_polars()["df"][0] == 79  # the default call reports n - 1
```

</Callout>

An incorrect shortcut also circulates: clustering standard errors on PUMA, the public-use geography, instead of using the replicates. PUMAs are not units of the ACS sample design, and a cluster-robust standard error computed on them reproduces neither the address-level sampling nor the weighting.

<Callout kind="box" title="Validation">

Three checks stand behind these numbers. R's `survey` package, reading the parquet files and joining the replicate weights on its own, reproduces the estimates, their standard errors, and the standard errors of the differences for eight states and three pairs to within <Fact id="ch3lab.r_max_diff" />, and does the same for the second table's estimates, standard errors, and the Delaware–New Jersey difference. The `svy` replication path agrees as well. The same code also reproduces an external benchmark, the Census Bureau's 2024 PUMS verification file: summing PERWT gives the published U.S. population of <Fact id="ch3lab.pums_us_total" />, <Fact id="ch3lab.pums_est_exact" /> of <Fact id="ch3lab.pums_rows" /> published totals (by sex, age group, and group-quarters type, for the nation and six states) match exactly, and <Fact id="ch3lab.pums_se_ok" /> of their standard errors match to the published integer. Those PUMS standard errors, not the full-sample margins on data.census.gov, are the right target: the public-use file is a subsample of the ACS, so its standard errors are larger.

</Callout>
