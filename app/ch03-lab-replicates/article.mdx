In 2024, <Fact id="ch3lab.us_rate" ci /> of U.S. adults aged 19 to 64 had no health insurance when they answered the American Community Survey (ACS). This is the Medicaid-relevant age group: children have their own Medicaid and CHIP eligibility rules, nearly everyone 65 and older has Medicare, and the adults in between are the ones whose coverage depends most on each state's Medicaid choices. By state, the rate runs from <Fact id="ch3lab.bottom_rate" /> in Massachusetts to <Fact id="ch3lab.top_rate" /> in Texas. Sorted, the 50 states and DC make a league table of the kind news stories print: Georgia second, Oklahoma third, Arizona tenth.

This lab asks which places in that table the data can support. The data are the ACS 2024 1-year public-use sample from IPUMS <Cite id="ipums_usa" />, restricted to the Census Bureau's universe for health insurance, the civilian noninstitutionalized population (everyone except residents of institutions such as prisons and nursing homes, and members of the Armed Forces). That leaves <Fact id="ch3lab.us_n" /> adults aged 19 to 64, from <Fact id="ch3lab.n_min" /> in Wyoming to <Fact id="ch3lab.n_max" /> in California.

## A league table and a naive test

The quickest way to attach uncertainty to each rate is the textbook standard error for a proportion from a simple random sample, $$\SE_{\text{iid}} = \sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$$, followed by a test for every pair of states. At the 90 percent level, the level the Census Bureau uses for ACS comparisons, that analysis finds <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_sig_naive" /> of the <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_total" /> pairs significantly different, including <Fact id="ch3lab.adj_sig_naive" /> of the 50 pairs that sit next to each other in the ranking.

The analysis looks defensible and is wrong. It treats the ACS as a simple random sample of independent people. The ACS samples addresses and records everyone living at each one, so members of a household, who often share an insurance status, enter the sample together. Its weights also vary widely across respondents, and unequal weights add variance that the iid formula never sees.

## Replicate weights: repeated sampling, pre-packaged

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

The naive standard error for Texas is <Fact id="ch3lab.top_se_iid" /> percentage points. Is the design-based standard error about the same, a little larger, or more than half again as large?

</Callout>

A standard error answers a repeated-sampling question: draw another sample under the same design, weight it the same way, and how far does the estimate move? Replicate weights are the survey world's pre-packaged repeated sampling. With the public-use file come 80 alternative sets of person weights (REPWTP1 to REPWTP80 in IPUMS). Each starts from a systematic perturbation of the sample's base weights and then goes through the full weighting again, with the nonresponse adjustments and the calibration to population controls redone for every replicate. Re-estimating a statistic with each set shows how far it moves.

That is why replicates travel with the data. The final weight is itself an estimate, and only the producer can repeat its own weighting on a perturbed sample. The spread of the replicate estimates therefore includes the variance that weight construction adds, and the variance that calibration removes, which no formula applied to the final weights can recover.

The ACS uses successive difference replication (SDR), developed at the Census Bureau for state and county income and poverty estimates <Cite id="fay1995" />. It is one of several replication procedures, alongside balanced repeated replication (BRR), Fay's variant of BRR, the jackknife, and the bootstrap. They form replicates differently and convert the spread into a variance with different constants; calling them all "the bootstrap" blurs procedures that are not interchangeable. For the ACS <Cite id="census_acs_dm" />:

$$
\widehat{\Var}(\hat\theta) = \frac{4}{80}\sum_{r=1}^{80}\bigl(\hat\theta_r - \hat\theta\bigr)^2, \qquad \text{df} = 79,
$$

where $$\hat\theta$$ is computed with the final weight $$w_i$$ (PERWT) and $$\hat\theta_r$$ with the replicate weight $$w_i^{(r)}$$ (REPWTP$$r$$). The ACS publishes 90 percent margins of error (MOE), $$1.645 \times \SE$$.

For Texas the replicate standard error is <Fact id="ch3lab.top_se_sdr" /> percentage points, against the naive <Fact id="ch3lab.top_se_iid" />. Across states the ratio of the two runs from <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_min" /> in Oklahoma to <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_max" /> in Minnesota, with a median of <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_median" />.<SideNote>The ratio belongs to the estimate, not to the survey, as [Chapter 3](/ch03-design) argues for the design effect. For the national rate it is <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_us" />, larger than for any state. Two states' replicate deviations are only slightly correlated, <Fact id="ch3lab.cross_corr_mean" /> on average, but the national estimate adds up every pair: those covariances supply <Fact id="ch3lab.us_cov_share" /> of its variance and make its standard error <Fact id="ch3lab.us_se_over_indep" /> what independent state estimates would give.</SideNote> A standard error that uses the weights but still treats people as independent closes much of that gap, yet it misses in both directions: depending on the state, the replicate standard error is between <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_wo_min" /> and <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_wo_max" /> the weights-only one. ## Which comparisons survive

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Two states' 90 percent margins of error overlap. Can the difference between them still be statistically significant at the 90 percent level?

</Callout>

It can. A difference between two states is itself an estimate, and it gets the same treatment: compute it on every replicate and take the SDR spread of the replicate differences.

$$
\widehat{\Var}(\hat\theta_a-\hat\theta_b) = \frac{4}{80}\sum_{r=1}^{80}\Bigl[\bigl(\hat\theta_{a,r}-\hat\theta_{b,r}\bigr)-\bigl(\hat\theta_a-\hat\theta_b\bigr)\Bigr]^2
$$

For independent estimates with equal standard errors, the standard error of the difference is $$\sqrt{2}$$ times one of them, not twice, so overlapping intervals are not a test. The replicate formula also carries the covariance between the two estimates without being asked. For two different states the covariance is small: swapping in the independence formula $$\sqrt{\SE_a^2 + \SE_b^2}$$ changes the verdict for <Fact id="ch3lab.indep_flips" /> of the <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_total" /> pairs. It matters when one estimate contains the other. California minus the nation has a replicate standard error of <Fact id="ch3lab.state_us_se_rep" /> percentage points; treating the two as independent gives <Fact id="ch3lab.state_us_se_ind" />.

With the replicate standard error of each difference, <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_sig_sdr" /> pairs are distinguishable, against <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_sig_naive" /> in the naive analysis. The totals hide where the change is. Most pairs sit far apart in the ranking and differ under any method, while a league table's claims are about neighbors. Of the 50 adjacent pairs, <Fact id="ch3lab.adj_sig_sdr" /> survive, not <Fact id="ch3lab.adj_sig_naive" /> (Figure 3L.2). One place apart, <Fact id="ch3lab.gap1_sdr" /> of pairs are distinguishable (<Fact id="ch3lab.gap1_naive" /> under the naive test), and every pair <Fact id="ch3lab.gap_all_sdr" /> or more places apart is distinguishable (under the naive test, every pair <Fact id="ch3lab.gap_all_naive" /> or more apart).

These are per-comparison tests, the ACS convention, and across <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_total" /> comparisons some false positives are expected. Holding the chance of any false positive to 10 percent with a Bonferroni correction leaves <Fact id="ch3lab.pairs_sig_bonf" /> distinguishable pairs.

## Re-rank on every replicate

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Wyoming ranks ninth and Arizona tenth. If you recompute the ranking with each set of replicate weights, which of the two stays in the top ten more often?

</Callout>

<ReplicateRanking id="ch3lab.state_replicates" />

Each replicate yields all 51 state rates, so it yields a complete ranking. The widget's top-k view re-ranks the states on every replicate and counts how often each lands in the top ten; its uncertainty view marks which states differ from any state you pick. One adjustment comes first. A single SDR replicate deviation $$\hat\theta_r-\hat\theta$$ carries only a quarter of the sampling variance (the formula multiplies the sum of 80 squared deviations by $$4/80$$), so the widget stretches every deviation by $$\sqrt{0.05 \times 80} = 2$$ before ranking.

Arizona stays in the top ten on <Fact id="ch3lab.r10_top_freq" /> rescaled replicates; Wyoming on only <Fact id="ch3lab.r9_top_freq" />. The two rates are equal to one decimal place, but Wyoming's rests on <Fact id="ch3lab.r9_n" /> respondents and Arizona's on <Fact id="ch3lab.r10_n" />. Tennessee, eleventh, makes the top ten on <Fact id="ch3lab.r11_top_freq" />. Across all replicates, <Fact id="ch3lab.rep_top_all" /> states are in the top ten every time and <Fact id="ch3lab.rep_top_any" /> at least once: the same states the pairwise tests identify.

Two cautions. Replicates approximate the spread of estimates across repeated samples; they are not posterior draws, and a top-ten frequency is not the probability that a state belongs in the top ten. And 80 replicates make a coarse distribution, whose frequencies move in steps of one-eightieth.

## Doing it yourself

With the replicate weights joined on SAMPLE, SERIAL, and PERNUM, the hand computation takes three lines:

```python
# one state's adults 19-64: y = uninsured (0/1), w = PERWT, W = REPWTP1-80 (n x 80)
theta = (w * y).sum() / w.sum()
theta_r = (W * y[:, None]).sum(axis=0) / W.sum(axis=0)
se = np.sqrt(4 / 80 * ((theta_r - theta) ** 2).sum())  # MOE = 1.645 * se
```

In R, the `survey` package supplies the SDR constant <Cite id="lumley2010" />, and `svyby` with `covmat = TRUE` keeps the replicate covariance for contrasts:

```r
des <- svrepdesign(data = d, weights = ~PERWT, repweights = "REPWTP[0-9]+",
                   type = "successive-difference", mse = TRUE)
est <- svyby(~uninsured, ~STATEFIP, des, svymean, covmat = TRUE)
svycontrast(est, c("48" = 1, "13" = -1))  # Texas minus Georgia
```

Setting `mse = TRUE` centers the squared deviations on the full-sample estimate, as the Census formula does.

<Callout kind="warning" title="svy ignores declared replicate weights unless told otherwise">

With replicate weights declared, the default `mean()` call in `svy 0.28` silently ignores them. For Texas it returns a standard error of <Fact id="ch3lab.svy_default_se" /> percentage points on <Fact id="ch3lab.svy_default_df" /> degrees of freedom, a linearization that treats every person as an independent draw. Passing `method="replication"` and `variance_center="estimate"` returns <Fact id="ch3lab.svy_rep_se" /> percentage points on 79 degrees of freedom, identical to the hand computation. Assert the degrees of freedom:

```python
s = svy.Sample(d, design=svy.Design(
    wgt="PERWT", rep_wgts=svy.SdrWgts(prefix="REPWTP", n_reps=80)))
est = s.estimation.mean("uninsured", method="replication",
                        variance_center="estimate")
assert est.to_polars()["df"][0] == 79  # the default call reports n - 1
```

</Callout>

An incorrect shortcut also circulates: clustering standard errors on PUMA, the public-use geography, instead of using the replicates. PUMAs are not units of the ACS sample design, and a cluster-robust standard error computed on them reproduces neither the address-level sampling nor the weighting.

<Callout kind="box" title="Validation">

Three checks stand behind these numbers. R's `survey` package, reading the parquet files and joining the replicate weights on its own, reproduces the estimates, their standard errors, and the standard errors of the differences for eight states and three pairs to within <Fact id="ch3lab.r_max_diff" />. The `svy` replication path agrees as well. The same code also reproduces an external benchmark, the Census Bureau's 2024 PUMS verification file: summing PERWT gives the published U.S. population of <Fact id="ch3lab.pums_us_total" />, <Fact id="ch3lab.pums_est_exact" /> of <Fact id="ch3lab.pums_rows" /> published totals (by sex, age group, and group-quarters type, for the nation and six states) match exactly, and <Fact id="ch3lab.pums_se_ok" /> of their standard errors match to the published integer. Those PUMS standard errors, not the full-sample margins on data.census.gov, are the right target: the public-use file is a subsample of the ACS, so its standard errors are larger.
