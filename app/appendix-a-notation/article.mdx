One notation runs through the booklet, and it is stricter than software. Statistical packages call almost any column of positive numbers a weight; this book gives each kind of weight its own symbol and never reuses one for another.

## The notation contract

| Symbol | Name | What it is | Where it does its work |
|---|---|---|---|
| $$\pi_i$$ | inclusion probability | the probability that unit $$i$$ is selected under the design | Chapters 0 and 2 |
| $$d_i = 1/\pi_i$$ | base or design weight | undoes unequal selection probabilities | Chapter 2 |
| $$g_i$$ | adjustment factor | nonresponse and calibration adjustment: raking, poststratification, trimming | Chapter 2 |
| $$w_i = d_i g_i$$ | final survey weight | the weight shipped on the file | Chapters 2 and 3 |
| $$a_i$$ | precision weight | inversely proportional to a row's variance, as for rows that are group means | Chapters 2 and 4 |
| $$f_i$$ | frequency weight | the number of identical observations a row stands for | Chapter 2 |
| $$w_i^{(r)}$$ | replicate weight | the weight of unit $$i$$ in replicate $$r = 1, \dots, R$$ | Lab 3 |
| $$s_a$$ | standardization weight | the share of age group $$a$$ in a fixed standard population | Chapter 3 |
| $$\omega_i$$ | implicit weight | the weight a regression places on unit $$i$$'s conditional effect | Chapters 4 and 5 |

The symbol $$w$$ is never overloaded merely because software calls everything a weight. A frequency weight changes the number of observations; a precision weight changes the variance attributed to each observation; a survey weight changes the population a sum represents. The three can give the same point estimate and three different standard errors.

## Estimands and populations

Three objects are kept apart throughout. The **estimand** is the population quantity an analysis targets, defined without reference to any data. The **estimator** is the rule that maps a sample to a number. The **estimate** is the number the rule returns on the sample in hand. A weighted and an unweighted mean of the same file are two estimators; whether they target the same estimand depends on the design.

Five populations are named because each gap between them is a place where an analysis can drift:

- The **target population** is the one the result is meant to describe.
- The **frame population** is the list, area sample, or telephone-number bank the survey draws from.
- The **sampled population** is the part of the frame with a positive probability of selection.
- The **respondent population** is those in the sampled population who would respond if selected.
- The **analytic population** is who remains after the analyst's restrictions and missing-data rules.

The **sample**, the units actually selected, and the **respondents**, the selected units that answered, are realizations of the sampled and respondent populations, not populations of their own. [Chapter 1](/ch01-estimands#five-populations) follows all five populations through one estimate.

Coverage honesty follows from the list: when a release omits a state, the target population is the participating jurisdictions, and the result is labeled that way ([Chapter 2](/ch02-weights), [Chapter 8](/ch08-field-guide)).

## What software calls a weight

| Software syntax | Symbol in this book | What the software assumes |
|---|---|---|
| Stata `[pweight=]` | $$w_i$$ | sampling weights; implies a robust (linearized) variance |
| Stata `[aweight=]` | $$a_i$$ | analytic weights inversely proportional to a row's variance; rescaled to sum to the number of rows |
| Stata `[fweight=]` | $$f_i$$ | integer frequency weights; the sample size becomes their sum |
| Stata `[iweight=]` | none | importance weights with no fixed statistical meaning; IPUMS uses them for CPS replicate designs because some replicate weights are negative |
| R `survey::svydesign(weights =)` | $$w_i$$ | sampling weights inside a declared design |
| R `lm(weights =)` | $$a_i$$ | precision weights: weighted least squares with error variance proportional to one over the weight |
| Python `svy.Design(wgt =)` | $$w_i$$ | sampling weights inside a declared design |
| Python `pyfixest.feols(weights =)` | $$a_i$$ by default | analytic weights (`weights_type="aweights"`), or frequency weights with `"fweights"`; paired with a robust or cluster-robust variance, the coefficients equal the survey-weighted ones |
| Python `statsmodels` `WLS(weights =)` | $$a_i$$ | precision weights |

The table's lesson is narrow. A weighted regression coefficient is the same number whichever of $$w_i$$, $$a_i$$, or $$f_i$$ the software thinks it was given; the standard error is not. Which variance is right depends on what the weight means and on how the data were drawn, which is the subject of [Chapter 3](/ch03-design) and [Chapter 6](/ch06-clustering).

## The master crosswalk

Each row names one object in three dialects. The last column states the condition under which the translation holds; where a condition is missing, the translation is an analogy, not an identity.

| Survey statistics | Econometrics | Causal inference | Condition or caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| finite-population quantity (total, mean, ratio, census regression coefficient) | population parameter; probability limit of an estimator | estimand (PATE, ATT, CATE) | name the population and the unit before the estimator |
| inclusion probability $$\pi_i$$ | sampling rate; selection probability | propensity score $$e(x)$$ | the causal analogy holds for inverse weighting, not for design-based variance |
| design weight $$d_i = 1/\pi_i$$ | probability weight (`pweight`) | inverse-propensity weight $$1/e(x)$$ | $$\pi_i$$ is known by design; $$e(x)$$ is estimated from a model |
| nonresponse and calibration adjustment $$g_i$$ (raking, poststratification) | reweighting to known margins; inverse probability weighting for missing data <Cite id="wooldridge2007" /> | balancing weights | calibrated weights are no longer inverse probabilities; their validity rests on a response or outcome model |
| Horvitz–Thompson estimator of a total | inverse-probability-weighted sum | inverse-probability-weighted (Horvitz–Thompson) estimator of mean potential outcomes | design-unbiased for a finite-population total when every $$\pi_i \gt 0$$ and is known <Cite id="horvitz1952" /> |
| Hájek (ratio) estimator of a mean | weighted mean; WLS on a constant | normalized inverse-probability weighting | approximately unbiased and consistent, not exactly unbiased |
| stratum | none standard (sampling stratification) | block in a blocked design | strata reduce variance when they predict the outcome |
| primary sampling unit (PSU) | cluster | cluster of jointly assigned units | clustering is a property of the sampling or assignment design, not of correlated errors alone <Cite id="abadie2023" /> |
| intra-PSU correlation | intraclass correlation; the Moulton problem | intraclass correlation of outcomes | the Moulton problem also needs a regressor that is correlated within clusters |
| Taylor linearization over PSU totals within strata, with replacement | cluster-robust sandwich (CRV1) | design-based variance for cluster-randomized designs | identical for a weighted mean with one stratum; with informative strata CRV1 omits the stratification gain ([Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations#taylor-linearization-for-a-ratio-mean-and-when-it-coincides-with-crv1)) |
| design effect $$\DEFF(\hat\theta)$$ | ratio of the correct variance to an iid variance | none standard | specific to an estimator and an outcome |
| clustering component of a design effect for a mean, $$1 + (\bar m - 1)\rho_y$$ | Moulton factor for a regression coefficient, $$1 + (\bar m - 1)\rho_x\rho_u$$ with equal cluster sizes | none standard | Moulton is a special-case regression analogue of the clustering component of a design effect, not the design effect itself <Cite id="moulton1990" /> |
| design degrees of freedom: PSUs minus strata (Taylor); replicates minus one (ACS and CPS replicates) | $$G - 1$$ in few-cluster inference | number of randomization units | a common rule, not a law; NCHS notes it is not directly applicable to the NHIS design |
| replicate weights (BRR, Fay, jackknife, successive difference, bootstrap) | resampling variance estimators | randomization inference is a different repetition | replicate weights are pre-packaged repeated sampling; the five methods are distinct procedures, and none is "a bootstrap" in general <Cite id="fay1995" /> |
| declare the design before analyzing | cluster at the level of sampling or assignment | analyze as randomized | identify every mechanism that induces common uncertainty; the "coarser level" rule holds for nested hierarchies <Cite id="abadie2023,cameron2015" /> |
| domain (subpopulation) estimation on the full design | subgroup estimate with full-sample clustering; interaction with a group indicator | conditional average effect for a subgroup | deleting rows gives the same point estimate and can change the variance when strata lose PSUs |
| frequency weight $$f_i$$ | `fweight` | none standard | changes the number of observations |
| precision weight $$a_i$$ | `aweight`; WLS for heteroskedasticity or group means | inverse-variance weights in meta-analysis | the precision motive for weighting <Cite id="solon2015,dickens1990" /> |
| implicit weights $$\omega_i$$ | variance weighting of OLS under heterogeneity <Cite id="angrist1998" /> | implied regression weights <Cite id="aronow2016,chattopadhyay2023" /> | nonnegative with saturated controls; can be negative with linear controls |
| standardization weights $$s_a$$ (direct age standardization) | reweighting to a reference distribution | standardization to a target covariate distribution | redefines the comparison population rather than repairing representation |
| design-based inference: $$y$$ fixed, the sample random | sampling-based inference: draws from a population | design-based inference: assignment random | which repetition is intended decides which variance is right <Cite id="abadie2020" /> |
| finite population correction | sampled-cluster fraction | finite-sample (Neyman) variance | when most clusters are observed, conventional cluster-robust SEs can be conservative |
| Kish effective sample size $$\nKish$$ | effective sample size of a weighted estimator | effective sample size of balancing weights | prices weight dispersion only, under stated conditions <Cite id="kish1965" /> |

## A note on the seed crosswalk

The booklet began from an article that compared survey statistics and econometrics in a seven-row table. Readers do not need that article to use this appendix. Its table was right about the direction of the analogy and wrong in several of its equivalences, and the corrections are binding everywhere in the booklet.

| The seed said | The crosswalk says | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Weights get the point estimate right; the design gets the uncertainty right | The weights help define the estimator; the sampling design and the weight construction determine repeated-sampling variance | final weights are estimated, and replication that repeats the weighting captures that |
| The weighted estimator is design-unbiased, however convoluted the sampling | Correct inclusion weights give design-unbiased or consistent estimators for specified finite-population quantities | the Horvitz–Thompson total is unbiased; the Hájek mean is consistent; calibrated weights are not inverse probabilities |
| Design effect = Moulton factor $$1 + (\bar m - 1)\rho$$ | Moulton is a special-case regression analogue of the clustering component of a design effect | a design effect also reflects strata and weighting, and the Moulton factor involves the regressor's clustering |
| Replicate weights are the survey world's pre-packaged cluster bootstrap | Replicate weights are pre-packaged repeated sampling; BRR, Fay, jackknife, SDR, and bootstrap are distinct | the ACS and CPS use successive difference replication, not a bootstrap |
