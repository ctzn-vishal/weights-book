A survey declaration can be wrong even when the estimate looks plausible. Two declarations show how.

The CPS ASEC 2025 declaration, as IPUMS documents it, names the person weight `ASECWT` and its <Fact id="ch7.asec_n_reps" /> replicate weights. Ask `svy` (version `0.28`, the version tested here) for the share of people uninsured for all of calendar 2024, once with the default estimation call and once with `method="replication"` and `variance_center="estimate"`. Both calls return <Fact id="ch7.asec_uninsured" />. The default call reports <Fact id="ch7.asec_df_default" /> degrees of freedom; the replication call reports <Fact id="ch7.asec_df" />. The first number gives the default call away: it ignored the declared replicate weights and linearized as if every person were an independent draw, so its degrees of freedom are the sample size minus one. Declaring a design and using it are different steps. The standard error moves from <Fact id="ch7.asec_se_default" /> to <Fact id="ch7.asec_se_rep" /> percentage points, <Fact id="ch7.asec_se_ratio" /> the default value, and the replicate value matches the Census Bureau's formula computed by hand. The interval is <Fact id="ch7.asec_uninsured" ci />. Nothing warned.

Declare BRFSS 2024 as CDC documents it: the final weight, the strata `_STSTR`, and the PSU `_PSU`. Ask for any national prevalence and R's `survey` package stops, and `svy` raises a `SingletonError`: <Fact id="ch7.brfss_singletons" /> strata in the full national file hold a single respondent, and a stratum with one PSU has no within-stratum variation to measure. Code that silently skips such strata, as many hand-rolled formulas do, treats them as contributing no variance at all. The declaration is incomplete until it includes a rule for single-PSU strata.

The six guides below each declare one current release: what it is and whom it covers, which weight, which variance method with which constant and degrees of freedom, what to do about lonely PSUs and pooled years, where the series break, and the traps that catch careful analysts. The chapter's build script generates them from the data pipeline's design manifests, the producers' documentation, and counts taken from the data, and every item says where it came from. Jump to a survey: [ACS](#acs-2024-1-year), [CPS ASEC](#cps-asec-2025), [NHIS](#nhis-2024), [ATUS](#atus-2024), [NHANES](#nhanes-august-2021august-2023), [BRFSS](#brfss-2024).

## How to read a guide

Each guide is a table with three columns: the item, the declaration, and its provenance. Provenance takes one of six forms.

- **design.json**: a machine-readable field of the dataset's design manifest in the data pipeline.
- **design.json notes**: the manifest's free-text notes. Only three of the pipeline's datasets carry the full machine-readable block (variance tier, lonely-PSU rule, pooling rule, series breaks, licensed geography); for the rest, those facts live in notes and are marked as such.
- **producer**: documentation from the Census Bureau, BLS, NCHS, CDC, or IPUMS, read on the date in the "Last verified" row.
- **computed from data**: counted by the build script from the book's data store.
- **verified in this build**: a behavior the build script confirmed by running code.
- **book guidance**: an editorial recommendation where neither manifest nor producer states a rule.

Each guide is followed by its declaration in three dialects. The Python (`svy`) and R (`survey`) declarations for NHIS, NHANES, BRFSS, ATUS, and CPS ASEC were executed against the book's store by the build script and give the same estimates and standard errors in both languages. The ACS declarations were not run on the full replicate file, which is three gigabytes; their calls were checked on a synthetic frame with the same column names. The Stata declarations follow the producers' published examples and were not executed, because Stata is not installed on the build machine. Paths point at the book's store; adapt them to your own extract.

A variance tier is a property of the extract, not a choice. Replicate designs (ACS, CPS ASEC) and Taylor designs (NHIS, NHANES, BRFSS) support design-based uncertainty. The book's ATUS extract carries weights only, so a standard error computed from it ignores the design and is not a design-based standard error; nothing in the extract says whether it errs high or low. ATUS can carry an estimand lesson in this book, but not an uncertainty lesson.

The code mixes three kinds of name, and a copy has to keep them apart. In the ACS, CPS ASEC, NHIS, and ATUS snippets, UPPERCASE names are IPUMS variables. In the NHANES snippets they are NCHS names, except `CYCLE_YEAR`, which the book's store adds. The BRFSS snippets use the store's names: `xststr`, `xpsu`, and `xllcpwt` are CDC's `_STSTR`, `_PSU`, and `_LLCPWT`, and `iyear` and `Health` are harmonized store variables; `Health` has two levels, and `"Poor"` means fair or poor. Lowercase outcomes such as `uninsured`, `uninsured_ly`, `current_smoker`, and `htn_measured` are derived in the store, and `fairpoor` is built in the snippet itself; an extract of your own has to define them. Within each survey, the three languages use the same target, the same rule for missing outcomes, and the same variance method.

## ACS 2024 1-year

The American Community Survey's 2024 1-year sample holds <Fact id="ch7.acs_n_persons" /> person records in the IPUMS extract. Its public design lives entirely in replicate weights: <Fact id="ch7.acs_n_reps" /> person weights and as many household weights, built by successive difference replication. The variance is the constant <Fact id="ch7.acs_scale" /> times the sum of squared deviations of the replicate estimates from the full-sample estimate, with <Fact id="ch7.acs_df" /> degrees of freedom. There are no strata or PSUs to declare alongside them: IPUMS says to treat the sample as a single stratum. Person estimates use `PERWT`; household estimates use `HHWT` on the first person of each household. Figure 7.2 lists each item of the declaration with its provenance.

<Figure n="7.2" caption="The ACS declaration is carried by replicate weights alone: no strata or PSUs, a fixed variance constant, and degrees of freedom set by the number of replicates.">
  <Chart id="ch7.guide_acs" />
</Figure>

```python
# Paths point at the book's store; adapt them to your own extract.
import polars as pl
import svy

keys = ["SAMPLE", "SERIAL", "PERNUM"]

    pl.scan_parquet("ipums/analysis/usa/acs/part_2020_2024.parquet")
    .filter(acs2024)
    .select(keys + ["PERWT", "uninsured"])

r = (
    pl.scan_parquet("ipums/analysis/usa/acs_repwt/part_2020_2024.parquet")
    .filter(acs2024)
    .select(keys + reps)
)
d = p.join(r, on=keys, how="inner").collect()

rep = svy.SdrWgts(prefix="REPWTP", n_reps=80)

Two traps deserve more than a table row. ## CPS ASEC 2025

The Annual Social and Economic Supplement is fielded in the first months of the year and asks about the year before: the 2025 file describes income, poverty, and coverage in calendar 2024. The `svy` call has a second option that matters. With `method="replication"` alone, `svy` centers the replicate deviations on the mean of the replicates rather than on the full-sample estimate. ## NHIS 2024

The National Health Interview Survey is a Taylor-linearization design (Figure 7.4). The 2024 public file holds <Fact id="ch7.nhis_n_adults" /> sample adults and <Fact id="ch7.nhis_n_children" /> sample children, each with a weight that sums to the adult or child population. The design has <Fact id="ch7.nhis_strata" /> strata and <Fact id="ch7.nhis_psus" /> PSUs, so the common rule of PSUs minus strata gives <Fact id="ch7.nhis_df" /> degrees of freedom. NCHS cautions that the rule is not directly applicable to the NHIS design and that it depends on the variable, and says a normal approximation may be adequate when a variable is spread across most clusters.

The trap is in the PSU codes. There are only <Fact id="ch7.nhis_psu_codes" /> distinct codes, reused across strata, so a PSU is identified by the pair of stratum and PSU. `svy` nests PSU codes within the declared strata on its own, and R's `svydesign` stops with an error unless told `nest = TRUE`; both behaviors were confirmed by the build. Code that groups or clusters on the PSU code alone gets no warning. For adult current cigarette smoking, <Fact id="ch7.nhis_smoking" ci />, the declared design gives a standard error of <Fact id="ch7.nhis_se_taylor" /> percentage points. A cluster-robust shortcut on the stratum-PSU key gives <Fact id="ch7.nhis_se_crv1" />, <Fact id="ch7.nhis_crv1_ratio" /> the design-based standard error, because it ignores the strata and so omits the stratification gain ([Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations)). Clustering on the PSU code alone, which merges PSUs from different strata, gives <Fact id="ch7.nhis_se_label" />, <Fact id="ch7.nhis_label_ratio" /> the design-based figure, and nothing about it looks wrong.

<Figure n="7.4" caption="NHIS 2024 is a Taylor design whose PSU codes repeat across strata: every declaration must nest PSUs within strata, and pooled years keep the same PSUs.">
  <Chart id="ch7.guide_nhis" />
</Figure>

```python
# Paths point at the book's store; adapt them to your own extract.
import polars as pl
import svy

d = pl.read_parquet(
    "ipums/analysis/nhis/nhis_core/part_2015_2024.parquet",
    columns=["YEAR", "STRATA", "PSU", "SAMPWEIGHT", "ASTATFLG", "current_smoker"],
).filter(pl.col("YEAR") == 2024)  # one annual file is one design

# svy nests PSU codes within strata; hand-rolled code must key on (STRATA, PSU).

# Sample adults are a domain: declare on the whole file, then estimate with where=.
est = s.estimation.mean("current_smoker", where=svy.col("ASTATFLG") == 1, drop_nulls=True)
print(est.to_dicts()[0])  # df = PSUs - strata
```

The cast in the Python declaration is another verified trap: `svy 0.28` raises an error when a `where=` condition refers to an 8- or 16-bit integer column, as the IPUMS status flags are stored. The outcome itself may stay narrow. The estimate is a domain estimate, declared on the whole file and restricted with `where=`; every PSU keeps adults, so here it equals the estimate on the adult rows, and the build confirms that it does.

Two more rules complete the declaration. Every stratum in the full 2024 file has at least <Fact id="ch7.nhis_min_psus" /> PSUs, so single-PSU strata arise only in small domains, where the manifest's rule (centering, equivalent to R's `adjust`) applies and PSUs retained should be reported beside the standard error. And <Fact id="ch7.nhis_shared_psus" /> of the 2024 PSUs also appear in 2023, because the 2016–2025 design keeps its areas for the decade. NCHS therefore pools years by dividing the weight by the number of years and keeping the stratum and PSU identifiers as they are. Treating each year's PSUs as separate clusters would treat recurring areas as independent.

## ATUS 2024

The American Time Use Survey interviews one person per household, drawn from households that have finished their final CPS interview, about a single designated day. The 2024 file has <Fact id="ch7.atus_n" /> respondents (Figure 7.5). It sums to person-days, not persons: an average over the weighted file is an average over days.

The book's extract carries no strata, PSUs, or replicate weights, so any standard error computed from it treats diary days as a weighted sample of independent draws. ## NHANES August 2021–August 2023

NHANES interviews people at home and examines them in a mobile center, and the two stages have different weights. In the August 2021–August 2023 cycle <Fact id="ch7.nhanes_n_int" /> people were interviewed and <Fact id="ch7.nhanes_n_exam" /> of them examined. Examination variables, and laboratory variables measured on every examinee, use the exam weight `WTMEC2YR`, alone or combined with interview variables; interview-only analyses use `WTINT2YR`. Analytes measured on a subsample carry their own weights, such as the fasting subsample's `WTSAF2YR`, and this release adds a phlebotomy weight. NCHS's rule is to use the weight of the smallest subsample whose variables enter the analysis (Figure 7.6 lists the alternates). The public design is masked: <Fact id="ch7.nhanes_strata" /> pseudo-strata of two pseudo-PSUs each, <Fact id="ch7.nhanes_psus" /> in all, which leaves <Fact id="ch7.nhanes_df" /> design degrees of freedom however many people are examined (Figure 7.6).

This cycle is not a continuation of earlier ones. It dropped the oversampling by race, Hispanic origin, and income, selected everyone aged 0 to 19 or 60 and older in a sampled household, used a self-administered screener, and had much lower response rates; NCHS's assessment found no major bias that weighting did not address. Blood pressure has been measured with an oscillometric device since 2017, which reads lower than the earlier auscultatory method. And two earlier files overlap: the 2017–2018 cycle and the 2017–March 2020 pre-pandemic file cover the same field period with different participant numbers, so they must never be analyzed together.

</Figure>

## BRFSS 2024

BRFSS is a telephone survey run by the states, designed for state-level estimates. The 2024 public file has <Fact id="ch7.brfss_n" /> respondents in <Fact id="ch7.brfss_jurisdictions" /> jurisdictions: 49 states, the District of Columbia, Guam, Puerto Rico, and the US Virgin Islands. Tennessee could not collect enough data to meet the minimum requirements, so a national estimate from this file describes adults in participating jurisdictions and should be labeled that way. Coverage changes from year to year: the 2023 file omits Kentucky and Pennsylvania, and the 2021 file omits Florida.

The weight is built in stages. A design weight combines the telephone stratum's weight, the number of adults in the household, and the number of landline phones; respondents reachable on both landline and cell phone get a compositing adjustment; the combined design weights are truncated within regions; and the result is raked to state population margins, with age by sex last. The book's store keeps only the final weight; [Chapter 2](/ch02-weights) rebuilds the chain from the raw file. The variance design has <Fact id="ch7.brfss_strata" /> strata and <Fact id="ch7.brfss_psus" /> PSUs, one per respondent, which gives <Fact id="ch7.brfss_df" /> design degrees of freedom. BRFSS uncertainty comes from weighting and stratification, not from clustering: the opposite of NHANES (Figure 7.7).

<Figure n="7.7" caption="BRFSS 2024 describes adults in participating jurisdictions, not the whole country; every respondent is a PSU, and single-respondent strata make a lonely-PSU rule part of the declaration.">
  <Chart id="ch7.guide_brfss" />
</Figure>

```python
# Paths point at the book's store; adapt them to your own extract.

## Three worksheets

The worksheets turn the book's argument into checklists. Each item is a decision to make and write down, not a box to tick for its own sake; the Analysis Ledger is where the answers go.

### Describing a population

- [ ] Name the target population, and check the release's coverage against it: missing states, group quarters, the institutionalized, the months collected.
- [ ] State the unit and the denominator: persons, households, person-days, or examined adults.
- [ ] Choose the weight that matches the unit and the source of every variable used: person or household, interview or exam, core or module version.
- [ ] Declare the whole design first: strata and PSUs, or the replicate weights with their method, constant, and centering.
- [ ] Estimate subgroups as domains of the full design, never by deleting rows; report PSUs retained and any lonely strata beside the standard error.
- [ ] Decide the lonely-PSU rule deliberately and write it down.
- [ ] Report the estimate, its standard error, the design degrees of freedom, the unweighted count, and the coefficient of variation; suppress cells too small to support an estimate.
- [ ] If rates are compared across populations with different age structures, say whether they are crude or standardized, and to which standard.
- [ ] Before comparing years, check for series breaks and pooling rules.
- [ ] Compare with a published figure where one exists, and label it an external benchmark.

### Estimating an effect

- [ ] Write the estimand before the regression: a population average effect, the effect on the treated, a population projection coefficient, or a descriptive adjusted contrast.
- [ ] Say whether a causal reading is claimed, and if so, under what assumption; otherwise call the contrast descriptive.
- [ ] Ask whether selection into the sample depends on the outcome (endogenous sampling). If it does, weights are needed for consistency. If it depends only on variables included as regressors, an unweighted fit of a correctly specified model is consistent, and weighting changes the estimand under heterogeneity rather than rescuing consistency.
- [ ] Run the regression weighted and unweighted, and treat a difference as a diagnostic of heterogeneity, misspecification, or endogenous sampling, not a verdict.
- [ ] Compute the implicit weights: which cells or groups the coefficient actually averages over, and how those compare with their population shares.
- [ ] Check overlap: list the cells where almost everyone or almost no one is treated.
- [ ] If the target is a population average, model the heterogeneity and average the effects over the population distribution.
- [ ] Choose the standard errors with the next worksheet.
- [ ] Write down what the analysis does not establish.

### Choosing standard errors

- [ ] Name the repetition the standard error describes: resampling units, reassigning treatment, nonresponse, or a model.
- [ ] List every mechanism that induces common uncertainty: survey PSUs within strata, assignment clusters such as states or schools, repeated observations of the same unit.
- [ ] If the mechanisms nest, cluster at the coarsest relevant level; if they do not, consider multiway clustering.
- [ ] Ask whether the sample holds most of the clusters in the population; if it does, conventional cluster-robust standard errors can be conservative.
- [ ] Count the independent units. With few clusters, use the wild cluster bootstrap or a small-sample-corrected variance and t critical values with the right degrees of freedom.
- [ ] For survey data, prefer the declared design (Taylor linearization or the producer's replicate weights) to a cluster-robust shortcut, which omits the stratification gain and, if keyed on PSU codes alone, can merge unrelated PSUs.
- [ ] With replicate weights, use the producer's method, constant, and centering, and assert the degrees of freedom the software reports.
- [ ] With weights only, do not present an interval as design-based: it ignores the design, and its error can run in either direction. Keep the analysis to point estimates and estimand lessons, or obtain the design information.
- [ ] Report the choice, the assumption behind it, and one alternative as a sensitivity check.

## From checklist to methods paragraph

Checklists help a reader recognize the pieces; a methods section has to state them. Here is a paragraph of a familiar kind, invented for illustration:

> *An example, not quoted from any paper.* We analyzed NHANES data with the survey weights to obtain nationally representative estimates, and standard errors accounted for the complex survey design. Hypertension control was compared across periods with t-tests.

Every sentence leaves a decision unstated: which population, which definition, which weight, which design variables and degrees of freedom, which denominator, and how many comparisons. The same analysis, written for this book's Chapter 3 result:

> Hypertension was defined as a mean of up to three oscillometric readings at or above 130/80 mm Hg, or current use of prescribed blood-pressure medication, following NCHS Data Brief 511. The target population is U.S. civilian noninstitutionalized adults 18 and older, excluding pregnant women, in August 2021–August 2023. Control is the share of adults with hypertension whose readings were below 130/80; it was estimated as a domain of the full design with the examination weight `WTMEC2YR`. Standard errors use Taylor linearization with the masked strata and PSUs (`SDMVSTRA`, `SDMVPSU`), which leave <Fact id="ch3.nhanes_df" /> design degrees of freedom, and intervals use t with those degrees of freedom. Control was <Fact id="ch3.htn_control" ci />. Changes from the 2017–March 2020 sample were tested as differences between independent samples with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom; <Fact id="ch3.chg_sig_design" /> of the 24 period comparisons reached the 5 percent level, about as many as chance alone would produce. The medication question changed between the periods, and the estimates describe measured blood pressure, not diagnosed hypertension.

Each clause fills a field of the Analysis Ledger, and the referee's checklist below asks for the same fields.

## Rosetta: svy, R, and Stata

The same tasks in three languages. The `svy` column was checked against `svy 0.28` on this machine; the R column uses the `survey` package; the Stata column uses official `svy` syntax and was not executed.

| Task | Python `svy` | R `survey` | Stata |
|---|---|---|---|
| Declare strata and PSUs | `svy.Design(stratum="h", psu="psu", wgt="w")` | `svydesign(ids = ~psu, strata = ~h, weights = ~w, nest = TRUE, data = d)` | `svyset psu [pweight=w], strata(h)` |
| Declare SDR replicate weights | `svy.Design(wgt="w", rep_wgts=svy.SdrWgts(prefix="rw", n_reps=80))` | `svrepdesign(data = d, weights = ~w, repweights = "^rw[0-9]+$", type = "successive-difference", mse = TRUE)` | `svyset [pweight=w], sdrweight(rw1-rw80) vce(sdr) mse` |
| Estimate on a replicate design | `s.estimation.mean("y", method="replication", variance_center="estimate")` | `svymean(~y, des)` | `svy: mean y` |
| Mean; proportion | `s.estimation.mean("y")`; `s.estimation.prop("y")` | `svymean(~y, des)`; `svymean(~factor(y), des)` | `svy: mean y`; `svy: proportion y` |
| Total | `s.estimation.total("y")` | `svytotal(~y, des)` | `svy: total y` |
| Ratio | `s.estimation.ratio("y", "x")` | `svyratio(~y, ~x, des)` | `svy: ratio y/x` |
| Domain estimate | `s.estimation.mean("y", where=svy.col("age") >= 65)` | `svymean(~y, subset(des, age >= 65))` | `svy, subpop(if age>=65): mean y` |

| Regression | `s.glm.fit("y", x=["x1", "x2"])` | `svyglm(y ~ x1 + x2, design = des)` | `svy: regress y x1 x2` |
| Design effect | `s.estimation.mean("y", deff="wr")` | `svymean(~y, des, deff = "replace")` | `estat effects` after `svy: mean y` |
| Lonely-PSU rule | `s = s.singleton.handle("center")` | `options(survey.lonely.psu = "adjust")` | `svyset psu [pweight=w], strata(h) singleunit(centered)` |
| Design degrees of freedom | `est.to_dicts()[0]["df"]` | `degf(des)` | reported by every `svy:` command |
| Cluster-robust shortcut | `pyfixest.feols("y ~ x", data=d, weights="w", vcov={"CRV1": "psu_key"})` | `sandwich::vcovCL(fit, cluster = ~psu_key)` | `regress y x [pweight=w], vce(cluster psu_key)` |

Four behaviors in this table were confirmed by running code. The `svy` estimation call ignores replicate weights unless the replication method is requested. Its `where=` conditions reject 8- and 16-bit integer columns. R's `svydesign` stops when PSU codes repeat across strata unless `nest = TRUE` is set. And R stops on single-PSU strata under its default setting, which is the behavior to keep until the rule is chosen on purpose. The last row is a shortcut, not a declaration: its key must combine stratum and PSU, and even then it ignores the strata. The design-effect row compares with a with-replacement simple random sample in Python and R, the reference this book uses throughout; Stata's `estat effects` uses its own reference, so check its definition before comparing numbers across languages.

## A referee's and an advisor's checklist

Ten questions to ask of any survey-based result before it is submitted, each tied to the Ledger field that should already answer it.

| # | Question | Ledger field |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Whom is the result about, and does the release actually cover them (states, group quarters, months, the institutionalized)? | Target population |
| 2 | What is the estimand, in one sentence, with its unit (persons, households, person-days) and its denominator? | Estimand |
| 3 | What rule produced the number: a weighted mean, a ratio, a standardized rate, a weighted or unweighted regression? | Estimator |
| 4 | Which weight was used, and does it match the unit and the source of every variable (interview or exam, person or household, core or module)? | Explicit weights |
| 5 | Would dropping the weights change the answer, and if so, is that representation, misspecification, or a different estimand? | Explicit weights |
| 6 | With heterogeneous effects, which groups or cells dominate the coefficient, and do they resemble the population? | Implicit weights |
| 7 | Which repetition does the standard error describe: sampling, assignment, nonresponse, or a model? | Source of randomness |
| 8 | Does the variance estimator match the design: strata and PSUs nested correctly, the producer's replicate method and constant, clustering at the level of assignment? What are the degrees of freedom? | Variance estimator |
| 9 | Were subgroups estimated as domains of the full design, how many PSUs and lonely strata remain, and which rule handled them? | Variance estimator |
| 10 | What must hold for the interpretation (coverage, accurate measurement, ignorable nonresponse, a causal assumption), and what does the result not establish? | Assumptions |

## Keeping the guides current

A guide is only as current as its "Last verified" row. The guides are rebuilt, not edited: when a producer revises a release, when the pipeline adds the machine-readable design fields that eleven of its manifests still lack, or when a new release is pinned, the build script regenerates every table and snippet and reruns the checks against the store. The American National Election Studies are the next guide to add. What the guides cannot do is certify an analysis. They fix the declaration, which is necessary for a defensible standard error and not sufficient for a defensible conclusion; the conclusion still depends on the estimand, the weights the estimator chose, and the repetition the analysis claims to describe.
