Two weighted turnout calculations from the same 2020 Current Population Survey file give <Fact id="ch1.turnout_census_2020" /> and <Fact id="ch1.turnout_resp_2020" />. The first counts citizen adults who did not answer the voting question as nonvoters; the second leaves them out. Before inspecting the weight column, inspect the denominator.

The chapter's four examples each yield more than one defensible-looking number, and the numbers disagree for different reasons. Some comparisons change the target: the population a number describes and the unit it counts. Some keep the target and change the estimator. Some change what is assumed about the people who gave no answer, and one changes the definition of the quantity itself. Not every plausible number is a different estimand.

| Comparison | What changes | Kind of change |
|---|---|---|
| Turnout: Census convention or respondents only | Who stays in the denominator | An assumption about the people who did not answer |
| Turnout among respondents, weighted or unweighted | How each respondent counts | The estimator |
| Work time: all days, weekdays, employed people, days with work | Which days are averaged | The target |
| Work time over all days, weighted or unweighted | How each diary counts | The estimator |
| Food insecurity among households, people, or children | The unit counted | The target |
| Poverty: official measure or Supplemental Poverty Measure | What counts as resources and needs | The definition of the quantity |

## What was turnout in 2020?

Every November after a federal election, the Current Population Survey (CPS) asks whether each adult citizen in its sample voted. Take the 2020 file, keep citizens 18 and older, and compute the share who voted. Three careful analysts can get three answers.

The first counts every citizen adult that the survey weight counts and treats anyone who did not answer the voting question as a nonvoter: <Fact id="ch1.turnout_census_2020" />. The second keeps only the people who answered: <Fact id="ch1.turnout_resp_2020" />. The third does the same without weights: <Fact id="ch1.turnout_unw_2020" />. All three run without an error, and all three look like "the share who voted."

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

One of the three is the figure the Census Bureau publishes. Which one, and what separates the other two from it?

</Callout>

The Census Bureau publishes the first. Weighting is not what separates it from the second. Dropping the weights moves the respondents-only answer by <Fact id="ch1.turnout_gap_weighting_2020" /> percentage points, while the first two answers are about <Fact id="ch1.turnout_gap_denominator_2020" /> points apart. The difference is the denominator. In 2020, <Fact id="ch1.turnout_nonresp_2020" /> of weighted citizen adults gave no answer to the voting question. The Census convention keeps them in the denominator and counts them as nonvoters. The respondents-only estimate drops them, and it can be read two ways. Read literally, it is a descriptive rate for a narrower group, citizen adults who answered, which is rarely the population anyone set out to study. Read as turnout among all citizen adults, it assumes that the people who did not answer voted at the same rate as those who did. The Census convention assumes instead that they did not vote. Both are assumptions about people the survey did not hear from. Official vote totals put 2020 turnout at about two-thirds of eligible citizens, much closer to the first answer <Cite id="mcdonald2020" />, though that external benchmark has a denominator problem of its own: someone has to estimate who was eligible.

### Five populations

The turnout disagreement is a disagreement about which population the estimate describes. Survey methodology separates five populations, and keeps each apart from what one survey happens to realize.

| Population | What it is | In the 2020 turnout example |
|---|---|---|
| Target | The population the question is about | U.S. citizens 18 and older, November 2020 |
| Frame | What the sample can be drawn from | The civilian population in housing units and other noninstitutional quarters; people in prisons, nursing homes, and on active military duty are outside it |
| Sampled | The part of the frame with a positive chance of selection | Essentially the whole frame. The households actually selected for November 2020 are the sample, a realization rather than a population |
| Respondent | Those in the sampled population who would respond if selected | People who would complete the basic CPS interview and answer the voting question. Those who did so in November 2020 are the observed respondents |
| Analytic | Who remains after the analyst's restrictions and missing-data rules, with their weights | Census convention: every citizen adult with a positive supplement weight. Respondents only: those who answered |

The frame is already narrower than the target, so the published rate describes citizen adults outside institutions and the military. The weights carry the sample back toward that population one step at a time. The CPS weight adjusts for households that did not complete the basic interview and calibrates the result to population totals. It does not adjust for people who skipped the voting questions: for every citizen adult it counts from 2008 to 2024, the supplement weight equals the basic monthly weight. What the nonrespondents contribute is left to a convention, and the two conventions choose differently. The analytic population is where that choice lives.

### A category change, not a change in voters

Starting with November 2022, "No Response" is no longer a category of the voting variable in the IPUMS files <Cite id="ipums_cps" />. In the 2022 and 2024 files, citizen adults who skipped the supplement carry the code "Not in universe," the label that elsewhere means a question did not apply. They keep a positive supplement weight: <Fact id="ch1.turnout_niu_2022" /> citizen adults in 2022 and <Fact id="ch1.turnout_niu_2024" /> in 2024. The survey still counts them. A recode that trusts the label drops them from the denominator, and turnout becomes <Fact id="ch1.turnout_recode_2022" /> for 2022 and <Fact id="ch1.turnout_recode_2024" /> for 2024, about <Fact id="ch1.turnout_recode_gap_2022" /> and <Fact id="ch1.turnout_recode_gap_2024" /> percentage points above the published rates. Counting everyone the weight counts, with these records as nonvoters, gives <Fact id="ch1.turnout_census_2022" /> and <Fact id="ch1.turnout_census_2024" />, the published rates to the decimal (Figure 1.1).

The voters did not change. The weighted share without an answer was <Fact id="ch1.turnout_nonresp_2022" /> in 2022 and <Fact id="ch1.turnout_nonresp_2024" /> in 2024, inside the range of <Fact id="ch1.turnout_nonresp_low" /> to <Fact id="ch1.turnout_nonresp_high" /> seen from 2008 to 2020. The weight encodes a decision about who counts. The recode overrode that decision without anyone making it.

## Estimand, estimator, estimate

<Callout kind="definition" title="Three objects">

An **estimand** is the population quantity a question asks for, here a mean over a specified population of days. An **estimator** is the rule that turns sample records into a number. An **estimate** is the number that rule produces from this sample.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

The unweighted and weighted all-days means use the same diaries. Which of the three objects differs between them? Which differs between the weighted all-days mean and the weighted weekday mean?

</Callout>

Write $$U$$ for a population of days and $$y_j$$ for the minutes of work on day $$j$$. The estimand is a property of the population; the estimator is a rule applied to the sample $$s$$:

$$
\theta_U = \frac{1}{N_U}\sum_{j \in U} y_j ,
\qquad
\hat\theta_U = \frac{\sum_{i \in s} w_i\,\delta_i\,y_i}{\sum_{i \in s} w_i\,\delta_i} .
$$

Here $$N_U$$ is the number of days in $$U$$, $$w_i$$ is the survey weight on diary $$i$$, and $$\delta_i = 1$$ when diary $$i$$ falls in $$U$$. Rows two through six of the table above use the same estimator. Only $$\delta$$ changes, because only the population changes. Weekdays, employed people, days with work, and employed people's work days are four choices of $$U$$ beyond the full population, so they are four more estimands. They do not compete with one another or with the published figure. A paper that reports any of them has answered a real question, provided it says which one.

The first two rows differ in another way. They share an estimand, the mean over all person-days, and use different estimators. The unweighted mean sets every $$w_i = 1$$, a claim that each diary stands for the same number of days. ATUS does not sample that way: weekend days are <Fact id="ch1.atus_weekend_share_sample" /> of the diaries but <Fact id="ch1.atus_weekend_share_weighted" /> of the weighted person-days, and the weights also adjust for who responds. [Chapter 2](/ch02-weights) takes the weight apart. The unweighted mean does not answer a different question. It answers this one badly.

### What the weight counts

Every weight has a unit, and the unit belongs to the estimand. The ATUS weight counts person-days. The 2023 weights sum to <Fact id="ch1.atus_weight_sum" /> person-days, 365 for each of about <Fact id="ch1.atus_persons_implied" /> people aged 15 and older.<SideNote>Dividing by 365 treats everyone as present all year. People turning 15, dying, or migrating make the quotient an average population.</SideNote> A weighted mean of ATUS diaries is an average over person-days. "Americans work <Fact id="ch1.atus_work_weighted_hours" /> hours a day" means the average person-day contains that much work, which is a different claim from how long a typical worker works. The employed-people rows are still averages over days, the days that belong to employed people. The days-worked rows average over days on which work happened, a population defined by the outcome itself.

In code the point shows in the structure. The estimator line never changes; only the filter does.

```python
import duckdb

MEAN = "SUM(WT06 * BLS_WORK) FILTER (WHERE {u}) / SUM(WT06) FILTER (WHERE {u})"
universes = {
    "all_days": "TRUE",
    "weekdays": "DAY BETWEEN 2 AND 6",            # DAY: 1 = Sunday ... 7 = Saturday
    "employed": "emp3 = 'Employed'",
    "days_worked": "BLS_WORK > 0",
    "employed_days_worked": "emp3 = 'Employed' AND BLS_WORK > 0",
}
means = ", ".join(f"{MEAN.format(u=u)} AS {name}" for name, u in universes.items())
duckdb.sql(f"""
    SELECT AVG(BLS_WORK) AS unweighted, {means}
    FROM 'analysis/atus/atus_respondent.parquet'
    WHERE YEAR = 2023
""").show()
```

The puzzle has no answer until the question names its population and its unit. A labor economist studying time on the job wants one of the last two rows; a researcher asking how Americans spend their days wants the second. That choice comes before any estimation, and the weight has to be read in the same unit.

## Which weight goes with which unit

The CPS Food Security Supplement, fielded each December for the U.S. Department of Agriculture, classifies a household as food insecure when its answers show difficulty affording enough food during the previous year <Cite id="rabbitt2024" />. The classification belongs to the household. The file copies it onto every person in the household and supplies both a household weight and a person weight. Each weight answers a different question.

USDA also publishes a child-specific measure, which asks whether the children themselves, and not only the household, were food insecure. Unit, weight, and definition each move the answer. The supplement carries no design variables or replicate weights, so these are point estimates without intervals; the lesson here is about targets, not uncertainty.

## When the estimand is a definition

The official poverty measure compares a family's pre-tax cash income with thresholds first set in the 1960s and raised each year with consumer prices. The Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM) counts tax credits and noncash benefits as resources, subtracts taxes and necessary spending such as medical and work expenses, and sets thresholds from current spending on basic needs, adjusted for local housing costs <Cite id="census_spm" />. Here both are computed from the Annual Social and Economic Supplement (ASEC) to the CPS, with the same person weight, for the same people. They are two estimands, and the choice between them is a choice of definition.

<Figure n="1.4" caption="The two definitions disagree in opposite directions by age. Child poverty was far lower under the SPM in 2021; for people 65 and older the SPM rate is the higher of the two in every year shown, by a wide margin from 2022 on.">

<Chart id="ch1.poverty_official_spm" />

</Figure>

In 2021 the official measure put <Fact id="ch1.pov_off_child_2021" ci /> of children in poverty and the SPM put <Fact id="ch1.pov_spm_child_2021" ci /> there, a gap of <Fact id="ch1.pov_gap_child_2021" ci /> percentage points (Figure 1.4). The Census Bureau attributes most of that year's SPM decline for children to the stimulus payments and the expanded, fully refundable Child Tax Credit, which count as resources under the SPM and not under the official measure <Cite id="creamer2022" />. SPM child poverty had been <Fact id="ch1.pov_spm_child_2020" /> in 2020; after the expansion lapsed it was <Fact id="ch1.pov_spm_child_2022" /> in 2022.

For older Americans the ordering reverses: <Fact id="ch1.pov_off_65_2021" /> official against <Fact id="ch1.pov_spm_65_2021" /> SPM in 2021, and <Fact id="ch1.pov_off_65_2024" /> against <Fact id="ch1.pov_spm_65_2024" /> in 2024, a gap of <Fact id="ch1.pov_gap_65_2024" ci /> percentage points. In the Census Bureau's accounting, subtracting out-of-pocket medical expenses raises the SPM rate for people 65 and older by more than it does for children <Cite id="creamer2022" />. Across all ages the SPM rate was below the official rate in 2021, <Fact id="ch1.pov_spm_all_2021" /> against <Fact id="ch1.pov_off_all_2021" />, and above it in every later year. Same survey, same weight, same people, and differences of opposite sign by age.

The ASEC is the one source in this chapter with replicate weights, so its intervals are design-based. Each uses the 160 successive-difference replicate weights, $$\widehat{\Var}(\hat\theta) = \tfrac{4}{160}\sum_{r=1}^{160}(\hat\theta_r - \hat\theta)^2$$, with 159 degrees of freedom. The interval for a gap comes from the replicate estimates of the gap itself, so it accounts for both measures being computed on the same children. [Chapter 3](/ch03-design#replicate-weights-briefly) and [Lab 3](/ch03-lab-replicates) develop the machinery.

Two qualifications apply to these rates. Official poverty here is IPUMS's `POVERTY` variable, not its `OFFPOV` variable, and the SPM rates use the ASEC person weight rather than the SPM weight IPUMS recommends, which this extract lacks. Checked against the Census Bureau's published rates for all people, children, and people 65 and older in 2019–2024, the official rates reproduce <Fact id="ch1.pov_match_official" />, and the SPM rates reproduce <Fact id="ch1.pov_match_spm" /> and are never more than <Fact id="ch1.pov_max_spm_gap" /> percentage points away.

## An oversample by design

<Callout kind="box" title="Provenance: the PSID's low-income oversample">

The Panel Study of Income Dynamics began in 1968 by combining a national probability sample with a large supplementary sample of low-income families, drawn on purpose to study poverty. A poverty rate computed from the pooled file without weights describes that pooled sample, in which poor families are overrepresented by design. It does not describe the country. Solon, Haider, and Wooldridge use this case to motivate the first reason to weight: when the goal is a population descriptive statistic and units were sampled with unequal probabilities, the weights are what point the estimator at the population quantity <Cite id="solon2015" />. The ATUS weekend oversample is the same situation in a current survey. The box describes the case; it does not reproduce the PSID numbers.

</Callout>

<Callout kind="note" title="A new case">

A survey records a household characteristic, say whether the home is overcrowded, and copies it onto every person record, alongside a household weight and a person weight. An analyst reports the person-weighted share. What has changed relative to the household rate, and when will the two differ?

<Details summary="One answer">

The unit has changed, and with it the estimand. A person-weighted share of a household characteristic counts each household once per member, so it describes people living in such households, not households. The classification is unchanged; the implicit weight on each household is now its size. The two rates differ whenever the characteristic is more or less common in larger households, as food insecurity is more common in households with children. For a household rate, use the household weight on one record per household.

</Details>

</Callout>
