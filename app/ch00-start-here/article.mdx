How much do Americans work in a day? The 2023 American Time Use Survey (ATUS) gives two answers from the same diaries and the same final weight: <Fact id="ch1.atus_work_weighted_hours" /> hours across all person-days of people 15 and older, and <Fact id="ch1.atus_work_employed_worked_hours" /> hours on the days when employed people did some work <Cite id="bls_atus" />. The outcome is the same in both: time spent working and in work-related activities, including travel for work. What differs is the set of days being averaged: every day of the year for everyone, or only the days on which an employed person worked.

Neither number is a mistake, and neither is the length of a typical paid workday. They answer different questions, and the difference between them has nothing to do with whether anyone "used the weights." Yet "Should I use the weights?" is the first question most applied researchers ask of a survey file. The question is incomplete. Inside it sit three separate decisions, and software asks about none of them: it asks for a weight variable and accepts almost any column of positive numbers.

- **Target: who and what the number describes.** Here, all person-days, or the work days of employed people. A different set of days is a different question, not a better or worse answer to the same one.
- **Contribution: how each observation counts.** ATUS assigns weekend days at a higher rate than weekdays, so weekends are about half of its diaries but two of every seven days in a year. Its weight gives each diary the number of person-days it stands for; counting every diary once would describe a sample that is half weekends.
- **Uncertainty: what could have come out differently.** Another draw of households, other people answering, another assignment of a policy. The two ATUS numbers come from an extract with weights but no design information, so they are point estimates: they carry a lesson about targets, not about uncertainty.

One question carries across the booklet:

<Callout kind="definition" title="The question to ask of any estimate">

**What is this number a weighted average of, and who chose the weights?**

</Callout>

Nearly every estimate an applied researcher reports is a weighted average of something: of respondents' answers, of cell means, of conditional effects. Sometimes a survey statistician chose the weights, knowing the selection probabilities. Sometimes the analyst chose them on purpose. And sometimes the estimator chose them, and nobody noticed.

### What the three stages show

In the first stage only the unweighted rate moves with the oversampling factor, because only it describes the sample's composition: it overstates the uninsured rate more as G is overrepresented. The weighted rate, in which each person counts $$1/\pi_i$$ times, stays centered on the population value across repeated samples, though any single sample can miss. The repair costs precision, which [Chapter 2](/ch02-weights) measures with Kish's approximation.

In the second stage the center of the distribution stays put while its width grows as fewer neighborhoods supply more residents each, and the naive standard error does not notice. A standard error clustered on neighborhoods tracks the actual spread on average. It runs slightly high when half the neighborhoods are sampled, because it ignores the finite-population correction, and with only a handful of neighborhoods it rests on a handful of cluster totals.

In the third stage the regression's target is a weighted average of cell effects, with weights proportional to each cell's share times $$p(1-p)$$ for its treated share $$p$$. Set either slider to zero and the target coincides with the population average effect: equal treated shares make the weights proportional to population shares, and equal effects make every average the same. Otherwise the target sits wherever the weights put it, and cells where treatment is split most evenly count for more than their share of the population.

## The vocabulary, in brief

Each term below is developed in the chapter where it does its work, and [Appendix A](/appendix-a-notation) collects the notation. None of it has to be learned before reading on.

### Estimand, estimator, estimate

The *estimand* is the population quantity you want, stated without reference to any data: the share of US adults who currently smoke, in 2024. The *estimator* is the rule that turns a sample into a number: a weighted proportion over respondents. The *estimate* is what the rule returns on the sample in hand. Arguments about "the right answer" are often arguments about estimands in disguise <Cite id="lundberg2021" />.

### Five populations

Every gap between these is a place where an analysis can drift, and [Chapter 1](/ch01-estimands#five-populations) follows all five through one estimate.

- The *target population* is the one the result is meant to describe.
- The *frame* is what the survey actually draws from.
- The *sampled population* is the part of the frame with a positive chance of selection.
- The *respondent population* is those in the sampled population who would answer if selected.
- The *analytic population* is who remains after the analyst's own restrictions and missing-data rules.

The *sample*, the units actually selected, and the *respondents*, the selected units that answered, are what one survey realizes from the sampled and respondent populations. They are not populations of their own.

### Kinds of weight

A survey weight is not one thing, and the booklet gives each part its own symbol.

- The *design weight* $$d_i = 1/\pi_i$$ undoes unequal selection probabilities $$\pi_i$$.
- A *nonresponse and calibration adjustment* $$g_i$$ moves the weights so that respondents match known population totals, and the final weight shipped on the file is $$w_i = d_i g_i$$. BRFSS 2024 builds its weight in this order: a design weight from the telephone stratum, the number of adults, and the number of phones; a correction for people reachable on both a landline and a cell phone; truncation of extreme design weights; then raking to demographic margins, state by state <Cite id="brfss2024" />. After raking, the weight is no longer an inverse selection probability <Cite id="gelman2007" />.
- A *frequency weight* $$f_i$$ says a row stands for $$f_i$$ identical observations. A *precision weight* $$a_i$$ says a row is more or less noisy than the others, as when each row is a group mean. Read as a frequency, precision, or sampling weight, the same column gives the same point estimate and three different standard errors, so the word "weight" in a software call explains nothing by itself ([Chapter 2](/ch02-weights)).
- A regression places *implicit weights* $$\omega_i$$ on units or cells that nobody supplied. With a binary treatment and a full set of dummies for covariate cells, ordinary least squares averages the cell-specific contrasts with weights proportional to the cell's share of the data times $$p_x(1-p_x)$$, where $$p_x$$ is the cell's treated share <Cite id="angrist1998" />. Cells in which nearly everyone, or nearly no one, is treated barely count. Equal case weights do not imply equal weighting of heterogeneous conditional effects <Cite id="aronow2016,sloczynski2022" />.
- *Standardization weights* $$s_a$$ redefine the comparison population: an age-standardized prevalence reweights age groups to a fixed standard population so that populations with different age structures can be compared ([Chapter 3](/ch03-design)).

### Four repetitions

A standard error is a statement about a hypothetical repetition, and the analysis has to say which one.

- *Sampling*: redraw the sample under the same design. NHANES draws counties, and its public file supports only <Fact id="ch7.nhanes_df" /> design degrees of freedom for the August 2021–August 2023 cycle; BRFSS treats every respondent as a separate sampling unit, which leaves it <Fact id="ch7.brfss_df" /> design degrees of freedom in 2024. Both are called complex surveys, and their uncertainty comes from opposite places ([Chapter 3](/ch03-design#two-complex-surveys-opposite-uncertainty)).
- *Assignment*: rerun the process that decided who was treated. When a policy varies across states, the number of independent assignments is the number of states, however many records there are ([Chapter 6](/ch06-clustering); <Cite id="abadie2023" />).
- *Response*: repeat the process that decided who answered. Because nonresponse and calibration adjustments are estimated from the sample, the weights themselves vary from sample to sample.
- *Model*: treat the finite population as one draw from a superpopulation. When every unit is observed, sampling uncertainty disappears, but assignment and model uncertainty can remain <Cite id="abadie2020" />.

The slogan that "weights get the point estimate and the design gets the uncertainty" survives only with a correction. The weights help define the estimator. The sampling design and the procedure that built the weights together determine how that estimator varies over repeated samples. Replicate weights that rerun the weighting in every replicate are one way producers package that fact for users.

<Callout kind="box" title="Four effective samples that are not the same thing">

"Effective sample size" names at least four different objects. They answer different questions, and none converts into another.

1. **Kish's effective sample size** prices the variance cost of unequal weights for a mean, under restrictive conditions. It says nothing about bias ([Chapter 2](/ch02-weights)).
2. **The design effective sample size** prices everything the design does to one estimator, so a survey has no single one ([Chapter 3](/ch03-design)).
3. **The regression-effective sample** is a composition, not a count: it says whose effects a regression coefficient summarizes ([Chapter 5](/ch05-hidden-weights)).
4. **Meng's data-defect effective sample size** prices bias as well as variance when inclusion is correlated with the outcome, and can be tiny for an enormous sample <Cite id="meng2018" />.

<Details summary="Formulas and conditions">

1. $$\nKish = n/(1+\CV(w)^2) = (\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$$, under an outcome unrelated to the weights, no clustering, and weights treated as fixed.
2. $$\ndesign(\hat\theta) = n/\DEFF(\hat\theta)$$, where the design effect $$\DEFF(\hat\theta)$$ is the design-based variance of one estimator divided by its variance under simple random sampling of the same size. Because it depends on the estimator and the outcome, one dataset yields several.
3. The sample reweighted by the implicit weights $$\omega_i$$ a regression places on each unit's conditional effect; with a binary treatment and correctly specified controls, $$\omega_i = (D_i - \E[D_i \mid X_i])^2$$. With linear controls and a treatment probability that is not linear in them, some implicit weights can be negative.
4. $$\nDDC$$ is the size of a simple random sample with the same mean squared error as a large sample whose inclusion is correlated with the outcome. It belongs to a planned essay, *When weighting is not enough*.

The first two are variance ratios, the third is a distribution over units, and the fourth is an equivalence in mean squared error. [Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations#four-effective-samples) derives each with its conditions.

</Details>

</Callout>

### One object, three dialects

The same objects travel under different names in different fields. This is a short version of the crosswalk in [Appendix A](/appendix-a-notation).

| The object | Survey statistics | Econometrics | Causal inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| The quantity sought | a finite-population parameter: total, mean, ratio | a population parameter, often a linear-projection coefficient | an estimand: PATE, ATT, CATE |
| How a unit enters | sampling weight, adjusted and calibrated | probability or analytic weight | inverse-propensity or balancing weight; implicit regression weight |
| Units that move together | primary sampling units within strata | clusters | units assigned together |
| The repetition | redrawing the sample under the design | redrawing from the data-generating process | rerunning the assignment |
| The variance | Taylor linearization or replicate weights | sandwich and cluster-robust estimators | randomization-based variance |
