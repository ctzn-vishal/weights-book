## Millions of records, dozens of policy shocks

On January 1, 2014, the Affordable Care Act's expansion of Medicaid to adults with incomes up to 138 percent of the federal poverty line took effect in <Fact id="ch6.states_jan2014" /> state units. Did uninsurance among low-income adults fall more where it took effect? The American Community Survey (ACS) looks built for the question <Cite id="ipums_usa" />. It asks about everyone in its sampled households, including whether each person has health coverage on the day of the interview. From 2011 through 2019 it holds <Fact id="ch6.records_full" /> records of adults ages 19 to 64 who live in households with family income at or below 138 percent of the poverty threshold, spread over <Fact id="ch6.units_full" /> state units: the 50 states and the District of Columbia.

The design in this chapter compares <Fact id="ch6.states_treated" /> states whose expansion took effect that January with <Fact id="ch6.states_comparison" /> states that had not expanded by the end of 2019. A PERWT-weighted linear probability model of being uninsured, with state and year fixed effects and an indicator for cohort states from 2014 on, returns a coefficient of <Fact id="ch6.ci_hc1" ci /> percentage points when the standard error is heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1). Change one argument, so that the standard error is clustered by state, and the same coefficient comes back as <Fact id="ch6.ci_state" ci />. Both versions are standard, and both run without complaint. The second standard error is <Fact id="ch6.ratio_state_hc1" /> the first.

## One cohort, one comparison, one window

Expansion did not arrive on one date. Michigan and New Hampshire expanded later in 2014; Pennsylvania, Indiana, and Alaska in 2015; Montana and Louisiana in 2016; Virginia and Maine in 2019; and more states after that. A single two-way fixed effects coefficient estimated across all of these dates averages many two-group comparisons, including some that use already-expanded states as controls for later ones, and when effects change over time some of those comparisons receive negative weight <Cite id="goodmanbacon2021" />. Estimators designed for staggered adoption avoid the problem <Cite id="callaway2021" />. This chapter avoids it by construction: one adoption date, and a comparison group that does not adopt within the window. With a single cohort and never-treated comparisons, the fixed effects regression reduces to one weighted 2×2 difference in differences: expansion states against never-expanded states, 2014–2019 against 2011–2013.

The rules below sort the 51 units using effective coverage dates from KFF's tracker of state decisions, checked state by state against federal and state documents <Cite id="kff2026" />. Figure 6.2 shows the result.

- **Cohort.** ACA expansion effective January 1, 2014. Of the <Fact id="ch6.states_jan2014" /> units that meet this, <Fact id="ch6.states_prior" /> are set aside. Six had already extended Medicaid to low-income adults statewide in 2010 or 2011, through the ACA's early option or a Section 1115 waiver: California, Connecticut, the District of Columbia, Minnesota, New Jersey, and Washington <Cite id="sommers2013" />. Five already covered low-income parents and childless adults broadly before 2014: Delaware, the District of Columbia, Massachusetts, New York, and Vermont <Cite id="kaestner2017,miller2021" />. For these states 2014 was a smaller change, and 2011–2013 is not a clean before.
- **Comparison.** No ACA expansion in effect by December 31, 2019, and no statewide change in adult eligibility inside the window. Wisconsin and Utah fail the second test: in April 2014 a Wisconsin waiver opened coverage to childless adults up to the poverty line while lowering parents' limit to it, and in April 2019 Utah began a partial expansion.
- **Later adopters.** Set aside.
- **Not counted as expansions.** Small pre-2014 programs stay in: Colorado's capped program for the poorest childless adults, county pilots in Cook County (Illinois), Cuyahoga County (Ohio), and St. Louis (Missouri), and eligibility cuts in Arizona, Hawaii, and Illinois. The closing panel reports what happens without them.
- **Window.** ACS survey years 2011 through 2019: three years before and six after. The window opens after the 2008–2009 recession and after the ACA's 2010 dependent-coverage provision reached young adults, and it closes before the 2020 ACS, an experimental release collected during the pandemic.

## Stage one: what the survey knows

The analysis has two stages, and they line up with the two things that could change.

The first stage estimates the uninsured share in each state and year. The ACS selects addresses within geographic strata and interviews everyone at a selected address. The person weight, PERWT, turns each record into a count of people. The 80 replicate weights repeat the sampling and weighting in a structured way (successive difference replication, the survey world's pre-packaged repeated sampling), so the spread of an estimate across replicates, scaled by 4/80, estimates its sampling variance with 79 degrees of freedom <Cite id="fay1995,census_acs_dm" />. Because the replicates redo the weighting as well as the selection, they also carry the uncertainty that weight construction adds. For the <Fact id="ch6.cells_design" /> state-year cells in the design, the median replicate standard error is <Fact id="ch6.median_cell_se" /> percentage points.

The second stage is the policy comparison, and it needs only the first stage's output. Write $$\bar y_{st}$$ for the PERWT-weighted uninsured share in state $$s$$ and year $$t$$, and $$W_{st}$$ for the cell's total weight. The state effect $$\alpha_s$$, the year effect $$\lambda_t$$, and the treatment indicator $$D_{st}$$ are all constant within a cell, so the weighted sum of squared residuals splits in two:

$$
\sum_{s,t}\sum_{i\in(s,t)} w_i\bigl(y_i-\alpha_s-\lambda_t-\beta D_{st}\bigr)^2
=\sum_{s,t}\sum_{i\in(s,t)} w_i\bigl(y_i-\bar y_{st}\bigr)^2
+\sum_{s,t} W_{st}\bigl(\bar y_{st}-\alpha_s-\lambda_t-\beta D_{st}\bigr)^2 .
$$

The first term does not involve the parameters. Minimizing over the person records therefore gives exactly the coefficient from a regression of the <Fact id="ch6.cells_design" /> cell shares on the same regressors, weighted by $$W_{st}$$; the build script confirms that the two agree to machine precision. A record affects the coefficient only through its cell's weighted share.

The identity concerns the point estimate. Whether the cell shares are independent pieces of information is the design question, and Figure 6.5 shows why the answer is not obvious.

## Clustering is a question about the design

Clustering is usually taught as a rule about errors: if residuals are correlated within groups, cluster by group. The rule cannot say which groups. Uninsurance residuals are correlated within states, and also within age groups, occupations, and genders. <Cite id="abadie2023" narrative /> replace the rule with a question about the design: what mechanism, if the study were repeated, would move many observations together? There are two candidates.

**Sampling design.** When a sample is drawn in clusters, a new sample replaces whole clusters at once, so the observations in a cluster rise and fall together across repetitions. The ACS household is such a cluster, since the survey selects an address and records everyone there. The fraction of clusters sampled matters. The ACS sees a small fraction of the nation's households, which is the setting where conventional clustering on the sampling unit fits. When most of a population's clusters are in the sample, sampling alone gives little reason to cluster, and conventional clustered standard errors can be severely inflated <Cite id="abadie2023" />.

**Assignment design.** When treatment is assigned in clusters, a different assignment changes the treatment of everyone in a cluster at once. Expansion was assigned to states: every low-income adult in a state lived under the same decision.

Neither mechanism operates by gender. The ACS does not sample genders, and no state expanded Medicaid for women only, so the design-based argument clusters by state and not by gender even though residuals are correlated within both. The same reasoning explains why a completely randomized experiment needs no clustering. If eligibility were assigned person by person in an independently drawn sample, nothing would move a state's records together, however correlated their outcomes.

The full statement has four parts. Identify every sampling or assignment mechanism that induces common uncertainty. In a nested hierarchy, the coarsest relevant level typically governs conventional clustering. Mechanisms that do not nest, such as a sample drawn by metropolitan area and a policy assigned by state where metropolitan areas cross state lines, may require different or multiway treatment. And the sampled fraction of clusters and the inferential target both matter. The familiar advice to "cluster at the coarser level" is the second part, not the whole statement.

In this application the mechanisms nest: persons within households, households within state-years, state-years within states. Which of them is relevant depends on the target. For a descriptive target, the change in the gap between these particular states with their policies taken as fixed, only the sample varies, and the household is the level. For the policy target, the effect of expansion, the assignment varies too, and the state is the coarsest relevant level. Every state that meets the rules is observed, so the sampled fraction of states is one. The case for clustering by state is an assignment argument, not a sampling argument; design-based uncertainty of this kind remains even when the sample is the whole population <Cite id="abadie2020" />. State fixed effects do not change this. They remove level differences between states, not shocks that persist within a state from year to year, which is why clustering below the state understates uncertainty in panel difference-in-differences <Cite id="bertrand2004" />.

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">
The state-clustered variance is a sum over clusters. With <Fact id="ch6.records_design" /> records in <Fact id="ch6.states_design" /> states, how many terms does the sum have?
</Callout>

Let $$\tilde D_{st}$$ be the treatment indicator after the weighted removal of state and year effects, and $$\hat e_{st}$$ the cell residual. The cluster-robust (CRV1) variance of the coefficient, with states as clusters $$g$$, is

$$
\widehat{\Var}_{\mathrm{CRV1}}(\hat\beta)=\frac{G}{G-1}\,\frac{N-1}{N-K}\,
\frac{\sum_{g=1}^{G}\Bigl(\sum_{t} W_{gt}\,\tilde D_{gt}\,\hat e_{gt}\Bigr)^{2}}{\Bigl(\sum_{s,t} W_{st}\,\tilde D_{st}^{2}\Bigr)^{2}} ,
$$

where $$G$$ is the number of states, $$N$$ the number of records, and $$K$$ the number of parameters not absorbed within clusters. The numerator has one term per state however many records each state contributes, and inference uses a $$t$$ distribution with $$G-1$$ degrees of freedom. The records determine how precisely each term is measured; the states determine how many terms there are.

The same coefficient therefore carries two ledgers, one for each target.

**Records.** HC1 treats each record as an independent draw. The design's records come from <Fact id="ch6.households_design" /> households, about <Fact id="ch6.persons_per_household" /> per household, so this is close to the ACS sampling design and far from the assignment design. The interval runs <Fact id="ch6.halfwidth_hc1" /> percentage points on either side of the estimate.

**Households and replicates.** Clustering on the ACS household cluster gives a standard error of <Fact id="ch6.se_household" /> percentage points and the replicate weights give <Fact id="ch6.se_sdr" />, against <Fact id="ch6.se_hc1" /> for HC1. All three describe survey sampling alone. The replicate value is the smallest, consistent with the stratification and calibration that the replicates reproduce and the other two rules ignore. For the descriptive ledger the replicate interval, <Fact id="ch6.ci_sdr" ci />, is the answer; the household-clustered interval, <Fact id="ch6.ci_household" ci />, is a close approximation.

**State-years.** Clustering by state-year allows shocks shared within a state in a given year but treats a state's 2013 and its 2016 as unrelated. No mechanism works at that level: expansion was assigned to a state for all later years at once, and a state's labor and insurance markets carry over from one year to the next. <Cite id="bertrand2004" narrative /> showed how badly this kind of clustering can understate uncertainty in difference-in-differences. The standard error rises to <Fact id="ch6.ratio_stateyear_hc1" /> the HC1 value, and still falls well short of the state-level rules.

**States.** Clustering by state follows the assignment: <Fact id="ch6.ci_state" ci />. The standard error, <Fact id="ch6.se_state" /> percentage points, is <Fact id="ch6.ratio_state_hc1" /> the HC1 value and <Fact id="ch6.ratio_state_sdr" /> the replicate value, and the interval runs <Fact id="ch6.halfwidth_state" /> points on either side.

**Few clusters.** <Fact id="ch6.states_design" /> clusters, <Fact id="ch6.states_treated" /> of them treated and unequal in size, is not many. Three standard remedies apply <Cite id="cameron2015,mackinnon2023" />. The first, a $$t$$ reference distribution with $$G-1$$ degrees of freedom, is already in the CRV1 interval. The second is the CRV3 jackknife, which re-estimates the coefficient leaving out one state at a time: <Fact id="ch6.ci_cv3" ci />. The third is the wild cluster restricted bootstrap, which imposes the null hypothesis and flips the signs of whole states' residuals at random <Cite id="cameron2008" />. With <Fact id="ch6.wcb_reps" /> replications, inverting it gives <Fact id="ch6.ci_wcb" ci />, and its p-value for no effect is <Fact id="ch6.p_wcb" />. The three agree closely, which was not guaranteed: with a few large treated states, they could have diverged.

**Randomization inference.** A different route asks how unusual the coefficient would be if expansion had gone to <Fact id="ch6.states_treated" /> of these states at random. Reassigning the cohort label <Fact id="ch6.ri_perms" /> times gives a p-value of <Fact id="ch6.p_ri" />, the smallest that many reassignments can produce, and inverting the test for a constant effect gives <Fact id="ch6.ci_ri" ci /> <Cite id="mackinnon2020" />. Expansion was a political decision, not a lottery, so this is a sensitivity analysis. It checks that the state-clustered interval is not badly off under a pure assignment model; it does not make the assignment random.

The level came first. The state was chosen from the design before any standard error was computed, and the estimators at that level were then compared for their accuracy with few clusters. The CRV1 interval is not the widest on the ladder (Figure 6.6), and "take the widest" would be a poor rule anyway: a rung with no mechanism behind it can be wide for the wrong reason.

```python
import pyfixest as pf

# acs: person records for the design states, 2011-2019; D = cohort x (YEAR >= 2014)
fit = pf.feols("uninsured ~ D | STATEFIP + YEAR", data=acs,
               weights="PERWT", vcov="hetero")    # HC1: records independent
fit.vcov({"CRV1": "CLUSTER"})                     # sampling design: ACS households
fit.vcov({"CRV1": "STATEFIP"})                    # assignment design: states
fit.tidy()
```

<SideNote>Two software details. The pyfixest CRV3 option scales the jackknife differently from the MacKinnon, Nielsen, and Webb definition used here, so it reports a somewhat larger standard error. Its wild bootstrap refuses weighted regressions, so the bootstrap in this chapter runs on the state-year regression multiplied through by the square root of the cell weights, which has the same cluster scores.</SideNote>

Start from the preset *State policy, survey records* and set the number of clusters to this chapter's design:

- **Inferential target.** The observed units. Every state that meets the cohort rules is in the data, and the question is about those states, not about a larger population of states.
- **Sampling mechanism.** Individuals were sampled within every cluster. The ACS samples addresses in every state; no state was left out by the sampling.
- **Assignment mechanism.** Treatment is assigned to whole clusters: everyone in a state shares its decision.
- **Nesting.** Yes. The survey's sampling units, addresses and the households at them, lie inside states.
- **Cluster fixed effects.** Yes: state fixed effects, alongside year effects.
- **Number of clusters.** <Fact id="ch6.states_design" /> states, <Fact id="ch6.states_treated" /> of them treated.

The worksheet's answer is to cluster at the state, the level where treatment is assigned, and, with fewer than about 50 assignment clusters, to use the wild cluster restricted bootstrap or CRV3 with $$t(G-1)$$ critical values rather than CRV1 with normal ones. The assumptions it lists are that states do not affect one another, that households nest inside states so the state level also covers the survey's clustering, and that treatment switches on within states over time, since state fixed effects absorb anything that never changes within a state. Because every state is observed and the states are the target, it adds that the uncertainty is about assignment rather than sampling, so the cluster-robust variance is conservative if effects differ across states. For sensitivity it asks for the heteroskedasticity-robust standard error only to measure how much clustering matters, and for CRV1, CRV3, and the wild bootstrap side by side, which the ladder above reports.

Two inputs carry the argument. Switch the assignment mechanism to the individual level and the recommendation becomes no clustering adjustment at all: when people are sampled within every state and treatment varies from person to person, correlated outcomes within states do not by themselves call for clustering <Cite id="abadie2023" />. Lower the number of clusters below 10 and it moves to randomization inference, which the ladder also reports. The two cutoffs, 50 and 10, are rules of thumb from the few-cluster literature <Cite id="mackinnon2023,cameron2015" />, not thresholds from Abadie, Athey, Imbens, and Wooldridge; read them as prompts to compare estimators, not as bright lines. The worksheet also states what it does not decide: whether the coefficient is causal. Parallel trends is an identification assumption, and no choice of standard error settles it.

## Back to the puzzle

The opening produced two intervals for one coefficient, and both are correct. They answer different questions. If the question is how much more uninsurance fell in these <Fact id="ch6.states_treated" /> states than in these <Fact id="ch6.states_comparison" />, with their policies taken as given, the only thing that would change in a repetition is the Census Bureau's sample, and the replicate interval is the honest one. If the question is what expansion did, a repetition would also redraw which states expanded, and the records in a state move together with that draw. For that question there are <Fact id="ch6.states_design" /> units of information, not <Fact id="ch6.records_design" />, and the state-clustered interval is the honest one. Neither interval is a cautious version of the other.
