## Thirty states, one decision each

Your regression has a policy that varies by state and an outcome measured on people, and its standard error rests on an argument you have not yet made. The software, left alone, treated every record as an independent draw. Your advisor says cluster by state. The referee says the policy was administered by counties, so cluster by county. One methods paper says the level of treatment assignment, another the level of sampling. Each choice gives a different interval around the same coefficient, and the widest is several times the narrowest. "The widest, to be safe" is not an answer, because the width of an interval is not evidence that it describes anything. The question that decides is what would change if the study were run again, and that question has an answer that can be read off the design. This chapter reads it off one study.

On January 1, 2014, the Affordable Care Act's expansion of Medicaid to adults with incomes up to 138 percent of the federal poverty line took effect wherever a state had agreed to it. The Supreme Court's 2012 ruling had made the expansion a state's choice, and states chose differently. Arkansas, Kentucky, and West Virginia expanded on the first day, and so did Arizona, Colorado, Hawaii, Illinois, Iowa, Maryland, Nevada, New Mexico, North Dakota, Ohio, Oregon, and Rhode Island. Texas, Florida, Georgia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and ten other states declined, and had still not expanded by the end of 2019. Between the two groups sit states that had already covered low-income adults before 2014, California and New York among them, and states such as Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Louisiana that expanded later.

The American Community Survey (ACS) interviews people in every one of these states every year and asks each whether they have health coverage on the day of the interview <Cite id="ipums_usa" />. For the <Fact id="ch6.states_treated" /> first-day expanders and <Fact id="ch6.states_comparison" /> states that never expanded in the window, it holds <Fact id="ch6.records_design" /> records of adults ages 19 to 64 in households at or below 138 percent of poverty, 2011 through 2019. A PERWT-weighted linear probability model of being uninsured, with state and year fixed effects and an indicator for expansion states from 2014 on, puts the change in the uninsured-rate gap at <Fact id="ch6.beta" /> percentage points. Treat every record as an independent draw, with a heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard error, and the coefficient comes back as <Fact id="ch6.ci_hc1" ci />. Change one argument so that the standard error is clustered by state, and the same coefficient comes back as <Fact id="ch6.ci_state" ci />, with a standard error <Fact id="ch6.ratio_state_hc1" /> as large.

## One cohort, one comparison, one window

Expansion did not arrive on one date. Michigan and New Hampshire expanded later in 2014; Pennsylvania, Indiana, and Alaska in 2015; Montana and Louisiana in 2016; Virginia and Maine in 2019. A single two-way fixed effects coefficient estimated across all of these dates averages many two-group comparisons, some of which use already-expanded states as controls for later ones and receive negative weight when effects change over time <Cite id="goodmanbacon2021" />; estimators designed for staggered adoption avoid this <Cite id="callaway2021" />. This chapter avoids it by construction: one adoption date, and a comparison group that does not adopt within the window, so every comparison is between expansion states and never-expanded states, 2014–2019 against 2011–2013. That removes the staggered-adoption problem. It does not make the comparison causal, which rests on the assumptions below.

The rules sort the <Fact id="ch6.units_full" /> state units, the 50 states and the District of Columbia, using effective coverage dates from KFF's tracker of state decisions, checked state by state against federal and state documents <Cite id="kff2026" />. Of the <Fact id="ch6.records_full" /> records in the full 2011–2019 file, the design keeps <Fact id="ch6.records_design" />. Figure 6.2 shows the result.

- **Cohort.** ACA expansion effective January 1, 2014. Of the <Fact id="ch6.states_jan2014" /> units that meet this, <Fact id="ch6.states_prior" /> are set aside because 2014 was a smaller change for them and 2011–2013 is not a clean before: six had already extended Medicaid to low-income adults statewide in 2010 or 2011 through the ACA's early option or a Section 1115 waiver (California, Connecticut, the District of Columbia, Minnesota, New Jersey, Washington) <Cite id="sommers2013" />, and five already covered low-income parents and childless adults broadly (Delaware, the District of Columbia, Massachusetts, New York, Vermont) <Cite id="kaestner2017,miller2021" />.
- **Comparison.** No ACA expansion in effect by December 31, 2019, and no statewide change in adult eligibility inside the window. Wisconsin and Utah fail the second test: in April 2014 a Wisconsin waiver opened coverage to childless adults up to the poverty line while lowering parents' limit to it, and in April 2019 Utah began a partial expansion.
- **Later adopters.** Set aside.
- **Window.** ACS survey years 2011 through 2019: three years before and six after. The window opens after the 2008–2009 recession and after the ACA's 2010 dependent-coverage provision reached young adults, and it closes before the 2020 ACS, an experimental release collected during the pandemic.

## Stage one: what the survey knows

The analysis has two stages, and they line up with the two things that could change in a repetition.

The first stage estimates the uninsured share in each state and year. The ACS selects addresses within geographic strata and interviews everyone at a selected address. The person weight, PERWT, turns each record into a count of people. The 80 replicate weights repeat the sampling and weighting in a structured way (successive difference replication), so the spread of an estimate across replicates, scaled by 4/80, estimates its sampling variance with 79 degrees of freedom, including the uncertainty that weight construction adds <Cite id="fay1995,census_acs_dm" />. For the <Fact id="ch6.cells_design" /> state-year cells in the design, the median replicate standard error is <Fact id="ch6.median_cell_se" /> percentage points.

The second stage is the policy comparison, and it needs only the first stage's output. Write $$\bar y_{st}$$ for the PERWT-weighted uninsured share in state $$s$$ and year $$t$$, and $$W_{st}$$ for the cell's total weight. The state effect $$\alpha_s$$, the year effect $$\lambda_t$$, and the treatment indicator $$D_{st}$$ are all constant within a cell, so the weighted sum of squared residuals splits in two:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{s,t}\sum_{i\in(s,t)} w_i\bigl(y_i-\alpha_s-\lambda_t-\beta D_{st}\bigr)^2
&=\sum_{s,t}\sum_{i\in(s,t)} w_i\bigl(y_i-\bar y_{st}\bigr)^2 \\
&\quad+\sum_{s,t} W_{st}\bigl(\bar y_{st}-\alpha_s-\lambda_t-\beta D_{st}\bigr)^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first term does not involve the parameters. Minimizing over the person records therefore gives exactly the coefficient from a regression of the <Fact id="ch6.cells_design" /> cell shares on the same regressors, weighted by $$W_{st}$$; the build script confirms that the two agree to machine precision. A record affects the coefficient only through its cell's weighted share.

The identity concerns the point estimate. Whether the cell shares are independent pieces of information is the design question, and Figure 6.5 shows why the answer is not obvious.

## Clustering is a question about the design

Clustering is usually taught as a rule about errors: if residuals are correlated within groups, cluster by group. The rule cannot say which groups. Uninsurance residuals are correlated within states, and also within age groups, occupations, and genders. <Cite id="abadie2023" narrative /> replace the rule with a question about the design: what mechanism, if the study were repeated, would move many observations together? There are two candidates.

**Sampling design.** When a sample is drawn in clusters, a new sample replaces whole clusters at once, so the observations in a cluster rise and fall together across repetitions. The ACS household is such a cluster: the survey selects an address and records everyone there. The fraction of clusters sampled matters. The ACS sees a small fraction of the nation's households, the setting where conventional clustering on the sampling unit fits; when most of a population's clusters are in the sample, sampling alone gives little reason to cluster, and conventional clustered standard errors can be severely inflated <Cite id="abadie2023" />.

**Assignment design.** When treatment is assigned in clusters, a different assignment changes the treatment of everyone in a cluster at once. Expansion was assigned to states: every low-income adult in Kentucky lived under Kentucky's decision, and every one in Tennessee under Tennessee's.

Neither mechanism operates by gender. The ACS does not sample genders, and no state expanded Medicaid for women only, so the argument clusters by state and not by gender even though residuals are correlated within both. For the same reason a completely randomized experiment needs no clustering: if eligibility were assigned person by person in an independently drawn sample, nothing would move a state's records together, however correlated their outcomes.

The full statement has four parts: identify every sampling or assignment mechanism that induces common uncertainty; in a nested hierarchy, let the coarsest relevant level govern conventional clustering; treat mechanisms that do not nest, such as a sample drawn by metropolitan area and a policy assigned by state, with multiway or other methods; and let the sampled fraction of clusters and the inferential target both matter. The familiar advice to "cluster at the coarser level" is the second part, not the whole statement.

Here the mechanisms nest: persons within households, households within state-years, state-years within states. Which is relevant depends on the target. For a descriptive target, the change in the gap between these particular states with their policies taken as fixed, only the sample varies, and the household is the level. For the policy target, the effect of expansion, the assignment varies too, and the state is the coarsest relevant level. Every state that meets the rules is observed, so the case for clustering by state is an assignment argument, not a sampling one; design-based uncertainty of this kind remains even when the sample is the whole population <Cite id="abadie2020" />. State fixed effects remove level differences between states, not shocks that persist within a state from year to year, which is why clustering below the state understates uncertainty in panel difference-in-differences <Cite id="bertrand2004" />.

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">
The state-clustered variance is a sum over clusters. With <Fact id="ch6.records_design" /> records in <Fact id="ch6.states_design" /> states, how many terms does the sum have?
</Callout>

Let $$\tilde D_{st}$$ be the treatment indicator after the weighted removal of state and year effects, and $$\hat e_{st}$$ the cell residual. The cluster-robust (CRV1) variance of the coefficient, with states as clusters $$g$$, is

$$
\widehat{\Var}_{\mathrm{CRV1}}(\hat\beta)=\frac{G}{G-1}\,\frac{N-1}{N-K}\,
\frac{\sum_{g=1}^{G}\Bigl(\sum_{t} W_{gt}\,\tilde D_{gt}\,\hat e_{gt}\Bigr)^{2}}{\Bigl(\sum_{s,t} W_{st}\,\tilde D_{st}^{2}\Bigr)^{2}} ,
$$

where $$G$$ is the number of states, $$N$$ the number of records, and $$K$$ the number of parameters not absorbed within clusters. The numerator has one term per state however many records each state contributes, and inference uses a $$t$$ distribution with $$G-1$$ degrees of freedom. The records determine how precisely each term is measured; the states determine how many terms there are.

The same coefficient therefore carries two ledgers, one for each target. Read them side by side: the estimator and both kinds of weight are the same, while the estimand, the source of randomness, the variance estimator, and the assumptions change.

## The standard-error ladder

<Chart id="ch6.se_ladder_table" />

**Records.** HC1 treats each record as an independent draw, a repetition that no mechanism in this design produces. The design's records come from <Fact id="ch6.households_design" /> households, about <Fact id="ch6.persons_per_household" /> per household, so HC1 lands near the household-clustered and replicate values by numerical accident, not because it knows the ACS design, whose strata and weighting it ignores. The interval runs <Fact id="ch6.halfwidth_hc1" /> percentage points on either side of the estimate.

**Households and replicates.** Clustering on the ACS household cluster gives a standard error of <Fact id="ch6.se_household" /> percentage points and the replicate weights give <Fact id="ch6.se_sdr" />, against <Fact id="ch6.se_hc1" /> for HC1. All three describe survey sampling alone. The replicate value is the smallest, consistent with the stratification and calibration that the replicates reproduce and the other two rules ignore. For the descriptive ledger the replicate interval, <Fact id="ch6.ci_sdr" ci />, is the answer; the household-clustered interval, <Fact id="ch6.ci_household" ci />, is a close approximation.

**State-years.** Clustering by state-year allows shocks shared within a state in a given year but treats a state's 2013 and its 2016 as unrelated. No mechanism works at that level: expansion was assigned to a state for all later years at once, and a state's labor and insurance markets carry over from one year to the next <Cite id="bertrand2004" />. The standard error rises to <Fact id="ch6.ratio_stateyear_hc1" /> the HC1 value and still falls well short of the state-level rules, which answers the prediction: wider than the record-level interval, but closer to it than to the state-clustered one.

**States.** Clustering by state follows the assignment: <Fact id="ch6.ci_state" ci />. The standard error, <Fact id="ch6.se_state" /> percentage points, is <Fact id="ch6.ratio_state_hc1" /> the HC1 value and <Fact id="ch6.ratio_state_sdr" /> the replicate value, and the interval runs <Fact id="ch6.halfwidth_state" /> points on either side.

**Few clusters.** <Fact id="ch6.states_design" /> clusters, <Fact id="ch6.states_treated" /> of them treated and unequal in size, is not many. Three standard remedies apply <Cite id="cameron2015,mackinnon2023" />. The first, a $$t$$ reference distribution with $$G-1$$ degrees of freedom, is already in the CRV1 interval. The second is the CRV3 jackknife, which re-estimates the coefficient leaving out one state at a time: <Fact id="ch6.ci_cv3" ci />. The third is the wild cluster restricted bootstrap, which imposes the null hypothesis and flips the signs of whole states' residuals at random <Cite id="cameron2008" />. With <Fact id="ch6.wcb_reps" /> replications, inverting it gives <Fact id="ch6.ci_wcb" ci />, with a p-value for no effect of <Fact id="ch6.p_wcb" />. The three agree closely, which a few large treated states could have prevented.

**Randomization inference.** A different route asks how unusual the coefficient would be if expansion had gone to <Fact id="ch6.states_treated" /> of these states at random. Reassigning the cohort label <Fact id="ch6.ri_perms" /> times gives a p-value of <Fact id="ch6.p_ri" />, the smallest that many reassignments can produce, and inverting the test for a constant effect gives <Fact id="ch6.ci_ri" ci /> <Cite id="mackinnon2020" />. Expansion was a political decision, not a lottery, so this is a sensitivity check on the state-clustered interval under a pure assignment model; it does not make the assignment random.

The level came first. The state was chosen from the design before any standard error was computed, and the estimators at that level were then compared for their accuracy with few clusters. The CRV1 interval is not the widest on the ladder (Figure 6.6); a rung with no mechanism behind it can be wide for the wrong reason.

<Details summary="In code: the ladder in pyfixest">

The first four rungs are one fit and a change of the `vcov` argument.

```python
import pyfixest as pf

# acs: person records for the design states, 2011-2019; D = cohort x (YEAR >= 2014)
fit = pf.feols("uninsured ~ D | STATEFIP + YEAR", data=acs,
               weights="PERWT", vcov="hetero")    # HC1: records independent
fit.vcov({"CRV1": "CLUSTER"})                     # sampling design: ACS households
fit.vcov({"CRV1": "STATEFIP"})                    # assignment design: states
fit.tidy()
```

Two software details. The pyfixest CRV3 option scales the jackknife differently from the MacKinnon, Nielsen, and Webb definition used here, so it reports a somewhat larger standard error. Its wild bootstrap refuses weighted regressions, so the bootstrap in this chapter runs on the state-year regression multiplied through by the square root of the cell weights, which has the same cluster scores. The replicate-weight rung reruns the fit on each replicate weight; no `vcov` option produces it.

</Details>

Start from the preset *State policy, survey records* and set the number of clusters to this chapter's design:

- **Inferential target.** The observed units: every state that meets the cohort rules is in the data, and the question is about those states.
- **Sampling mechanism.** Individuals sampled within every cluster; the ACS samples addresses in every state.
- **Assignment mechanism.** Whole clusters: everyone in a state shares its decision.
- **Nesting.** Yes; addresses and households lie inside states.
- **Cluster fixed effects.** Yes, state fixed effects alongside year effects.
- **Number of clusters.** <Fact id="ch6.states_design" /> states, <Fact id="ch6.states_treated" /> of them treated.

The worksheet's answer is to cluster at the state, the level where treatment is assigned, and, with fewer than about 50 assignment clusters, to use the wild cluster restricted bootstrap or CRV3 with $$t(G-1)$$ critical values rather than CRV1 with normal ones. It lists the assumptions that answer rests on: states do not affect one another; households nest inside states, so the state level also covers the survey's clustering; and treatment switches on within states over time, since state fixed effects absorb anything that never changes within a state. Because every state is observed and the states are the target, it adds that the uncertainty is about assignment rather than sampling, so the cluster-robust variance is conservative if effects differ across states.

Two inputs carry the argument. Switch the assignment mechanism to the individual level and the recommendation becomes no clustering adjustment at all: when treatment varies from person to person, correlated outcomes within states do not by themselves call for clustering <Cite id="abadie2023" />. Lower the number of clusters below 10 and it moves to randomization inference. The cutoffs, 50 and 10, are rules of thumb <Cite id="mackinnon2023,cameron2015" />, prompts to compare estimators rather than bright lines. The worksheet does not decide whether the coefficient is causal; no choice of standard error settles that.

## Two questions, two intervals

The opening set a record-level interval beside a state-clustered one, but those are not the two answers; the descriptive question and the policy question each have their own. If the question is how much more uninsurance fell in these <Fact id="ch6.states_treated" /> states than in these <Fact id="ch6.states_comparison" />, with their policies taken as given, only the Census Bureau's sample would change in a repetition, and the replicate interval, <Fact id="ch6.ci_sdr" ci />, describes it. If the question is what expansion did, a repetition would also redraw which states expanded, and for that question there are <Fact id="ch6.states_design" /> units of information, not <Fact id="ch6.records_design" />; the state-clustered interval describes it, with the few-cluster checks beside it. Neither interval is a cautious version of the other.

<SideNote>Weighting each state-year cell equally instead of by population targets the average state rather than the average low-income adult, and gives <Fact id="ch6.sens_equal_beta" />. That is a change of estimand, the subject of [Chapter 5](/ch05-hidden-weights), not a change of variance.</SideNote>

The principle does not depend on states or on the ACS: a standard error describes a repetition, and clustering asks which observations would move together in it, through the sampling of units or the assignment of the regressor. [Chapter 7](/ch07-cluster-choice) carries it across a classroom lottery, a state variable in an individual regression, shocks that travel over the map or hit every unit at once, and a shift-share exposure.

<Callout kind="note" title="A new case">

A study observes every state, not a sample of them, and a policy was adopted by some states and not others. With no sampling of states, is there any uncertainty left for a standard error to describe?

<Details summary="One answer">

Yes. The survey still samples households within each state, so each state's outcome carries sampling error, though here it is small. The larger uncertainty is about assignment: which states adopted could have been otherwise, and the policy question treats that assignment as the repetition. Observing every state removes the sampling argument for clustering by state, not the assignment argument <Cite id="abadie2023" />. Uncertainty about the design itself, the cohort rules and parallel trends, is in no standard error at all.
