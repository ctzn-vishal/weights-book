## Did you use the weights?

The question arrives in the second paragraph of the referee report, and it sounds like a question about software: did you use the weights? You ran a regression on a public-use file. The person weight is a column you left alone, or one you added because a manual said to, and you now have to decide whether to rerun everything, and if the rerun gives a different number, which one is right, and what to say if it does not. This chapter answers the referee with a regression that is easy to care about. A worker weighing a remote offer, a manager setting pay bands for a hybrid team, and a reporter with a headline to write all want the same number: do people who work from home earn more or less than people who commute?

The 2024 American Community Survey (ACS) can answer a version of that question. Among full-time wage and salary workers aged 25 to 64, people who usually worked from home in the survey's reference week earned <Fact id="ch4.raw_wls" /> log points more per hour than commuters, about <Fact id="ch4.raw_wls_pct" /> more, in the survey-weighted data. Nobody should publish that number as the answer. Home workers are far more likely to hold a degree: <Fact id="ch4.prev_ba" /> of workers with at least a bachelor's degree work from home, against <Fact id="ch4.prev_lths" /> of workers without a high school diploma. Adjust for age, sex, and education and the gap is <Fact id="ch4.adj_wls" /> log points, about <Fact id="ch4.adj_wls_pct" />. Add fixed effects for detailed occupation, industry, and state, and it is <Fact id="ch4.occind_wls" />, about <Fact id="ch4.occind_wls_pct" />: an accountant at home set against an accountant in the office. Three defensible numbers, and the choice of comparison, not the arithmetic, separates them. Every one of them describes who earns what; none says what working from home does to pay.

Now the referee's question. The adjusted coefficient is <Fact id="ch4.adj_ols" /> log points without survey weights and <Fact id="ch4.adj_wls" /> with them, a difference of <Fact id="ch4.adj_gap_abs" />. Two conclusions are tempting. The first is that weighting does not matter for this question. The second is that, because the two estimates agree, either one is the average home–commute wage contrast in the population. The first is close to right for the headline, for reasons <Cite id="solon2015" narrative /> set out, and wrong for one group of workers inside it, as this chapter shows. The second is wrong, and [Chapter 5](/ch05-hidden-weights) measures by how much in a finer version of the same model.

## What are we weighting for?

The ACS does not sample everyone at the same rate. It samples addresses in small counties more heavily than addresses in large ones, some kinds of household answer less often than others, and the Census Bureau's person weight `PERWT` is built to undo both, then calibrated so the weighted sample matches population totals by age, sex, race, and Hispanic origin <Cite id="census_acs_dm" />. For a share, the weight's job is the whole job. Unweighted, <Fact id="ch4.prev_unw" /> of the records in this chapter's sample work from home; weighted, <Fact id="ch4.prev_w" ci /> of the population they stand for does. The second number is the one a reporter wants.

For the wage gap the question is harder, because "the gap" can mean three different things.

1. **The population's adjusted contrast.** In the population of full-time workers, the coefficient on working from home in the best linear predictor of log wages given age, sex, and education. This is a fixed number about the 2024 population, whether or not the linear equation is a true model of wages.
2. **The sample's adjusted contrast.** The same coefficient, defined for the sample as drawn. If the ACS over-represents small states and under-represents young renters, this is the projection in a population with that tilt.
3. **The average of many contrasts.** The home–commute gap differs from one age-sex-education cell to another. An average of those cell contrasts, weighted by each cell's share of the population, is a third quantity, and a regression does not report it without further work.

Survey weights move the estimator from the second target to the first. They say nothing about the third: a regression averages heterogeneous contrasts with weights of its own choosing, whether or not the records carry `PERWT` ([Chapter 5](/ch05-hidden-weights)). And none of the three is causal. Workers and employers decide who works from home, and jobs that allow it differ from jobs that do not in ways the survey does not record. This chapter's target is the first quantity, stated as an equation below, and its question is how much the weights change the estimate of it and what the answer means.

The target is the population linear projection

$$
y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + X_i'\gamma + \varepsilon_i ,
$$

where $$y_i$$ is the log hourly wage of person $$i$$, $$D_i = 1$$ if the person works from home (WFH), and $$X_i$$ holds age, age squared, a female indicator, and four education levels. The coefficient $$\beta$$ is a well-defined population quantity whether or not the equation is a true model of wages: it is the coefficient on $$D_i$$ in the population's best linear predictor of $$y_i$$. That answers the prediction. The weighted regression estimates the population projection coefficient, which is not, in general, the population's average home–commute contrast. Agreement between the weighted and unweighted fits says something about the first two quantities and nothing about the third.

The sample is the <Fact id="ch4.n_sample" /> ACS person records of wage and salary workers aged 25 to 64 who usually worked at least 30 hours a week, worked at least 40 weeks in the past 12 months, had positive wage income, and were at work in the survey's reference week; <Fact id="ch4.n_wfh" /> of them worked from home. <Callout kind="definition" title="What 'works from home' means in the ACS">

The indicator $$D_i$$ is `TRANWORK = 80`, "Worked at home," from the ACS question about how the person usually got to work last week. `TRANWORK` records the *primary* means of transportation, so a hybrid worker who spends two days at home and three at the office is coded by the commute. Working from home in this chapter means usually working at home in the reference week, not teleworking at all. The Current Population Survey's telework item, `TELWRKPAY`, counts anyone who teleworked for pay at any time last week, hybrid workers included. The two surveys measure different things and estimate different contrasts, and no reweighting of either reconciles them: [Chapter 3](/ch03-design#interlude-weighting-repairs-representation-not-measurement)'s measurement lesson in a new setting. The CPS item is not in this book's data, so this chapter makes no numerical comparison.

## Why the weights barely matter here

Solon, Haider, and Wooldridge give three reasons to weight a regression aimed at a causal or structural parameter. The first is **precision**. If the error variance differs across observations in a known way, weighting each observation by the inverse of its variance, a precision weight $$a_i$$, gives a more efficient estimator; the textbook case is a regression on group averages, weighted by group size. The second is **consistency**. If the chance of entering the sample depends on the outcome even after conditioning on the regressors, sampling is *endogenous*, unweighted estimates of the population relationship are inconsistent, and weighting by inverse selection probabilities repairs them <Cite id="wooldridge1999" />. If sampling depends only on the regressors, it is *exogenous*, and both estimators are consistent for a correctly specified model, with the unweighted one usually more precise. The third is recovering a population average effect when effects are heterogeneous; they show that weighting alone does not deliver one.

The precision motive has a sharp limit, identified by <Cite id="dickens1990" narrative />. When group averages share a group-level error component, such as a state-wide shock, the variance of a group mean stops shrinking with group size, and weighting by size can make estimates less precise. Neither the motive nor the critique concerns `PERWT`, which is not a precision weight but a survey weight $$w_i$$. Used as a regression weight where it is not needed for consistency, it costs precision. The coefficient of variation of `PERWT` in this sample is <Fact id="ch4.cv_w" />, so Kish's effective sample for a simple mean, $$\nKish/n$$, is <Fact id="ch4.kish_ratio" /> of the records <Cite id="kish1965" />, and the heteroskedasticity-robust variance of the WLS coefficient is <Fact id="ch4.weighting_var_ratio" /> times that of OLS.

That leaves consistency. If, given working from home, age, sex, and education, the chance of being sampled and responding is unrelated to wages, sampling is exogenous; with a correctly specified model the sample projection and the population projection coincide, and OLS and WLS agree. The ladder says they nearly do. Agreement has a second explanation: the contrast varies across groups, so the model is misspecified, but the groups the weights push up or down have similar contrasts. Either way, the choice of weights is not what moves this coefficient, and the check costs one extra line of code. What agreement does not show is that either coefficient is the third quantity above, the population's average contrast. A regression leans on groups where both home workers and commuters are plentiful, whatever their share of the population. `PERWT` fixes whom the sample represents; it does not fix how the regression weighs one group's contrast against another's.

### When weighting would matter more

The agreement is a finding about this coefficient in this sample, not a rule. Three conditions make weighting matter more.

- **Inclusion related to the outcome.** If the chance of being sampled or of responding depends on wages even given the regressors, the unweighted fit is inconsistent for the population projection <Cite id="wooldridge1999" />.
- **Composition that interacts with heterogeneous contrasts.** If the contrast differs across groups and the sample's mix of groups differs from the population's, the two estimators average the same contrasts with different weights.
- **Influential records.** A few records with very large weights or high leverage can move a weighted coefficient that the unweighted fit barely notices.

The influence check below finds no dominant group here, and the second condition is the one that surfaces once the sample is split. Whether inclusion depends on wages cannot be checked from the sample alone.

## Testing the difference: DuMouchel and Duncan

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

The weighted and unweighted coefficients differ by <Fact id="ch4.adj_gap_abs" /> log points. With <Fact id="ch4.n_sample" /> records, will a formal test say that they differ?

</Callout>

<Cite id="dumouchel1983" narrative /> turned the comparison into a test. Regress the outcome by OLS on the covariates, the weight, and the weight interacted with each covariate, and test whether the weight terms are jointly zero:

$$
y_i = X_i'b + (\tilde w_i X_i)'c + u_i, \qquad H_0 : c = 0 ,
$$

where $$\tilde w_i = w_i/\bar w$$ is `PERWT` divided by its mean and $$X_i$$ includes the intercept and $$D_i$$. The null is the restriction under which the weighted and unweighted estimators share a probability limit. Here the test rejects: F = <Fact id="ch4.dd_f" /> on <Fact id="ch4.dd_q" /> restrictions with household-clustered standard errors, p <Fact id="ch4.dd_p" />. The joint test covers every weight term, including those on age, sex, and education, whose coefficients this chapter does not interpret. The term that bears on the work-from-home coefficient is the weight's interaction with $$D_i$$, with t = <Fact id="ch4.dd_t_wd" />: workers who carry larger weights show smaller home–commute contrasts. The levels model rejects as well (F = <Fact id="ch4.dd_lev_f" />).

A rejection is a diagnostic, not a verdict <Cite id="bollen2016" />. With close to a million records the test has the power to detect departures far too small to matter: the two work-from-home coefficients differ by <Fact id="ch4.adj_gap_abs" /> log points, <Fact id="ch4.log_gap_rel" /> of the unweighted coefficient and about <Fact id="ch4.gap_rel_se" /> standard errors of the weighted one. The rejection does not say which number is wanted or why they differ, and at least four stories fit: the model may be *misspecified*, with heterogeneous contrasts that the weights average differently; sampling may be *endogenous*; a few heavily weighted records may have *leverage*; or the estimators may target *different estimands*, the sample's projection and the population's, both legitimate.

## The standard error: which repetition?

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Figure 4.5 lists five standard errors for the same coefficient. Which do you expect to be largest, and by roughly how much?

</Callout>

Agreement of point estimates says nothing about uncertainty. A standard error describes a repetition, and the five in Figure 4.5 describe different ones.

<Figure n="4.5" caption="The robust, household-clustered, and replicate standard errors nearly coincide; the state-clustered one is several times larger because it imagines a repetition the ACS never performs.">
  <Chart id="ch4.se_table" />
</Figure>

The classical WLS standard error (<Fact id="ch4.se_wls_iid" />) treats `PERWT` as a precision weight and is too small. The heteroskedasticity-robust (<Fact id="ch4.se_wls_hc1" />) and household-clustered (<Fact id="ch4.se_wls_crhh" />) standard errors are larger. The design-based answer uses the ACS's 80 replicate weights, built by successive difference replication (SDR): re-solve the weighted regression once with each replicate weight $$w_i^{(r)}$$ and combine the deviations as $$\Var(\hat\beta) = \tfrac{4}{80}\sum_{r=1}^{80}(\hat\beta_r - \hat\beta)^2$$ with 79 degrees of freedom ([In code](#in-code)). The Census Bureau built each replicate weight by rerunning its weighting on perturbed base weights, so the SDR standard error reflects the sample design and the weight construction together. It is <Fact id="ch4.se_wls_sdr" />, <Fact id="ch4.se_ratio_sdr_hh" /> times the household-clustered value: the ACS samples addresses, so households (<Fact id="ch4.n_households" /> of them here) are its only clusters, and stratification and calibration trim a little off the variance. Applying the same replicate perturbations to equal case weights gives an approximate design-based standard error for OLS, and the WLS variance is <Fact id="ch4.design_var_ratio" /> times as large: under this design, as under the robust formulas, the unweighted estimator is the more precise one.

Clustering by state (<Fact id="ch4.n_states" /> clusters, counting the District of Columbia) gives <Fact id="ch4.se_wls_crstate" />, <Fact id="ch4.se_ratio_state_sdr" /> times the replicate standard error. The arithmetic is right; the question is different. It imagines a repetition that redraws whole states, and neither the ACS sample nor anything in this descriptive target is drawn or assigned by state. [Chapter 6](/ch06-clustering#clustering-is-a-question-about-the-design) sets out when such a repetition is the relevant one.

<Details summary="The Moulton factor when the regressor varies within clusters">

[Chapter 3](/ch03-design#the-moulton-bridge) used the factor of <Cite id="moulton1990" narrative /> for a regressor that is constant within clusters. When the regressor varies within clusters and cluster sizes $$m$$ vary, the factor becomes

$$
1 + \Big[\tfrac{\Var(m)}{\bar m} + \bar m - 1\Big]\rho_x\,\rho_u
$$

where $$\bar m$$ is the mean cluster size and $$\rho_x$$ and $$\rho_u$$ are the within-cluster correlations of the regressor and the error. It approximates how much clustering inflates the variance of an OLS coefficient relative to the classical formula. It is a special-case regression analogue of the clustering component of a design effect, not the design effect itself ([Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations#the-moulton-factor-as-a-special-case)): it prices clustering only, and it assumes that every pair of errors in a cluster is equally correlated. The observed inflation is the ratio of the cluster-robust to the classical variance of the unweighted coefficient. For households the factor predicts <Fact id="ch4.moulton_hh" /> against an observed <Fact id="ch4.moulton_hh_emp" />. For states it predicts <Fact id="ch4.moulton_state" /> against an observed <Fact id="ch4.moulton_state_emp" />. If the home–commute contrast differs from state to state, two home workers in the same state share more of their error than a home worker and a commuter do, so errors are not equally correlated and the factor understates the inflation; with clusters as large as states, the size term turns a modest departure into a large miss.

</Details>

<Callout kind="box" title="Provenance: the divorce-law regressions">

The lead example in Solon, Haider, and Wooldridge is a panel of state divorce rates from 1956 to 1988, used by <Cite id="friedberg1998" narrative /> and by <Cite id="wolfers2006" narrative /> to estimate the effect of unilateral divorce laws and re-estimated by <Cite id="leesolon2011" narrative />. Friedberg and Wolfers weighted each state-year by population to correct heteroskedasticity. In the log specification of their Table 1, the robust standard errors for OLS over the first eight years after reform are about half those for WLS: weighting by population made the estimates less precise, which is Dickens's point. In levels, OLS and WLS differed sharply, and WLS without California moved toward OLS, which the authors read as a sign of heterogeneous effects rather than a verdict for either estimator. This box is provenance only: it reproduces none of those estimates, and the pattern has been checked against the working-paper version of the article, not the published tables.

</Callout>

## In code

The whole chapter is one weighted least-squares solve, repeated. The replicate standard error re-solves it once per replicate weight and combines the deviations with the SDR constant:

```python
import numpy as np

def wls(X, y, w):
    Xw = X * w[:, None]
    return np.linalg.solve(Xw.T @ X, Xw.T @ y)

beta = wls(X, y, perwt)                          # X: intercept, D, age, age^2, female, education
reps = np.array([wls(X, y, repwt[:, r]) for r in range(80)])
se = np.sqrt(4 / 80 * ((reps - beta) ** 2).sum(axis=0))   # t with 79 df
```

The OLS column of every figure is the same call with `w` equal to one. The DuMouchel–Duncan test is an OLS fit with `X`, the normalized weight, and their products as regressors, followed by a joint test of the product terms with household-clustered standard errors. The gap test of Figure 4.4 is the same loop run twice per replicate, once with `repwt[:, r]` and once with `repwt[:, r] / perwt` applied to equal case weights, and the SDR constant applied to the replicate differences.
