Among full-time wage and salary workers aged 25 to 64 in the 2024 American Community Survey (ACS), people who usually worked from home in the survey's reference week earned more per hour than people who commuted: <Fact id="ch4.raw_wls" /> log points more in the survey-weighted data. Home workers are also far more likely to hold a degree, so adjust for age, sex, and education. The adjusted coefficient is <Fact id="ch4.adj_ols" /> log points without survey weights and <Fact id="ch4.adj_wls" /> with them. Add fixed effects for detailed occupation, industry, and state, and the weighted coefficient falls to <Fact id="ch4.occind_wls" />. Here the choice of comparison moves the answer far more than the choice of survey weights.

Every number in this chapter describes who earns what; none says what working from home does to pay. The hourly wages are constructed from annual wage income, usual weekly hours, and a band of weeks worked, not reported directly.

Then comes the question every referee asks: did you use the weights? The two adjusted coefficients differ by <Fact id="ch4.adj_gap_abs" /> log points. Two conclusions are tempting. The first is that weighting does not matter for this question. The second is that, because the two estimates agree, either one is the average home–commute wage contrast in the population. The first is close to right, for reasons <Cite id="solon2015" narrative /> set out. The second is wrong, and [Chapter 5](/ch05-hidden-weights) measures by how much in a finer version of the same model.

## The estimand comes first

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

The ACS samples some places at higher rates than others and reaches some people more easily than others; the person weight is built to undo both. If you run the regression below with that weight, what population quantity do you get: the average home–commute wage gap, or something else?

</Callout>

The target is a descriptive adjusted contrast. In the 2024 population of full-time, full-year wage and salary workers, how much higher is the log hourly wage of people who work from home than that of commuters of the same age, sex, and education? The chapter summarizes that contrast with the population linear projection

$$
y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + X_i'\gamma + \varepsilon_i ,
$$

where $$y_i$$ is the log hourly wage of person $$i$$, $$D_i = 1$$ if the person works from home (WFH), and $$X_i$$ holds age, age squared, a female indicator, and four education levels. The coefficient $$\beta$$ is a well-defined population quantity whether or not the equation is a true model of wages: it is the coefficient on $$D_i$$ in the population's best linear predictor of $$y_i$$. That answers the prediction. The weighted regression estimates the population projection coefficient, which is not, in general, the population's average home–commute contrast. Nor is it the causal effect of working from home: workers and employers decide who works from home, and jobs that allow it differ from jobs that do not in ways the survey does not record.

The sample is the <Fact id="ch4.n_sample" /> ACS person records of wage and salary workers aged 25 to 64 who usually worked at least 30 hours a week, worked at least 40 weeks in the past 12 months, had positive wage income, and were at work in the survey's reference week; <Fact id="ch4.n_wfh" /> of them worked from home. "Full-time" here means 30 or more usual hours, a looser line than the common 35. Weighted by the person weight `PERWT` ($$w_i$$ in this book's notation), they stand for about <Fact id="ch4.pop_millions" /> million people. Hourly wage is annual wage and salary income divided by usual weekly hours times the midpoint of the weeks-worked interval, because the ACS asks about weeks only in bands. Wage income is top-coded by state, and <Fact id="ch4.topcode_share" /> of records sit at their state's cap.

## What "works from home" means in the ACS

The indicator $$D_i$$ is `TRANWORK = 80`, "Worked at home," from the ACS question about how the person usually got to work last week. `TRANWORK` records the *primary* means of transportation: the one used on most days, or for most of the distance. A hybrid worker who spends two days at home and three at the office is coded by the commute. Working from home in this chapter therefore means usually working at home in the reference week, not teleworking at all: <Fact id="ch4.prev_w" ci /> of the target population, and <Fact id="ch4.prev_unw" /> of the unweighted records.

The Current Population Survey (CPS) asks something else. Its telework item, `TELWRKPAY`, records whether a person teleworked or worked at home for pay at any time last week, so a hybrid worker counts as a teleworker. The two surveys measure different things and so estimate different contrasts, and no reweighting of either survey reconciles them: weights change who is represented, not what was measured. That is [Chapter 3](/ch03-design#interlude-weighting-repairs-representation-not-measurement)'s measurement lesson in a new setting. The CPS item is not in this book's data, so this chapter makes no numerical comparison.

## Why the weights barely matter here

Solon, Haider, and Wooldridge give three reasons to weight a regression aimed at a causal or structural parameter. The first is **precision**. If the error variance differs across observations in a known way, weighting each observation by the inverse of its variance, a precision weight $$a_i$$, gives a more efficient estimator. The textbook case is group averages: a state mean built from many respondents is less noisy than one built from a few, so weight by group size. The second is **consistency**. If the chance of entering the sample depends on the outcome even after conditioning on the regressors, sampling is *endogenous*, and unweighted estimates of the population relationship are inconsistent; weighting by inverse selection probabilities repairs them <Cite id="wooldridge1999" />. If sampling depends only on the regressors, it is *exogenous*, and both estimators are consistent for a correctly specified model, with the unweighted one usually more precise.

The precision motive has a sharp limit, identified by <Cite id="dickens1990" narrative />. When group averages share a group-level error component, such as a state-wide shock, the variance of a group mean stops shrinking in proportion to group size, and weighting by size can make estimates less precise, not more. Neither the motive nor the critique concerns `PERWT`, which is not a precision weight. It is a survey weight $$w_i$$: an inverse selection probability, adjusted for nonresponse and calibrated to Census population estimates <Cite id="census_acs_dm" />. Used as a regression weight where it is not needed for consistency, it costs precision. The coefficient of variation of `PERWT` in this sample is <Fact id="ch4.cv_w" />, so Kish's effective sample for a simple mean, $$\nKish/n$$, is <Fact id="ch4.kish_ratio" /> of the records <Cite id="kish1965" />, and the heteroskedasticity-robust variance of the WLS coefficient is <Fact id="ch4.weighting_var_ratio" /> times that of OLS.

That leaves consistency. The ACS selects addresses at rates that vary by geography, and its weights adjust for nonresponse and calibrate to population totals by age, sex, race, and Hispanic origin. If, given working from home, age, sex, and education, the chance of being sampled and responding is unrelated to wages, sampling is exogenous; with a correctly specified model the sample projection and the population projection then coincide, and OLS and WLS agree. The ladder says they nearly do. Agreement has a second explanation: the model is misspecified, because the contrast varies across groups, but the groups the weights push up or down have similar contrasts. The third motive in Solon, Haider, and Wooldridge, recovering a population average effect when effects are heterogeneous, starts from that reading. They show that weighting alone does not deliver one, and [Chapter 5](/ch05-hidden-weights) measures what the regression reports instead.

## What agreement tells you, and what it does not

This chapter's result is a negative one: at every rung of the ladder, weighting barely moves the coefficient, and the controls move it far more. The agreement is still informative. It is what exogenous sampling predicts: if, given the regressors, the ACS's sampling rates, nonresponse adjustments, and calibration are unrelated to wages, the sample projection matches the population projection and either estimator reports it. Heterogeneous contrasts that the weights happen not to reshuffle would produce the same agreement. Either way, the choice of weights is not what moves this coefficient. That is worth checking before any weighted regression is published, and the check costs one extra line of code.

Agreement does not show that either coefficient is the population's average contrast. Both are weighted averages of heterogeneous comparisons, and the regression chooses those weights whether or not the records are weighted. Among workers with at least a bachelor's degree, <Fact id="ch4.prev_ba" /> work from home; among workers without a high school diploma, <Fact id="ch4.prev_lths" />. A regression leans on groups where both home workers and commuters are plentiful, whatever their share of the population. `PERWT` fixes whom the sample represents. It does not fix how the regression weighs one group's contrast against another's, which is the question [Chapter 5](/ch05-hidden-weights) takes up with the same sample and a finer version of this model.

### When weighting would matter more

The agreement is a finding about this coefficient in this sample, not a rule. Three conditions make weighting matter more.

- **Inclusion related to the outcome.** If the chance of being sampled or of responding depends on wages even after conditioning on the regressors, the unweighted fit is inconsistent for the population projection, and the weights do real work <Cite id="wooldridge1999" />.
- **Composition that interacts with heterogeneous contrasts.** If the contrast differs across groups and the sample's mix of groups differs from the population's, the two estimators average the same contrasts with different weights, and the gap grows with both the heterogeneity and the difference in mix.
- **Influential records.** A few records with very large weights or high leverage can move a weighted coefficient that the unweighted fit barely notices.

The influence check below finds no single dominant group here, and the small gap fits the second condition in a mild form. Whether inclusion depends on wages cannot be checked from the sample alone.

## Testing the difference: DuMouchel and Duncan

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

The weighted and unweighted coefficients differ by <Fact id="ch4.adj_gap_abs" /> log points. With <Fact id="ch4.n_sample" /> records, will a formal test say that they differ?

</Callout>

<Cite id="dumouchel1983" narrative /> turned the comparison into a test. Regress the outcome by OLS on the covariates, the weight, and the weight interacted with each covariate, and test whether the weight terms are jointly zero:

$$
y_i = X_i'b + (\tilde w_i X_i)'c + u_i, \qquad H_0 : c = 0 ,
$$

where $$\tilde w_i = w_i/\bar w$$ is `PERWT` divided by its mean and $$X_i$$ includes the intercept and $$D_i$$. The null is the restriction under which the weighted and unweighted estimators share a probability limit. Here the test rejects: F = <Fact id="ch4.dd_f" /> on <Fact id="ch4.dd_q" /> restrictions with household-clustered standard errors, p <Fact id="ch4.dd_p" />. The joint test covers every weight term, including those on age, sex, and education, whose coefficients this chapter does not interpret. The term that bears on the work-from-home coefficient is the weight's interaction with $$D_i$$, with t = <Fact id="ch4.dd_t_wd" />: workers who carry larger weights show smaller home–commute contrasts. The levels model rejects as well (F = <Fact id="ch4.dd_lev_f" />).

A rejection is a diagnostic, not a verdict <Cite id="bollen2016" />. With close to a million records the test has the power to detect departures far too small to matter: the two work-from-home coefficients differ by <Fact id="ch4.adj_gap_abs" /> log points, <Fact id="ch4.log_gap_rel" /> of the unweighted coefficient and about <Fact id="ch4.gap_rel_se" /> standard errors of the weighted one. The rejection is evidence against the restriction that would give the two estimators a common probability limit. It does not say which number is wanted or why they differ, and at least four stories fit. The model may be *misspecified*: if the contrast varies across groups and the weights shift the mix of groups, the two estimators average the same heterogeneous contrasts differently. Sampling may be *endogenous*. A few heavily weighted records may have *leverage*. Or the estimators may target *different estimands*, the sample's projection and the population's, both legitimate.

### Which groups move the gap

In the divorce-law example below, one state mattered. If a single state, occupation, or education group accounted for the gap here, dropping it would close the gap. Figure 4.3 drops each in turn and re-estimates both regressions. The weighted coefficient is the smaller one, so the gap, WLS minus OLS, is negative. The largest single move is <Fact id="ch4.infl_max_change" /> log points, and it widens the gap: without <Fact id="ch4.infl_educ" /> workers, the gap becomes <Fact id="ch4.infl_educ_gap" />. ## The standard error: which repetition?

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Figure 4.4 lists five standard errors for the same coefficient. Which do you expect to be largest, and by roughly how much?

</Callout>

Agreement of point estimates says nothing about uncertainty. A standard error describes a repetition, and the five in Figure 4.4 describe different ones.

<Figure n="4.4" caption="The robust, household-clustered, and replicate standard errors nearly coincide; the state-clustered one is several times larger because it imagines a repetition the ACS never performs.">
  <Chart id="ch4.se_table" />
</Figure>

In Figure 4.4 the classical WLS standard error (<Fact id="ch4.se_wls_iid" />) treats `PERWT` as a precision weight and is too small. The heteroskedasticity-robust (<Fact id="ch4.se_wls_hc1" />) and household-clustered (<Fact id="ch4.se_wls_crhh" />) standard errors are larger. The design-based answer uses the ACS's 80 replicate weights, built by successive difference replication (SDR). Re-solve the weighted regression once with each replicate weight $$w_i^{(r)}$$ and combine the deviations as $$\Var(\hat\beta) = \tfrac{4}{80}\sum_{r=1}^{80}(\hat\beta_r - \hat\beta)^2$$ with 79 degrees of freedom:

```python
import numpy as np

def wls(X, y, w):
    Xw = X * w[:, None]
    return np.linalg.solve(Xw.T @ X, Xw.T @ y)

beta = wls(X, y, perwt)                          # X: intercept, D, age, age^2, female, education
reps = np.array([wls(X, y, repwt[:, r]) for r in range(80)])
se = np.sqrt(4 / 80 * ((reps - beta) ** 2).sum(axis=0))   # t with 79 df
```

The Census Bureau built each replicate weight by rerunning its weighting on perturbed base weights, so the SDR standard error reflects the sample design and the weight construction together. It is <Fact id="ch4.se_wls_sdr" />, <Fact id="ch4.se_ratio_sdr_hh" /> times the household-clustered value: the ACS samples addresses, so households (<Fact id="ch4.n_households" /> of them here) are its only clusters, and stratification and calibration trim a little off the variance. Applying the same replicate perturbations to equal case weights gives an approximate design-based standard error for OLS, and the WLS variance is <Fact id="ch4.design_var_ratio" /> times as large: under this design, as under the robust formulas, the unweighted estimator is the more precise one. Clustering by state (<Fact id="ch4.n_states" /> clusters, counting the District of Columbia) gives <Fact id="ch4.se_wls_crstate" />, <Fact id="ch4.se_ratio_state_sdr" /> times the replicate standard error. The arithmetic is right; the question is different. It imagines a repetition that redraws whole states, and neither the ACS sample nor anything in this descriptive target is drawn or assigned by state. [Chapter 6](/ch06-clustering#clustering-is-a-question-about-the-design) sets out when such a repetition is the relevant one.

<Details summary="The Moulton factor when the regressor varies within clusters">

[Chapter 3](/ch03-design#the-moulton-bridge) used the factor of <Cite id="moulton1990" narrative /> for a regressor that is constant within clusters. When the regressor varies within clusters and cluster sizes $$m$$ vary, the factor becomes

$$
1 + \Big[\tfrac{\Var(m)}{\bar m} + \bar m - 1\Big]\rho_x\,\rho_u
$$

where $$\bar m$$ is the mean cluster size and $$\rho_x$$ and $$\rho_u$$ are the within-cluster correlations of the regressor and the error. It approximates how much clustering inflates the variance of an OLS coefficient relative to the classical formula. It is a special-case regression analogue of the clustering component of a design effect, not the design effect itself ([Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations#the-moulton-factor-as-a-special-case)): it prices clustering only, not unequal weights or stratification, and it assumes that every pair of errors in a cluster is equally correlated. The observed inflation is the ratio of the cluster-robust to the classical variance of the unweighted coefficient. For households the factor predicts <Fact id="ch4.moulton_hh" /> against an observed <Fact id="ch4.moulton_hh_emp" />, close for a special case. For states it predicts <Fact id="ch4.moulton_state" /> against an observed <Fact id="ch4.moulton_state_emp" />. If the home–commute contrast differs from state to state, two home workers in the same state share more of their error than a home worker and a commuter do, so errors are not equally correlated and the factor understates the inflation. With clusters as large as states, the size term multiplies two small correlations by a very large number, so a modest departure from the assumption becomes a large miss.

</Details>

<Callout kind="box" title="Provenance: the divorce-law regressions">

The lead example in Solon, Haider, and Wooldridge is a panel of state divorce rates from 1956 to 1988, used by <Cite id="friedberg1998" narrative /> and by <Cite id="wolfers2006" narrative /> to estimate the effect of unilateral divorce laws and re-estimated by <Cite id="leesolon2011" narrative />. Friedberg and Wolfers weighted each state-year by population to correct heteroskedasticity. In the log specification of their Table 1, the robust standard errors for OLS over the first eight years after reform are about half those for WLS: weighting by population made the estimates less precise, which is Dickens's point. In levels, OLS and WLS differed sharply, and WLS without California moved toward OLS, which the authors read as a sign of heterogeneous effects rather than a verdict for either estimator. This box is provenance only: it reproduces none of those estimates, and the pattern has been checked against the working-paper version of the article, not the published tables.
