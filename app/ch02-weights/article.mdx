How does one phone-survey respondent become thousands of adults? In 2024 the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), the state-based telephone survey run by CDC and state health departments, released <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_file" /> interviews. Each record carries a final weight, `_LLCPWT`: the number of adults that respondent stands for. The median respondent stands for <Fact id="ch2.brfss_weight_median" /> adults. The average California respondent stands for <Fact id="ch2.brfss_mean_w_ca" />, the average Vermont respondent for <Fact id="ch2.brfss_mean_w_vt" />: California supplied <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_ca" /> interviews and Vermont <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_vt" />, for adult populations of very different size. The largest weight in the file is <Fact id="ch2.brfss_weight_max" />.

This chapter follows one number through the steps that turn a telephone interview into a weight. The number moves at every step. Some moves repair known differences in the chance of being sampled; one is an artifact of scale; the largest encodes a modeling decision about which population totals the sample must reproduce.

## One survey, two prevalences

Frequent mental distress (FMD) means reporting 14 or more of the past 30 days on which one's mental health was not good. BRFSS asks it as a single question, `MENTHLTH`, coded as a count of days, 88 for none, and 77 or 99 for don't know or refused. Of the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_fmd" /> respondents in this chapter's target population who gave a count, <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_unweighted" /> reported 14 or more days. Most analysts' first line of code returns exactly that share:

```python
brfss.filter(pl.col("fmd").is_not_null())["fmd"].mean()
```

The line runs, and its result reads like a prevalence. It is the share of *respondents*, and respondents are not a sample in which every adult had the same chance of appearing. With the final weight the estimate is <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_final" ci />, <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_gap_pp" /> percentage points higher.

<KeyNumber id="ch2.brfss_fmd_final" label="Frequent mental distress, adults in the 49 states and DC that released 2024 BRFSS data" />

"Use the weight" settles which number to report. It does not say what the weight did. `_LLCPWT` is the last link in a chain whose intermediate weights and factors CDC ships on the public file, and the estimate can be computed at each link.

### Who the number is about

A prevalence needs a population, and this one is not the United States. The 2024 public file has no interviews from Tennessee, which could not collect enough data to meet CDC's minimum for release <Cite id="brfss2024" />. It does include Guam, Puerto Rico, and the US Virgin Islands, which draw simple random samples of landline numbers rather than the stratified samples the states use. The target here is **adults in the 49 states and the District of Columbia that released 2024 data**. The <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_terr" /> territorial interviews are set aside, leaving <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_target" /> respondents whose final weights add up to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_weight_total" /> adults. The <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_missing_share" /> who answered don't know or refused are excluded from the outcome, so the estimand is prevalence among adults who would answer.

The missing state is not a rounding error. In 2022, the most recent year in which all 50 states and DC released data, Tennessee's respondents represented <Fact id="ch2.tn_2022_adults" /> adults, <Fact id="ch2.tn_2022_share" /> of the weighted total. The set of jurisdictions also changes from year to year, so a trend or pooled estimate labeled "U.S. adults" describes a population that shifts beneath it (Figure 2.1).

## A design weight you can see

First, a survey whose weight can be traced by hand. The American Time Use Survey (ATUS) asks each respondent about one day, assigned in advance and never substituted. BLS assigns a share $$0.10$$ of the sample to each weekday and $$0.25$$ to each weekend day, so weekdays and weekends each receive half <Cite id="bls2026" />. People work much less on weekends.

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">
Weekend days are about half of the ATUS diaries but two of every seven days in a year. Is the unweighted mean of minutes worked per day too high or too low, and by roughly how much?
</Callout>

Of the <Fact id="ch2.atus_n" /> diaries in 2023, <Fact id="ch2.atus_weekend_sample_share" /> describe a Saturday or Sunday. Respondents worked <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_weekday" /> minutes on the average weekday diary and <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_weekend" /> on the average weekend diary, so the unweighted mean of <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_unweighted" /> minutes leans toward the weekend.

The repair is the oldest idea in survey weighting: weight each unit by the inverse of its probability of selection <Cite id="horvitz1952" />.

$$
d_i = \frac{1}{\pi_i}, \qquad \hat{\bar y} = \frac{\sum_i d_i\, y_i}{\sum_i d_i}.
$$

A weekday diary has $$\pi_i \propto 0.10$$ and gets $$d_i \propto 10$$; a weekend diary has $$\pi_i \propto 0.25$$ and gets $$d_i \propto 4$$. The ratio on the right, the Hájek estimator, needs only relative weights. With $$d_i$$ alone, weekends shrink to <Fact id="ch2.atus_weekend_share_d" /> of person-days and the mean rises to <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_design" /> minutes (Figure 2.2).

<Figure n="2.2" caption="Weekend diaries are half the ATUS sample; the design weight shrinks them to about two-sevenths of person-days.">
<Chart id="ch2.atus_day_weights" />
</Figure>

The published weight, `WT06`, gives <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_weighted" /> minutes, which matches the published BLS figure to rounding <Cite id="bls_atus" />. It still carries the day allocation: the average weekday diary has <Fact id="ch2.atus_wt_ratio" /> times the weight of the average weekend diary, near the design ratio of $$10/4$$, and weekends get <Fact id="ch2.atus_weekend_share_wt06" /> of weighted person-days. The remaining distance comes from the rest of the weight: other design factors (ATUS oversamples some demographic groups), a nonresponse adjustment, and calibration to population totals. The book writes a shipped weight as

$$
w_i = d_i\, g_i,
$$

with $$d_i$$ the design weight and $$g_i$$ the product of the adjustments. The day-of-week part of $$d_i$$ is documented exactly; $$g_i$$ is not something a user can rebuild.

<Ledger id="ch2.atus_design_weight" />

This ATUS extract has no design variables, so it carries an estimand lesson and no uncertainty lesson: a standard error computed from `WT06` alone would understate the uncertainty.

## Five links in a chain

BRFSS ships the intermediate weights of its chain on the public file, and CDC's 2024 Overview documents how each is made <Cite id="brfss2024" />. Every definition below was checked against the file.

1. **Stratum weight, `_STRWT`.** States sample telephone numbers within strata defined by geography and, for landlines, by the density of listed household numbers. `_STRWT` is the count of numbers in the stratum divided by the count selected: the inverse of the stratum's sampling fraction.
2. **Adults and phones, `_WT2RAKE`.** A landline call reaches a household, and the interviewer picks one adult at random, so an adult's chance of selection falls with the number of adults $$A_i$$ in the household and rises with its number of residential landline numbers $$k_i$$. The factor applies to the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_landline" /> landline respondents. A cell phone reaches a person, so the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_cell" /> cell-frame respondents get a factor of one.
3. **Dual-frame factor, `_DUALCOR`.** Since 2014 every adult reached on a cell phone is eligible, so an adult with both kinds of phone could have been sampled from either frame. These dual users, <Fact id="ch2.brfss_dual_share" /> of respondents, get a compositing factor $$\lambda_s$$ if reached by landline and $$1-\lambda_s$$ if reached by cell. The factor is set by state and ranges from <Fact id="ch2.brfss_lambda_min" /> to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_lambda_max" /> for landline-frame dual users.
4. **Raking input, `_LLCPWT2`.** The composite design weight is truncated within geographic regions at the mean plus or minus $$1.96$$ standard deviations.
5. **Final weight, `_LLCPWT`.** Raking, or iterative proportional fitting, adjusts the weights until they reproduce population totals on eight margins in every state: sex by age, race and ethnicity, education, marital status, home ownership, sex by race, age by race, and telephone ownership. States with sub-state regions or large counties add regional and county margins. The totals come from Claritas population estimates, the 2019–2023 American Community Survey, and NCHS estimates of wireless-only households.

The first three links multiply into the design weight

$$
d_i = \texttt{\_STRWT}_{h(i)} \times \frac{\min(A_i,\,5)}{\min(k_i,\,3)} \times c_i,
\qquad
c_i = \begin{cases} \lambda_s & \text{dual user reached by landline} \\ 1-\lambda_s & \text{dual user reached by cell} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
$$

with $$A_i = k_i = 1$$ on the cell frame.

### What moved the estimate

The path is not monotone (Figure 2.3). The stratum weight raises the estimate to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_strwt" />; the adults-and-phones factor lowers it to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_wt2rake" />; the dual-frame factor raises it to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_composite" />; the raking input lowers it to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_llcpwt2" />; and raking lifts it to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_final" />.

Part of the early movement is geography. BRFSS gives every state a sample large enough for state estimates, so small states are heavily oversampled. Weighting respondents equally within each state but holding every state at its final weighted total already moves the unweighted figure to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_statenorm" />.

The design stages are not estimates of anything on their own. Summed over respondents, `_STRWT` totals <Fact id="ch2.brfss_strwt_total" />: telephone numbers thinned by nonresponse, not adults. At that scale the dual-frame factor cuts the weighted share of dual users from <Fact id="ch2.share_dual_wt2rake" /> to <Fact id="ch2.share_dual_composite" />. The raking input moves everything to the population scale. `_LLCPWT2` totals <Fact id="ch2.brfss_llcpwt2_total" />, its total in each state is between <Fact id="ch2.brfss_l2_ratio_lo" /> and <Fact id="ch2.brfss_l2_ratio_hi" /> times the final weight's, and the dual-user share is back at <Fact id="ch2.share_dual_llcpwt2" />, essentially its final value. The Overview describes truncation at this step; the rescaling is visible only in the data.

Raking is the largest move and the one that reallocates people (Figure 2.4). Adults aged 18–24 are <Fact id="ch2.share_age1824_unw" /> of respondents and <Fact id="ch2.share_age1824_final" /> of weighted adults; adults 65 and older fall from <Fact id="ch2.share_age65_unw" /> to <Fact id="ch2.share_age65_final" />. College graduates fall from <Fact id="ch2.share_college_unw" /> to <Fact id="ch2.share_college_final" />, and adults without a high school diploma rise from <Fact id="ch2.share_lths_unw" /> to <Fact id="ch2.share_lths_final" />; the design weights barely touch education. Cell-only adults rise from <Fact id="ch2.share_cellonly_unw" /> to <Fact id="ch2.share_cellonly_final" />. Under the final weight, FMD is <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_age1824" /> among adults 18–24 and <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_age65" /> among adults 65 and older, so shifting weight toward the young raises the overall estimate. The weights also spread as they move down the chain: their coefficient of variation is <Fact id="ch2.brfss_cv_strwt" /> for the stratum weight, <Fact id="ch2.brfss_cv_llcpwt2" /> for the raking input, and <Fact id="ch2.brfss_cv_final" /> after raking.

### What can be rebuilt

The chain has three kinds of link (Figure 2.5), and the difference matters to anyone who wants to modify a weight.

- **Rebuilt exactly from public variables.** `_RAWRAKE`, the adults-and-phones factor, equals $$\min(A_i,5)/\min(k_i,3)$$ for every landline respondent with a known count of numbers; the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_ll_unknown_phones" /> with an unknown count are treated as having one. `_WT2RAKE` equals `_STRWT` times `_RAWRAKE` on all but <Fact id="ch2.brfss_wt2rake_exceptions" /> records, all Illinois cell interviews that carry another stratum's weight. The two compositing factors sum to one in every state. `_STRWT` itself is shipped but not derivable: the counts behind it are not public.
- **Documented, not rebuildable.** `_LLCPWT2` depends on sub-state regions, which are removed from the public file, and on a rescaling the Overview does not describe. `_LLCPWT` depends on control totals and regional and county margins that are not on the file.
- **Illustrative only.** Raking `_LLCPWT2` to the final weight's own state totals on the eight public margins gives <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_rerake_recon" />, against <Fact id="ch2.trim_none_fmd" /> for the published weight, yet the rebuilt weights differ from the published ones by a median of <Fact id="ch2.brfss_recon_median_dev" />. The visible margins reproduce nearly all of the raking step's effect on this estimate, but not the weights themselves.

### How uncertain the estimate is

BRFSS declares strata (`_STSTR`) and a primary sampling unit (`_PSU`), but the PSU is the respondent's sequence number: each respondent is its own PSU. The design is stratified and single-stage. The target population has <Fact id="ch2.brfss_strata" /> strata, and the FMD estimate has <Fact id="ch2.brfss_df" /> design degrees of freedom: respondents with a valid answer minus strata. The full national file in [Chapter 7](/ch07-field-guide#brfss-2024), territories included, has more. Of the strata, <Fact id="ch2.brfss_singletons" /> hold a single respondent, where a variance formula that needs two units per stratum has nothing to work with. R's `survey` package stops on them by default, which is the right default. This chapter centers each lone unit at the grand mean, the rule in CDC's own R example; removing them instead changes the standard error only in its fifth significant digit.

```python
import svy
design = svy.Design(stratum="xststr", psu="psu_key", wgt="xllcpwt")  # psu_key = stratum + _PSU
s = svy.Sample(brfss, design=design).singleton.handle("center")
s.estimation.mean("fmd0", where=svy.col("valid") == 1, deff="wr")
```

```r
options(survey.lonely.psu = "adjust")
des <- svydesign(ids = ~1, strata = ~xststr, weights = ~xllcpwt, data = brfss)
svymean(~fmd, des, na.rm = TRUE, deff = "replace")
```

Both give a standard error of <Fact id="ch2.se_as_sampling" /> percentage points and agree to at least six significant digits. The weights help define the estimator; the sampling design *and the construction of the weights* determine how it would vary over repeated samples. Taylor linearization with fixed weights captures the first and ignores the second: raking is itself estimated from the sample <Cite id="lumley2010" />.

## Weights that are not inverse probabilities

Only the first three links of the BRFSS chain have a probability interpretation. The rescaling in `_LLCPWT2` and the raking in `_LLCPWT` make the sample reproduce known population totals, and they rest on a model of nonresponse: within the raked categories, respondents resemble nonrespondents. <Cite id="gelman2007" narrative /> opens by calling survey weighting "a mess." Weights in practice, he stresses, are generally not inverse probabilities of selection: they typically combine selection probabilities with adjustments for nonresponse. He adds that using weights beyond a simple mean or ratio is not straightforward, and that even a weighted mean's standard error is hard to get right. The alternative he explores is to model survey responses given every variable that affects inclusion and nonresponse and then poststratify, which he concedes quickly becomes complicated.

The word "weight" also names three different objects in statistical software.

- A **frequency weight** $$f_i$$ says the record stands for $$f_i$$ identical observations; the sample size is $$\sum_i f_i$$.
- A **precision (analytic) weight** $$a_i$$ says observation $$i$$ has variance $$\sigma^2/a_i$$, as when each record is a mean over $$a_i$$ people.
- A **sampling weight** $$w_i = d_i\, g_i$$ says the record represents $$w_i$$ population units, with a variance that depends on the design that produced it.

All three give the same weighted mean and different standard errors. Read as a frequency weight, `_LLCPWT` treats every weighted adult as interviewed and gives a standard error of <Fact id="ch2.se_as_frequency" /> percentage points for FMD; read as a precision weight, <Fact id="ch2.se_as_analytic" />; read as a sampling weight with the declared design, <Fact id="ch2.se_as_sampling" />. Stata's `fweight`, `aweight`, and `pweight`, and the `weights` argument of R's `lm` (a precision weight), each commit to one reading whether or not the user meant it.

<Callout kind="box" title="Two effective sample sizes for one BRFSS estimate">

[Chapter 0](/ch00-start-here) separates four objects called "effective sample size." Two of them can be computed here, and they answer different questions.

**The Kish effective sample size**, $$\nKish = n/(1+\CV(w)^2) = (\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$$, prices weight dispersion alone <Cite id="kish1965" />: the size of a simple random sample that would give a weighted mean the same variance *if* the outcome were unrelated to the weights and the design had no strata or clusters. The final BRFSS weight has a coefficient of variation of <Fact id="ch2.kish_brfss_cv" />, so $$\nKish/n$$ is <Fact id="ch2.kish_brfss_ratio" />, against <Fact id="ch2.kish_atus_ratio" /> for ATUS and <Fact id="ch2.kish_asec_ratio" /> for the CPS ASEC (Figure 2.6). Much of the loss is the state allocation: within the median state, $$\nKish/n$$ is <Fact id="ch2.brfss_kish_ratio_state_median" />. A national estimate pays for sample sizes chosen for state estimates.

## Trimming: precision bought with bias

Large weights make estimates noisy, so producers trim them, as CDC does to the design weight before raking. Analysts are sometimes tempted to trim the final weight themselves. Figure 2.7 caps `_LLCPWT` at each state's 99th or 95th percentile and then rescales all of the state's weights to restore the state's total, so state totals do not change.

<Figure n="2.7" caption="Capping the final weight shrinks the standard error modestly, moves the estimate, and undoes part of the raking; re-raking restores the margins and gives back some precision.">
<Chart id="ch2.trimming" />
</Figure>

The 95th-percentile cap cuts the coefficient of variation from <Fact id="ch2.brfss_cv_final" /> to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_cv" /> and raises $$\nKish$$ from <Fact id="ch2.brfss_nkish_final" /> to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_nkish" />, but the standard error falls only to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_se_ratio" /> of its untrimmed value: standard errors move with the square root of the effective sample size. The estimate falls by <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_shift_pp" /> percentage points to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_fmd" ci />, a shift of <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_shift_se" /> standard errors. The milder 99th-percentile cap gives <Fact id="ch2.trim_p99_fmd" />, with CV <Fact id="ch2.trim_p99_cv" /> and $$\nKish$$ <Fact id="ch2.trim_p99_nkish" />.

The shift is not noise. Within a state the largest weights tend to belong to groups that raking pushed up, so capping them undoes part of the raking: after the 95th-percentile cap, adults 18–24 fall to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_age1824" /> of weighted adults, below the <Fact id="ch2.share_age1824_final" /> the population totals require, while cell-only adults rise to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_cellonly" />. A trimmed weight that no longer matches the margins has traded part of a bias correction for lower variance, and the size of the bias it readmits is unknown. Re-raking the capped weights to the same margins restores them exactly and returns the estimate to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95r_fmd" />, with CV <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95r_cv" /> and $$\nKish$$ <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95r_nkish" />: part of the precision gain survives, part is given back. Producers iterate between trimming and raking for this reason <Cite id="valliant2018" />; trimming a final weight without re-raking changes the population the estimate describes.

## Back to the puzzle

The unweighted <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_unweighted" /> is a fact about who answered the telephone. The weighted <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_final" /> is an estimate for adults in the 49 states and DC that released 2024 data, and the gap between the two is the sum of five moves with different signs. The design weights repair unequal chances of selection, which the survey controls and records. Raking, the largest adjustment, repairs unequal chances of responding, which are unknown and are modeled by assuming that respondents resemble nonrespondents within the raked categories. The estimate is as good as that assumption.
