Among <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_fmd" /> respondents to the 2024 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) who gave a valid count of poor mental health days, <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_unweighted" /> reported frequent mental distress: 14 or more of the past 30 days on which their mental health was not good. Apply the final survey weight to the same answers and the estimate is <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_final" ci />. No answer changed. How much each answer counted did.

Frequent mental distress (FMD) is a self-reported count of days, not a clinical diagnosis. The respondents are adults in the 49 states and the District of Columbia that released 2024 data, and because only valid answers enter, the estimate describes prevalence among adults who would answer the question. BRFSS is the state-based telephone survey run by CDC and state health departments, and each record carries a final weight, `_LLCPWT`: the number of adults that respondent stands for. Where the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_gap_pp" /> percentage points between the two estimates come from is a question about how that weight was built.

## One survey, two prevalences

Most analysts' first line of code returns the unweighted share:

```python
brfss.filter(pl.col("fmd").is_not_null())["fmd"].mean()
```

The line runs, and its result reads like a prevalence. It is the share of *respondents*, and respondents are not a sample in which every adult had the same chance of appearing.

<KeyNumber id="ch2.brfss_fmd_final" label="Frequent mental distress, adults in the 49 states and DC that released 2024 BRFSS data" />

"Use the weight" settles which number to report. It does not say what the weight did. `_LLCPWT` is the last of five links, and CDC ships every intermediate weight on the public file, so the estimate can be computed at each link. The first three are design weights: they undo known differences in the chance of being sampled, arising from the telephone stratum, the number of adults and phones in the household, and the chance of being reachable on both a landline and a cell phone. The fourth truncates the design weight and puts it on the population scale. The fifth rakes the weights until they reproduce population totals by age, sex, race and ethnicity, education, and other characteristics, state by state.

## Five links in a chain

CDC's 2024 Overview documents how each link is made <Cite id="brfss2024" />, and every definition below was checked against the file.

1. **Stratum weight, `_STRWT`.** States sample telephone numbers within strata defined by geography and, for landlines, by the density of listed household numbers. `_STRWT` is the count of numbers in the stratum divided by the count selected: the inverse of the stratum's sampling fraction.
2. **Adults and phones, `_WT2RAKE`.** A landline call reaches a household, and the interviewer picks one adult at random, so an adult's chance of selection falls with the number of adults $$A_i$$ in the household and rises with its number of residential landline numbers $$k_i$$. The factor applies to the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_landline" /> landline respondents. A cell phone reaches a person, so the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_cell" /> cell-frame respondents get a factor of one.
3. **Dual-frame factor, `_DUALCOR`.** Since 2014 every adult reached on a cell phone is eligible, so an adult with both kinds of phone could have been sampled from either frame. These dual users, <Fact id="ch2.brfss_dual_share" /> of respondents, get a compositing factor $$\lambda_s$$ if reached by landline and $$1-\lambda_s$$ if reached by cell. The factor is set by state and ranges from <Fact id="ch2.brfss_lambda_min" /> to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_lambda_max" /> for landline-frame dual users.
4. **Raking input, `_LLCPWT2`.** The composite design weight is truncated within geographic regions at the mean plus or minus $$1.96$$ standard deviations and, as the data show, rescaled to the population scale state by state.
5. **Final weight, `_LLCPWT`.** Raking, or iterative proportional fitting, adjusts the weights until they reproduce population totals on eight margins in every state: sex by age, race and ethnicity, education, marital status, home ownership, sex by race, age by race, and telephone ownership. States with sub-state regions or large counties add regional and county margins. The totals come from Claritas population estimates, the 2019–2023 American Community Survey, and NCHS estimates of wireless-only households.

The first three links multiply into the design weight $$d_i$$, which reflects each adult's chance of selection, and the last two act as an adjustment $$g_i$$, so that the final weight is $$w_i = d_i g_i$$. The design weight is

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_i &= \texttt{\_STRWT}_{h(i)} \times \frac{\min(A_i,\,5)}{\min(k_i,\,3)} \times c_i, \\[4pt]
c_i &= \begin{cases} \lambda_s & \text{dual user reached by landline} \\ 1-\lambda_s & \text{dual user reached by cell} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

with $$A_i = k_i = 1$$ on the cell frame.

### What can be rebuilt

The chain has three kinds of link (Figure 2.4), and the difference matters to anyone who wants to modify a weight.

- **Rebuilt exactly from public variables.** `_RAWRAKE`, the adults-and-phones factor, equals $$\min(A_i,5)/\min(k_i,3)$$ for every landline respondent with a known count of numbers; the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_ll_unknown_phones" /> with an unknown count are treated as having one. `_WT2RAKE` equals `_STRWT` times `_RAWRAKE` on all but <Fact id="ch2.brfss_wt2rake_exceptions" /> records, all Illinois cell interviews that carry another stratum's weight. The two compositing factors sum to one in every state. `_STRWT` itself is shipped but not derivable: the counts behind it are not public.
- **Documented, not rebuildable.** `_LLCPWT2` depends on sub-state regions, which are removed from the public file, and on a rescaling the Overview does not describe. `_LLCPWT` depends on control totals and regional and county margins that are not on the file.
- **Illustrative only.** Raking `_LLCPWT2` to the final weight's own state totals on the eight public margins gives <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_rerake_recon" />, against <Fact id="ch2.trim_none_fmd" /> for the published weight, yet the rebuilt weights differ from the published ones by a median of <Fact id="ch2.brfss_recon_median_dev" />. The visible margins reproduce nearly all of the raking step's effect on this estimate, but not the weights themselves.

### How uncertain the estimate is

BRFSS declares strata (`_STSTR`) and a primary sampling unit (`_PSU`), but the PSU is the respondent's sequence number: each respondent is its own PSU. The design is stratified and single-stage. The target population has <Fact id="ch2.brfss_strata" /> strata, and the FMD estimate has <Fact id="ch2.brfss_df" /> design degrees of freedom: respondents with a valid answer minus strata. The full national file in [Chapter 7](/ch07-field-guide#brfss-2024), territories included, has more. Of the strata, <Fact id="ch2.brfss_singletons" /> hold a single respondent, where a variance formula that needs two units per stratum has nothing to work with. R's `survey` package stops on them by default, which is the right default. This chapter centers each lone unit at the grand mean, the rule in CDC's own R example; removing them instead changes the standard error only in its fifth significant digit.

```python
import svy
design = svy.Design(stratum="xststr", psu="psu_key", wgt="xllcpwt")  # psu_key = stratum + _PSU
s = svy.Sample(brfss, design=design).singleton.handle("center")
s.estimation.mean("fmd0", where=svy.col("valid") == 1, deff="wr")
```

```r
options(survey.lonely.psu = "adjust")
des <- svydesign(ids = ~1, strata = ~xststr, weights = ~xllcpwt, data = brfss)
svymean(~fmd, des, na.rm = TRUE, deff = "replace")
```

Both give a standard error of <Fact id="ch2.se_as_sampling" /> percentage points and agree to at least six significant digits. That standard error treats the final weights as fixed, although raking is itself estimated from the sample <Cite id="lumley2010" />. A calibration-aware linearization, or replicate weights that repeat the raking, would carry that part of the variance too. The public file carries no replicate weights, and not all of the raking margins or their control totals, so it supports neither directly.

## Inverse probabilities, and where they end

### A design weight you can see

Only the first three links of the BRFSS chain have a probability interpretation, and even they rest on counts the public cannot see. The American Time Use Survey (ATUS) shows a design weight in the open. It asks each respondent about one day, assigned in advance and never substituted. BLS assigns a share $$0.10$$ of the sample to each weekday and $$0.25$$ to each weekend day, so weekdays and weekends each receive half <Cite id="bls2026" />. People work much less on weekends.

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">
Weekend days are about half of the ATUS diaries but two of every seven days in a year. Is the unweighted mean of minutes worked per day too high or too low, and by roughly how much?
</Callout>

Of the <Fact id="ch2.atus_n" /> diaries in 2023, <Fact id="ch2.atus_weekend_sample_share" /> describe a Saturday or Sunday. Respondents worked <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_weekday" /> minutes on the average weekday diary and <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_weekend" /> on the average weekend diary, so the unweighted mean of <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_unweighted" /> minutes leans toward the weekend and is too low.

The repair is the oldest idea in survey weighting: weight each unit by the inverse of its probability of selection <Cite id="horvitz1952" />.

$$
d_i = \frac{1}{\pi_i}, \qquad \hat{\bar y} = \frac{\sum_i d_i\, y_i}{\sum_i d_i}.
$$

A weekday diary has $$\pi_i \propto 0.10$$ and gets $$d_i \propto 10$$; a weekend diary has $$\pi_i \propto 0.25$$ and gets $$d_i \propto 4$$. The ratio on the right, the Hájek estimator, needs only relative weights. With $$d_i$$ alone, weekends shrink to <Fact id="ch2.atus_weekend_share_d" /> of person-days and the mean rises to <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_design" /> minutes (Figure 2.5).

<Figure n="2.5" caption="Weekend diaries are half the ATUS sample; the design weight shrinks them to about two-sevenths of person-days.">
<Chart id="ch2.atus_day_weights" />
</Figure>

The published weight, `WT06`, gives <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_weighted" /> minutes, which matches the published BLS figure to rounding <Cite id="bls_atus" />. It still carries the day allocation: the average weekday diary has <Fact id="ch2.atus_wt_ratio" /> times the weight of the average weekend diary, near the design ratio of $$10/4$$, and weekends get <Fact id="ch2.atus_weekend_share_wt06" /> of weighted person-days. The remaining distance comes from the rest of the weight, the $$g_i$$ in $$w_i = d_i g_i$$: other design factors (ATUS oversamples some demographic groups), a nonresponse adjustment, and calibration to population totals. The day-of-week part of $$d_i$$ is documented exactly; $$g_i$$ is not something a user can rebuild.

<Ledger id="ch2.atus_design_weight" />

This ATUS extract has no design variables, so it carries an estimand lesson and no uncertainty lesson. A standard error computed from `WT06` alone would leave out the design, and nothing in the extract says whether it would come out too small or too large.

### Where the probability interpretation ends

The rescaling in `_LLCPWT2` and the raking in `_LLCPWT` make the sample reproduce known population totals, and they rest on a model of nonresponse: within the raked categories, respondents resemble nonrespondents. <Cite id="gelman2007" narrative /> opens by calling survey weighting "a mess." Weights in practice, he stresses, are generally not inverse probabilities of selection: they typically combine selection probabilities with adjustments for nonresponse. He adds that using weights beyond a simple mean or ratio is not straightforward, and that even a weighted mean's standard error is hard to get right. The alternative he explores is to model survey responses given every variable that affects inclusion and nonresponse and then poststratify, which he concedes quickly becomes complicated.

### What software calls a weight

The word "weight" also names three different objects in statistical software.

- A **frequency weight** $$f_i$$ says the record stands for $$f_i$$ identical observations; the sample size is $$\sum_i f_i$$.
- A **precision (analytic) weight** $$a_i$$ says observation $$i$$ has variance $$\sigma^2/a_i$$, as when each record is a mean over $$a_i$$ people.
- A **sampling weight** $$w_i = d_i\, g_i$$ says the record represents $$w_i$$ population units, with a variance that depends on the design that produced it.

All three give the same weighted mean and different standard errors. Read as a frequency weight, `_LLCPWT` treats every weighted adult as interviewed and gives a standard error of <Fact id="ch2.se_as_frequency" /> percentage points for FMD; read as a precision weight, <Fact id="ch2.se_as_analytic" />; read as a sampling weight with the declared design, <Fact id="ch2.se_as_sampling" />. Stata's `fweight`, `aweight`, and `pweight`, and the `weights` argument of R's `lm` (a precision weight), each commit to one reading whether or not the user meant it.

<Callout kind="box" title="Two effective sample sizes for one BRFSS estimate">

[Chapter 0](/ch00-start-here) separates four objects called "effective sample size." Two of them can be computed here, and they answer different questions.

## Trimming: precision bought with bias

Large weights make estimates noisy, so producers trim them, as CDC does to the design weight before raking. Analysts are sometimes tempted to trim the final weight themselves. Figure 2.7 caps `_LLCPWT` at each state's 99th or 95th percentile and then rescales all of the state's weights to restore the state's total, so state totals do not change.

<Figure n="2.7" caption="Capping the final weight shrinks the standard error modestly, moves the estimate, and undoes part of the raking; re-raking restores the margins and gives back some precision.">
<Chart id="ch2.trimming" />
</Figure>

The 95th-percentile cap cuts the coefficient of variation from <Fact id="ch2.brfss_cv_final" /> to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_cv" /> and raises $$\nKish$$ from <Fact id="ch2.brfss_nkish_final" /> to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_nkish" />, but the standard error falls only to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_se_ratio" /> of its untrimmed value: standard errors move with the square root of the effective sample size. The estimate falls by <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_shift_pp" /> percentage points to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_fmd" ci />, a shift of <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_shift_se" /> standard errors. The milder 99th-percentile cap gives <Fact id="ch2.trim_p99_fmd" />, with CV <Fact id="ch2.trim_p99_cv" /> and $$\nKish$$ <Fact id="ch2.trim_p99_nkish" />.

The shift has a visible compositional source: trimming changes which groups carry weight. Within a state the largest weights tend to belong to groups that raking pushed up, so capping them undoes part of the raking. After the 95th-percentile cap, adults 18–24 fall to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_age1824" /> of weighted adults, below the <Fact id="ch2.share_age1824_final" /> the population totals require, while cell-only adults rise to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_cellonly" />. A trimmed weight that no longer matches the margins has traded part of a bias correction for lower variance, and the size of the bias it readmits is unknown. Re-raking the capped weights to the same margins restores them exactly and returns the estimate to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95r_fmd" />, with CV <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95r_cv" /> and $$\nKish$$ <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95r_nkish" />: part of the precision gain survives, part is given back. Producers iterate between trimming and raking for this reason <Cite id="valliant2018" />; trimming a final weight without re-raking changes the population the estimate describes.

BRFSS truncates the design weight before raking; the widget trims after raking and then rakes again, the iteration described in the previous section. In this population the answer to the prediction is: away from the truth, on average. At the default cap, trimming raises $$\nKish$$ in nearly every sample and pulls the estimate down. The largest weights belong to the young adults and adults without a degree whom nonresponse made scarce, so capping them moves the estimate back toward the respondents' composition. In any one sample the trimmed estimate can land nearer the truth by chance. Re-raking restores the margins and, on average, returns the estimate to about where raking left it.

<Callout kind="note" title="A new case">

An analyst caps a survey's final weights and rescales them so that every state keeps its weighted total, but the capped weights no longer match the population's age distribution. What can the analyst still say about a state's weighted total, and what can no longer be assumed about an outcome that varies with age?

<Details summary="One answer">

State totals, and anything that depends only on them, are unchanged. The age mix is not: the capped weights describe a population with the wrong age distribution, so an outcome that varies with age, such as frequent mental distress, is pulled toward the respondents' age mix. How far depends on how much the outcome differs across the misrepresented groups, which the capped weights alone cannot reveal. Re-raking to the margins restores the age distribution, and comparing the capped and re-raked estimates shows how much the lost margins mattered for that outcome.

</Details>

</Callout>
