## What is this weight column?

You have decided to use the weights. Now the codebook lists six of them. The 2024 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) file carries `_STRWT`, `_RAWRAKE`, `_WT2RAKE`, `_DUALCOR`, `_LLCPWT2`, and `_LLCPWT`, and the documentation says to use the last. The American Time Use Survey file carries `WT06` and a page of text about weekends. Before you attach either to an estimate, the question worth an hour is: what is this column, and what did the producer do to it? Because the answer decides what the weighted number means, whose chance of selection it undoes, and what it silently assumes about the people who never answered.

Two weights, two logics. The ATUS weight begins as something you can see: BLS assigns half of its diary days to weekends on purpose, and a weekend diary therefore stands for fewer days than a weekday diary. That part of `WT06` is an inverse probability, and you could rebuild it from the design description. The BRFSS weight ends as something you cannot see. Somewhere in the 2024 file is a respondent who answered a telephone interviewer's questions and, in the estimates CDC publishes, stands for <Fact id="ch2.brfss_weight_max" /> adults. The median respondent stands for <Fact id="ch2.brfss_weight_median" />. The average Vermont respondent stands for <Fact id="ch2.brfss_mean_w_vt" /> adults and the average California respondent for <Fact id="ch2.brfss_mean_w_ca" />, because Vermont supplied <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_vt" /> interviews and California <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_ca" /> for adult populations of very different size. These numbers are `_LLCPWT`, and across the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_target" /> respondents in the states and the District of Columbia they add up to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_weight_total" /> adults. That respondent does not have a one-in-<Fact id="ch2.brfss_weight_max" /> chance of selection. The number is the product of five steps, and only the first three are probabilities.

The weight decides how much each answer counts. Among the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_fmd" /> respondents who gave a valid count of poor mental health days, <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_unweighted" /> reported frequent mental distress: 14 or more of the past 30 days on which their mental health was not good. Weighted, the same answers give <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_final" ci />. No answer changed. The <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_gap_pp" /> percentage points between the two figures come from the weight. This chapter takes the BRFSS weight apart step by step, asks how much of it can be rebuilt from the public file, sets the ATUS design weight beside it as the case where the probability is visible, and then asks what a person's weight means when it is no longer the inverse of a probability.

<KeyNumber id="ch2.brfss_fmd_final" label="Frequent mental distress, adults in the 49 states and DC that released 2024 BRFSS data" />

Frequent mental distress (FMD) is a self-reported count of days, not a clinical diagnosis. BRFSS is the state-based telephone survey run by CDC and state health departments, and every record carries the final weight along with every intermediate weight, so the estimate can be computed at each step of the construction. The first three steps are design weights: they undo known differences in the chance of being sampled, arising from the telephone stratum, the number of adults and phones in the household, and the chance of being reachable on both a landline and a cell phone. The fourth truncates the design weight and puts it on the population scale. The fifth rakes the weights until they reproduce population totals by age, sex, race and ethnicity, education, and other characteristics, state by state.

### What each link does

CDC's 2024 Overview documents how each link is made <Cite id="brfss2024" />, and every definition was checked against the file. The stratum weight `_STRWT` is the inverse of the sampling fraction for telephone numbers in the respondent's stratum. The adults-and-phones factor applies to the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_landline" /> landline respondents, whose chance of selection falls with the number of adults $$A_i$$ in the household and rises with its number of landline numbers $$k_i$$; a cell phone reaches a person, so the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_n_cell" /> cell-frame respondents get a factor of one. The dual-frame factor handles the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_dual_share" /> of respondents who could have been sampled from either frame, with a state-specific compositing factor $$\lambda_s$$ that ranges from <Fact id="ch2.brfss_lambda_min" /> to <Fact id="ch2.brfss_lambda_max" /> for landline-frame dual users. The raking input `_LLCPWT2` truncates the composite design weight within geographic regions at the mean plus or minus $$1.96$$ standard deviations and, as the data show, rescales it state by state. The final weight `_LLCPWT` rakes to population totals on eight margins in every state: sex by age, race and ethnicity, education, marital status, home ownership, sex by race, age by race, and telephone ownership.

The first three links multiply into the design weight $$d_i$$, which reflects each adult's chance of selection, and the last two act as an adjustment $$g_i$$, so that the final weight is $$w_i = d_i g_i$$. The design weight is

$$
\begin{aligned}
d_i &= \texttt{\_STRWT}_{h(i)} \times \frac{\min(A_i,\,5)}{\min(k_i,\,3)} \times c_i, \\[4pt]
c_i &= \begin{cases} \lambda_s & \text{dual user reached by landline} \\ 1-\lambda_s & \text{dual user reached by cell} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$

with $$A_i = k_i = 1$$ on the cell frame.

<Details summary="Each link as CDC documents it, and the fourth link in detail">

States sample telephone numbers within strata defined by geography and, for landlines, by the density of listed household numbers; `_STRWT` is the count of numbers in the stratum divided by the count selected. A landline call reaches a household and the interviewer picks one adult at random, which is where the adults-and-phones factor comes from. Since 2014 every adult reached on a cell phone is eligible, so an adult with both kinds of phone could have been sampled from either frame and gets $$\lambda_s$$ if reached by landline and $$1-\lambda_s$$ if reached by cell. Raking is iterative proportional fitting; states with sub-state regions or large counties add regional and county margins, and the control totals come from Claritas population estimates, the 2019–2023 American Community Survey, and NCHS estimates of wireless-only households.

At the `_STRWT` scale the dual-frame factor cuts the weighted share of dual users from <Fact id="ch2.share_dual_wt2rake" /> to <Fact id="ch2.share_dual_composite" />. The fourth link then does two things at once, so its movement is not the effect of truncation alone. CDC's Overview describes truncation at this step; the data also show a rescaling. `_LLCPWT2` totals <Fact id="ch2.brfss_llcpwt2_total" />, its total in each state is between <Fact id="ch2.brfss_l2_ratio_lo" /> and <Fact id="ch2.brfss_l2_ratio_hi" /> times the final weight's, and the dual-user share is back at <Fact id="ch2.share_dual_llcpwt2" />, essentially its final value. Because the rescaling differs from state to state, it can move the national estimate on its own.

</Details>

### What can be rebuilt

The chain has three kinds of link (Figure 2.4), and the difference matters to anyone who wants to modify a weight, or to understand what a modified weight has lost.

- **Rebuilt exactly from public variables.** `_RAWRAKE`, the adults-and-phones factor, equals $$\min(A_i,5)/\min(k_i,3)$$ for every landline respondent with a known count of numbers; the <Fact id="ch2.brfss_ll_unknown_phones" /> with an unknown count are treated as having one. `_WT2RAKE` equals `_STRWT` times `_RAWRAKE` on all but <Fact id="ch2.brfss_wt2rake_exceptions" /> records, all Illinois cell interviews that carry another stratum's weight. The two compositing factors sum to one in every state. `_STRWT` itself is shipped but not derivable: the counts behind it are not public.
- **Documented, not rebuildable.** `_LLCPWT2` depends on sub-state regions, which are removed from the public file, and on a rescaling the Overview does not describe. `_LLCPWT` depends on control totals and regional and county margins that are not on the file.
- **Illustrative only.** Raking `_LLCPWT2` to the final weight's own state totals on the eight public margins gives <Fact id="ch2.brfss_fmd_rerake_recon" />, against <Fact id="ch2.trim_none_fmd" /> for the published weight, yet the rebuilt weights differ from the published ones by a median of <Fact id="ch2.brfss_recon_median_dev" />. The visible margins reproduce nearly all of the raking step's effect on this estimate, but not the weights themselves.

## How uncertain the estimate is

BRFSS declares strata (`_STSTR`) and a primary sampling unit (`_PSU`), but the PSU is the respondent's sequence number: each respondent is its own PSU. The design is stratified and single-stage. The target population has <Fact id="ch2.brfss_strata" /> strata, and the FMD estimate has <Fact id="ch2.brfss_df" /> design degrees of freedom: respondents with a valid answer minus strata. Of the strata, <Fact id="ch2.brfss_singletons" /> hold a single respondent, where a variance formula that needs two units per stratum has nothing to work with. This chapter centers each lone unit at the grand mean, the rule in CDC's own R example; removing them instead changes the standard error only in its fifth significant digit.

The design-based standard error is <Fact id="ch2.se_as_sampling" /> percentage points. It treats the final weights as fixed, although raking is itself estimated from the sample <Cite id="lumley2010" />. A calibration-aware linearization, or replicate weights that repeat the raking, would carry that part of the variance too. The public file carries no replicate weights, and not all of the raking margins or their control totals, so it supports neither directly.

<Details summary="In code: the declaration in Python and R">

The full national file in [Chapter 8](/ch08-field-guide#brfss-2024), territories included, has more strata and more degrees of freedom. R's `survey` package stops on singleton strata by default, which is the right default; the option below overrides it.

## Inverse probabilities, and where they end

### The other logic: a design weight you can see

Only the first three links of the BRFSS chain have a probability interpretation, and even they rest on counts the public cannot see. The ATUS weight is the contrasting case, and it is worth holding the two side by side. ATUS asks each respondent about one day, assigned in advance and never substituted. BLS assigns a share $$0.10$$ of the sample to each weekday and $$0.25$$ to each weekend day, so weekdays and weekends each receive half <Cite id="bls2026" />. People work much less on weekends. Here the producer's decision is printed in the user guide, the probability is known, and a reader with the file can check what the weight does about it.

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">
Weekend days are about half of the ATUS diaries but two of every seven days in a year. Is the unweighted mean of minutes worked per day too high or too low, and by roughly how much?
</Callout>

Of the <Fact id="ch2.atus_n" /> diaries in 2023, <Fact id="ch2.atus_weekend_sample_share" /> describe a Saturday or Sunday. Respondents worked <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_weekday" /> minutes on the average weekday diary and <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_weekend" /> on the average weekend diary, so the unweighted mean of <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_unweighted" /> minutes leans toward the weekend and is too low.

The repair is the oldest idea in survey weighting: weight each unit by the inverse of its probability of selection <Cite id="horvitz1952" />.

$$
d_i = \frac{1}{\pi_i}, \qquad \hat{\bar y} = \frac{\sum_i d_i\, y_i}{\sum_i d_i}.
$$

A weekday diary has $$\pi_i \propto 0.10$$ and gets $$d_i \propto 10$$; a weekend diary has $$\pi_i \propto 0.25$$ and gets $$d_i \propto 4$$. The ratio on the right, the Hájek estimator, needs only relative weights. With $$d_i$$ alone, weekends shrink to <Fact id="ch2.atus_weekend_share_d" /> of person-days and the mean rises to <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_design" /> minutes (Figure 2.5).

<Figure n="2.5" caption="Weekend diaries are half the ATUS sample; the design weight shrinks them to about two-sevenths of person-days.">
<Chart id="ch2.atus_day_weights" />
</Figure>

The published weight, `WT06`, gives <Fact id="ch2.atus_work_weighted" /> minutes, which matches the published BLS figure to rounding <Cite id="bls_atus" />. It still carries the day allocation: the average weekday diary has <Fact id="ch2.atus_wt_ratio" /> times the weight of the average weekend diary, near the design ratio of $$10/4$$, and weekends get <Fact id="ch2.atus_weekend_share_wt06" /> of weighted person-days. The remaining distance is the $$g_i$$ in $$w_i = d_i g_i$$: other design factors, a nonresponse adjustment, and calibration to population totals, none of which a user can rebuild.<SideNote>This ATUS extract has no design variables, so it carries an estimand lesson and no uncertainty lesson. A standard error computed from `WT06` alone would leave out the design.</SideNote>

So the two weights differ in where the visible part ends. In ATUS, a known design probability does most of the work and the adjustment finishes it. In BRFSS, the design links are a scaffold, and the step that moves the estimate most is the one that rests on a model of who answers. Both columns are called "the weight," and both give a weighted mean with the same formula. What differs is how much of each weight you can defend from the design alone, and how much rests on the producer's assumptions about nonresponse.

### Where the probability interpretation ends

The rescaling in `_LLCPWT2` and the raking in `_LLCPWT` make the sample reproduce known population totals, and they rest on a model of nonresponse: within the raked categories, respondents resemble nonrespondents. <Cite id="gelman2007" narrative /> opens by calling survey weighting "a mess." Weights in practice, he stresses, are generally not inverse probabilities of selection: they combine selection probabilities with adjustments for nonresponse, and once they do, a weighted mean's standard error is no longer straightforward and anything beyond a mean or ratio is harder still. The respondent who stands for <Fact id="ch2.brfss_weight_max" /> adults does not have a one-in-<Fact id="ch2.brfss_weight_max" /> chance of selection. That number says how many adults, in the raked categories the respondent belongs to, the sample was short of.

### What software calls a weight

The word "weight" also names three different objects in statistical software, and the option you type commits you to one of them.

- A **frequency weight** $$f_i$$ says the record stands for $$f_i$$ identical observations; the sample size is $$\sum_i f_i$$.
- A **precision (analytic) weight** $$a_i$$ says observation $$i$$ has variance $$\sigma^2/a_i$$, as when each record is a mean over $$a_i$$ people.
- A **sampling weight** $$w_i = d_i\, g_i$$ says the record represents $$w_i$$ population units, with a variance that depends on the design that produced it.

All three give the same weighted mean and different standard errors. Read as a frequency weight, `_LLCPWT` treats every weighted adult as interviewed and gives a standard error of <Fact id="ch2.se_as_frequency" /> percentage points for FMD; read as a precision weight, <Fact id="ch2.se_as_analytic" />; read as a sampling weight with the declared design, <Fact id="ch2.se_as_sampling" />.<SideNote>Stata's `fweight`, `aweight`, and `pweight`, and the `weights` argument of R's `lm` (a precision weight), each commit to one reading whether or not the user meant it.</SideNote>

<Callout kind="box" title="Two effective sample sizes for one BRFSS estimate">

[Chapter 0](/ch00-start-here) separates four objects called "effective sample size." Two of them can be computed here, and they answer different questions.

**The Kish effective sample size**, $$\nKish = n/(1+\CV(w)^2) = (\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$$, prices weight dispersion alone <Cite id="kish1965" />: the size of a simple random sample that would give a weighted mean the same variance *if* the outcome were unrelated to the weights and the design had no strata or clusters. Much of the loss is the state allocation: within the median state, $$\nKish/n$$ is <Fact id="ch2.brfss_kish_ratio_state_median" />. A national estimate pays for sample sizes chosen for state estimates.

## Trimming: precision bought with bias

Large weights make estimates noisy, so producers trim them, as CDC does to the design weight before raking. Analysts are sometimes tempted to trim the final weight themselves, and the respondent standing for <Fact id="ch2.brfss_weight_max" /> adults is the kind of record that invites it. The 95th-percentile cap cuts the coefficient of variation from <Fact id="ch2.brfss_cv_final" /> to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_cv" /> and raises $$\nKish$$ from <Fact id="ch2.brfss_nkish_final" /> to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_nkish" />, but the standard error falls only to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_se_ratio" /> of its untrimmed value: standard errors move with the square root of the effective sample size. The estimate falls by <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_shift_pp" /> percentage points to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_fmd" ci />, a shift of <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_shift_se" /> standard errors. The milder 99th-percentile cap gives <Fact id="ch2.trim_p99_fmd" />, with CV <Fact id="ch2.trim_p99_cv" /> and $$\nKish$$ <Fact id="ch2.trim_p99_nkish" />.

The shift has a visible compositional source: trimming changes which groups carry weight. Within a state the largest weights tend to belong to groups that raking pushed up, so capping them undoes part of the raking. After the 95th-percentile cap, adults 18–24 fall to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_age1824" /> of weighted adults, below the <Fact id="ch2.share_age1824_final" /> the population totals require, while cell-only adults rise to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95_cellonly" />. A trimmed weight that no longer matches the margins has traded part of a bias correction for lower variance, and the size of the bias it readmits is unknown. Re-raking the capped weights to the same margins restores them exactly and returns the estimate to <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95r_fmd" />, with CV <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95r_cv" /> and $$\nKish$$ <Fact id="ch2.trim_p95r_nkish" />: part of the precision gain survives, part is given back. Producers iterate between trimming and raking for this reason <Cite id="valliant2018" />; trimming a final weight without re-raking changes the population the estimate describes.

<Callout kind="note" title="A new case">

An analyst caps a survey's final weights and rescales them so that every state keeps its weighted total, but the capped weights no longer match the population's age distribution. What can the analyst still say about a state's weighted total, and what can no longer be assumed about an outcome that varies with age?

<Details summary="One answer">

State totals, and anything that depends only on them, are unchanged. The age mix is not: the capped weights describe a population with the wrong age distribution, so an outcome that varies with age, such as frequent mental distress, is pulled toward the respondents' age mix. How far depends on how much the outcome differs across the misrepresented groups, which the capped weights alone cannot reveal. Re-raking to the margins restores the age distribution, and comparing the capped and re-raked estimates shows how much the lost margins mattered for that outcome.

</Details>
