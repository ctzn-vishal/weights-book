[Chapter 4](/ch04-regression) ended with a reassuring result. The survey-weighted regression of log hourly wage on working from home, age, sex, and education gave <Fact id="ch4.adj_wls" />, and its unweighted twin gave almost the same number. The weights are applied correctly, the sample represents the population, and the two estimators agree. It is tempting to read the coefficient as the average home–commute wage contrast in the population.

Split the same workers more finely, into <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> cells defined by occupation group and education, and compute the home–commute contrast in log hourly wage inside each cell. Now there are two defensible-looking summaries. Average the cell contrasts using each cell's share of the population and the answer is <Fact id="ch5.pate" ci />. Run the survey-weighted regression with a fixed effect for every cell, which compares home workers and commuters only within cells, and the answer is <Fact id="ch5.cellfe_wls" ci />, about <Fact id="ch5.proj_pate_ratio" /> times as large. Both sit well below Chapter 4's coefficient because both compare workers within occupation groups. Both use the ACS person weight. Both "represent the population." They are different weighted averages of the same contrasts, and the regression, not the survey, chose its weights.

## Who works from home

Working from home (WFH) is concentrated in particular jobs. Across the <Fact id="ch5.n_og" /> major occupation groups of the 2018 Standard Occupational Classification, the share ranges from <Fact id="ch5.prev_occ_lo" /> in <Fact id="ch5.prev_occ_lo_label" /> to <Fact id="ch5.prev_occ_hi" /> in <Fact id="ch5.prev_occ_hi_label" /> (Figure 5.1), and, as Chapter 4 showed, it rises steeply with education.

The contrasts vary too. When both the chance of working from home and the wage contrast differ across groups, any single coefficient is a weighted average of group contrasts, and the question is who chose the weights.

## Cells and their contrasts

The cells cross the <Fact id="ch5.n_og" /> occupation groups with four education levels, <Fact id="ch5.n_base_cells" /> cells in all. A contrast needs both home workers and commuters, so within each occupation group a thin education cell is merged into its neighbor until every cell has at least 100 records and at least 30 workers on each side. In all, <Fact id="ch5.n_merges" /> thin cells, all of them "less than high school" cells in occupations where few workers lack a diploma, were merged into the high school cell, leaving <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> cells. No record is dropped. The model is Chapter 4's with the education dummies refined into cells:

$$
y_i = \beta D_i + \alpha_{c(i)} + z_i'\gamma + \varepsilon_i ,
$$

where $$y_i$$ is log hourly wage, $$c(i)$$ is person $$i$$'s cell, and $$z_i$$ holds age, age squared, and a female indicator. The cell contrast $$\hat\tau_c$$ is the survey-weighted difference in mean log wage between home workers and commuters in cell $$c$$, after subtracting the fitted age–sex profile $$z_i'\hat\gamma$$. The contrasts run from <Fact id="ch5.tau_min" /> to <Fact id="ch5.tau_max" />, and the share working from home from <Fact id="ch5.p_cell_min" /> to <Fact id="ch5.p_cell_max" />.

## Which cells the regression listens to

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

The survey-weighted regression with cell fixed effects is an exact weighted average of the <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> cell contrasts. Before reading on: do the largest cells get the most weight? What happens to a large cell in which almost nobody works from home?

</Callout>

With a full set of cell fixed effects, the regression coefficient equals

$$
\hat\beta = \frac{\sum_c \omega_c\, \hat\tau_c}{\sum_c \omega_c}, \qquad \omega_c = N_c\, p_c (1 - p_c),
$$

where $$N_c$$ is the cell's weighted population and $$p_c$$ its weighted share working from home <Cite id="angrist1998" />. A cell's weight is its size times the variance of the work-from-home indicator inside it. <Cite id="aronow2016" narrative /> write the same weights at the level of individuals, $$\omega_i = (D_i - \hat E[D_i \mid X_i])^2$$. With cell effects alone, the predicted probability within a cell is $$p_c$$, so the individual weights, each multiplied by its survey weight $$w_i$$, add up to $$N_c p_c(1-p_c)$$. A cell where almost everyone commutes has $$p_c(1-p_c)$$ near zero, however large it is. The identity holds exactly in this sample: the build recomputes the coefficient from the cell contrasts and weights and matches the regression to machine precision. [Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations#variance-weighting-in-ols-with-a-binary-treatment) gives the derivation.

The implicit weights are never negative in this form, and survey weights enter them only through $$N_c$$ and $$p_c$$. Survey weighting makes those two quantities estimates for the population rather than for the sample. It does not decide that the factor $$p_c(1-p_c)$$ belongs in the average; the regression does.

The unweighted regression gives every record the same case weight, but it does not give every cell's contrast the same weight, or a weight in proportion to its sample size: its implicit weights are $$n_c \hat p_c(1-\hat p_c)$$. Across cells they are almost identical to the survey-weighted regression's (correlation <Fact id="ch5.reg_share_corr" />). Equal case weights do not imply equal weighting of heterogeneous conditional effects.

## The regression-effective sample

A survey-weighted regression can represent the population correctly in the design sense, with every cell at its estimated population size, while placing highly unequal implicit weight on heterogeneous conditional contrasts. The implicit weights form the third of [Chapter 2](/ch02-weights#weights-that-are-not-inverse-probabilities)'s four effective samples: a composition, not a count. Figure 5.4 sums them within occupation groups and sets each group's share of the weight against its share of workers.

</Figure>

In aggregate, the ten cells with the most weight hold <Fact id="ch5.top10_reg" /> of it but only <Fact id="ch5.top10_pop" /> of workers. The <Fact id="ch5.lowp_n_cells" /> cells where fewer than one worker in twenty works from home hold <Fact id="ch5.lowp_pop" /> of workers and <Fact id="ch5.lowp_reg" /> of the weight; cells where a quarter or more work from home hold <Fact id="ch5.hip_pop" /> of workers and <Fact id="ch5.hip_reg" /> of the weight. Measured as an effective number of equally weighted cells (the inverse of the sum of squared shares), the population's <Fact id="ch5.eff_cells_pop" /> shrink to <Fact id="ch5.eff_cells_reg" /> in the regression, and a worker in the most balanced cell counts <Fact id="ch5.per_worker_ratio" /> times as much as a worker in the least balanced one.

The implicit weights and the three averages can be reproduced from the cell data behind the interactive below:

```python
import numpy as np

share, p, tau = cells["pop_share"], cells["treat_share"], cells["effect"]
omega = share * p * (1 - p)                     # Angrist (1998) implicit weights

projection = np.sum(omega * tau) / np.sum(omega)    # = survey-weighted coefficient
pate = np.sum(share * tau) / np.sum(share)           # population-weighted
att = np.sum(share * p * tau) / np.sum(share * p)    # home-worker-weighted
atu = np.sum(share * (1 - p) * tau) / np.sum(share * (1 - p))
```

## When the weights turn negative

With a full set of cell fixed effects the weights are non-negative. With linear covariates they need not be. <Cite id="chattopadhyay2023" narrative /> show that an OLS coefficient on a binary treatment is a difference between a weighted mean of treated outcomes and a weighted mean of control outcomes, with the weight on person $$i$$ proportional to $$D_i - \hat\ell_i$$ (times $$w_i$$ in a survey-weighted regression), where $$\hat\ell_i$$ is the fitted value from a linear probability model of the treatment on the regression's controls. A commuter whose fitted probability is below zero enters the comparison with the wrong sign, as if counted among the home workers.

In Chapter 4's headline specification this does not happen: fitted probabilities of working from home run from <Fact id="ch5.lpm_min_head" /> to <Fact id="ch5.lpm_max_head" />, so every implied weight is positive. Add fixed effects for detailed occupation, industry, and state, as on the last rung of Chapter 4's ladder, and the additive linear probability model gives fitted values from <Fact id="ch5.lpm_min_occind" /> to <Fact id="ch5.lpm_max_occind" />. Then <Fact id="ch5.neg_commuters" /> of commuters, weighted by `PERWT`, carry negative weight (Figure 5.5). Their weights are small, together <Fact id="ch5.neg_mass" /> of the total absolute weight, but they are concentrated where home work is rare: in <Fact id="ch5.neg_top_occ" />, <Fact id="ch5.neg_top_occ_share" /> of commuters are counted with the wrong sign.<SideNote>Strictly, even the cell model's linear age and sex terms push a few fitted probabilities just below zero (the lowest is <Fact id="ch5.m5_lpm_min" />, affecting <Fact id="ch5.m5_neg_share" /> of commuters). The cell-level weights above are exact and remain non-negative.</SideNote>

## Smaller groups get larger weights

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Only <Fact id="ch5.sl_rho_wls" /> of workers work from home. A population average gives the contrast for home workers that share of the weight and the contrast for commuters the rest. How much weight do you expect the regression to give each?

</Callout>

<Cite id="sloczynski2022" narrative /> shows that an OLS coefficient on a binary treatment with additive controls can be written as follows; with weighted moments, the same holds for a survey-weighted regression:

$$
\hat\beta = \omega_1\, \ATT + \omega_0\, \ATU, \qquad \omega_1 = \frac{(1-\rho)\, V_0}{\rho\, V_1 + (1-\rho)\, V_0}, \quad \omega_0 = 1 - \omega_1 ,
$$

where $$\omega_1$$ is the implicit weight on the treated group's term (Słoczyński writes $$w_1$$, the symbol the table and ledger keep), $$\rho$$ is the share treated, and $$V_d$$ is the variance of the linear propensity score (the fitted values of a linear probability model of the treatment on the controls) among people with treatment status $$d$$. If the propensity score were equally variable in both groups, $$\omega_1 = 1-\rho$$: OLS would give the treated group's effect the weight that belongs to the untreated group, and vice versa. The smaller group gets the larger weight.

  <Chart id="ch5.sloczynski" />
</Figure>

In the survey-weighted cell regression (Figure 5.6), <Fact id="ch5.sl_rho_wls" /> of workers work from home and the weight on the home-worker term is <Fact id="ch5.sl_w1_wls" />. The unweighted regression, in which <Fact id="ch5.sl_rho_ols" /> of records are home workers, gives <Fact id="ch5.sl_w1_ols" />, and Chapter 4's model with education only gives <Fact id="ch5.sl4_w1" />. That is the same pattern as Figure 5.3, where the regression sat next to the home-worker average.

Słoczyński's $$\ATT$$ and $$\ATU$$ are not the cell-based analogues of Figure 5.3. They are average partial linear effects: within each group, log wage is projected on the propensity score by a straight line, and each term compares one group's mean with the other group's line evaluated at the first group's mean score. Here they are <Fact id="ch5.sl_att" /> for home workers and <Fact id="ch5.sl_atu" /> for commuters, implying an average partial effect of <Fact id="ch5.sl_ape" />, against cell-based analogues of <Fact id="ch5.att" /> and <Fact id="ch5.atu" />; the two pairs disagree even on which group's contrast is larger. They would coincide only if, within each group, mean log wage were a straight-line function of the propensity score. Across occupation-by-education cells it is not, and the commuter term is the most exposed: it evaluates the home workers' line, fitted mostly in cells where home work is common, at the low scores typical of commuters. Read as causal quantities, the two terms would also require unconfoundedness. The weights rest on neither assumption: they depend only on the share working from home and on how much the propensity score varies within each group. Applied to the cell-based analogues, they give <Fact id="ch5.sl_hybrid" />, close to the fitted coefficient. Read Figure 5.6 for its weights, not for its ATT and ATU columns. The decomposition itself is exact, and the build checks the implementation against the numerical example in Słoczyński's paper and against simulations with known answers.

## What the survey weights did and did not do

The opening's two numbers are both averages of the same <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> contrasts. The survey-weighted regression, <Fact id="ch5.cellfe_wls" />, weights each cell by $$N_c p_c(1-p_c)$$; the population-weighted average, <Fact id="ch5.pate" />, weights it by $$N_c$$. `PERWT` did its job in both: every cell's population and share working from home are estimated for the population, and the weighted and unweighted regressions agree, as in Chapter 4. What the weights cannot do is choose the average. With heterogeneous contrasts, a regression with additive controls reports a variance-weighted average that leans on overlap cells, here the college-educated managers, business and computer workers, and office staff among whom home work is common enough to compare. The population's average contrast is a different and smaller number, and survey weights will not turn a regression coefficient into it <Cite id="solon2015" />.

Four habits follow for anyone reporting a regression like this one. First, name the target: the population projection, a population-weighted average of conditional contrasts, or an average for the treated. Second, if the population average is the target, estimate it directly: compute the contrast in each cell, or fit a model in which the treatment interacts with the cells, and aggregate with population shares. Third, report the regression-effective sample next to the coefficient: the implicit weights by group, set against population shares, as in Figure 5.4 and in the table inside the interactive. Fourth, report Słoczyński's $$\omega_1$$ beside the treated share; when they sit far apart and the treated and untreated contrasts differ, the coefficient answers mostly a question about the smaller group. None of this changes the variance recipe: every aggregate in this chapter, including the population-weighted average, gets its standard error by rerunning the whole calculation on each of the 80 replicate weights.
