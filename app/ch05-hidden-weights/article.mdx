[Chapter 4](/ch04-regression) ended with a reassuring result. Both calculations use the released person weight to target the same specified population, and they agree. Both summaries above sit well below Chapter 4's coefficient because both compare workers within occupation groups. They differ from each other because the regression, not the survey, chose its weights.

## Who works from home

Working from home (WFH) means, as in Chapter 4, usually working at home in the survey's reference week, and it is concentrated in particular jobs. Across the <Fact id="ch5.n_og" /> major occupation groups of the 2018 Standard Occupational Classification, the share ranges from <Fact id="ch5.prev_occ_lo" /> in <Fact id="ch5.prev_occ_lo_label" /> to <Fact id="ch5.prev_occ_hi" /> in <Fact id="ch5.prev_occ_hi_label" /> (Figure 5.1), and, as Chapter 4 showed, it rises steeply with education.

The contrasts vary too. Within education groups, the home–commute gap is <Fact id="ch5.educ_tau_ba" /> log points for college graduates and <Fact id="ch5.educ_tau_lths" /> for workers without a diploma. When both the chance of working from home and the wage contrast differ across groups, any single coefficient is a weighted average of group contrasts, and the question is who chose the weights.

## Cells and their contrasts

The cells cross the <Fact id="ch5.n_og" /> occupation groups with four education levels, <Fact id="ch5.n_base_cells" /> cells in all. A contrast needs both home workers and commuters, so within each occupation group a thin education cell is merged into its neighbor until every cell has at least 100 records and at least 30 workers on each side. In all, <Fact id="ch5.n_merges" /> thin cells, all of them "less than high school" cells in occupations where few workers lack a diploma, were merged into the high school cell, leaving <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> cells. No record is dropped. The model is Chapter 4's with the education dummies refined into cells:

$$
y_i = \beta D_i + \alpha_{c(i)} + z_i'\gamma + \varepsilon_i ,
$$

where $$y_i$$ is log hourly wage, $$c(i)$$ is person $$i$$'s cell, and $$z_i$$ holds age, age squared, and a female indicator. The cell contrast $$\hat\tau_c$$ is the survey-weighted difference in mean log wage between home workers and commuters in cell $$c$$, after subtracting the fitted age–sex profile $$z_i'\hat\gamma$$. That profile is common to all cells and fitted on the whole sample, so every contrast in this chapter is net of it; these are not the contrasts that separate regressions within each cell would give. The contrasts run from <Fact id="ch5.tau_min" /> to <Fact id="ch5.tau_max" />, and the share working from home from <Fact id="ch5.p_cell_min" /> to <Fact id="ch5.p_cell_max" />.

## Which cells the regression listens to

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

The survey-weighted regression with cell fixed effects is an exact weighted average of the <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> cell contrasts. Before reading on: do the largest cells get the most weight? What happens to a large cell in which almost nobody works from home?

</Callout>

A comparison within a cell needs both kinds of worker. A cell where almost everyone commutes offers only a handful of home workers, so however large it is, it says little about the home–commute contrast, and a regression that compares only within cells leans on the cells where both groups are plentiful. With a full set of cell fixed effects, the regression coefficient equals

$$
\hat\beta = \frac{\sum_c \omega_c\, \hat\tau_c}{\sum_c \omega_c}, \qquad \omega_c = N_c\, p_c (1 - p_c),
$$

where $$N_c$$ is the cell's weighted population and $$p_c$$ its weighted share working from home <Cite id="angrist1998" />. A cell's weight is its size times the variance of the work-from-home indicator inside it, so the answer to the prediction is no: a cell where almost everyone commutes has $$p_c(1-p_c)$$ near zero, however large it is. <Cite id="aronow2016" narrative /> write the same weights at the level of individuals, $$\omega_i = (D_i - \hat E[D_i \mid X_i])^2$$. With cell effects alone, the predicted probability within a cell is $$p_c$$, so the individual weights, each multiplied by its survey weight $$w_i$$, add up to $$N_c p_c(1-p_c)$$.

The identity is exact for the adjusted contrasts. The age–sex coefficients $$\hat\gamma$$ come from the same least-squares fit as $$\hat\beta$$, so holding them at $$\hat\gamma$$ and refitting only the treatment and cell effects returns the same $$\hat\beta$$. That refit is a regression of the adjusted outcome $$y_i - z_i'\hat\gamma$$ on the treatment and the cell dummies alone, to which Angrist's algebra applies. The build recomputes the coefficient from the adjusted cell contrasts and weights and matches the regression to machine precision; [Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations#variance-weighting-in-ols-with-a-binary-treatment) gives the derivation, including this step.

The implicit weights are never negative in this form, and survey weights enter them only through $$N_c$$ and $$p_c$$. Survey weighting makes those two quantities estimates for the population rather than for the sample. It does not decide that the factor $$p_c(1-p_c)$$ belongs in the average; the regression does.

## One set of contrasts, several averages

Figure 5.3 sets the two cell regressions beside three averages of the same <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> contrasts: the *population-share average*, which weights each cell by its share of workers; the *home-worker-share average*, which weights it by its share of home workers; and the *commuter-share average*, which weights it by its share of commuters. The unweighted regression gives every record the same case weight, but it does not give every cell's contrast the same weight, or a weight in proportion to its sample size: its implicit weights are $$n_c \hat p_c(1-\hat p_c)$$. Across cells they are almost identical to the survey-weighted regression's (correlation <Fact id="ch5.reg_share_corr" />). Equal case weights do not imply equal weighting of heterogeneous conditional effects.

## The regression-effective sample

A survey-weighted regression can represent the population correctly in the design sense, with every cell at its estimated population size, while placing highly unequal implicit weight on heterogeneous conditional contrasts. The implicit weights form the third of the booklet's four effective samples ([Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations#four-effective-samples)): a composition, not a count. Figure 5.4 sums them within occupation groups and sets each group's share of the weight against its share of workers.

</Figure>

The concentration in the opening, ten cells with <Fact id="ch5.top10_reg" /> of the weight and <Fact id="ch5.top10_pop" /> of workers, runs through the whole distribution. The <Fact id="ch5.lowp_n_cells" /> cells where fewer than one worker in twenty works from home hold <Fact id="ch5.lowp_pop" /> of workers and <Fact id="ch5.lowp_reg" /> of the weight; cells where a quarter or more work from home hold <Fact id="ch5.hip_pop" /> of workers and <Fact id="ch5.hip_reg" /> of the weight. Measured as an effective number of equally weighted cells (the inverse of the sum of squared shares), the population's <Fact id="ch5.eff_cells_pop" /> shrink to <Fact id="ch5.eff_cells_reg" /> in the regression, and a worker in the most balanced cell counts <Fact id="ch5.per_worker_ratio" /> times as much as a worker in the least balanced one.

The implicit weights and the three averages can be reproduced from the cell data behind the interactive below:

```python
import numpy as np

share, p, tau = cells["pop_share"], cells["treat_share"], cells["effect"]
omega = share * p * (1 - p)                     # Angrist (1998) implicit weights

projection = np.sum(omega * tau) / np.sum(omega)    # = survey-weighted coefficient
pate = np.sum(share * tau) / np.sum(share)           # population-share average
att = np.sum(share * p * tau) / np.sum(share * p)    # home-worker-share average
atu = np.sum(share * (1 - p) * tau) / np.sum(share * (1 - p))  # commuter-share average
```

## What the survey weights did and did not do

The opening's two numbers are both averages of the same <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> contrasts. The survey-weighted regression, <Fact id="ch5.cellfe_wls" />, weights each cell by $$N_c p_c(1-p_c)$$; the population-share average, <Fact id="ch5.pate" />, weights it by $$N_c$$. `PERWT` did its job in both: every cell's population and share working from home are estimated for the population, and the weighted and unweighted regressions agree, as in Chapter 4. What the weights cannot do is choose the average. With heterogeneous contrasts, a regression that compares within cells reports a variance-weighted average that leans on overlap cells, here the college-educated managers, business and computer workers, and office staff among whom home work is common enough to compare. The population's average contrast is a different and smaller number, and survey weights will not turn a regression coefficient into it <Cite id="solon2015" />.

Four habits follow for anyone reporting a regression like this one. First, name the target: the population projection, a population-weighted average of conditional contrasts, or an average for the treated. Second, if the population average is the target, estimate it directly: compute the contrast in each cell, or fit a model in which the treatment interacts with the cells, and aggregate with population shares. That estimate has costs of its own. It counts small cells at full population weight, where contrasts rest on few home workers and are imprecise, and it depends on how the workers were divided into cells. Third, report the regression-effective sample next to the coefficient: the implicit weights by group, set against population shares, as in Figure 5.4 and in the table inside the interactive. Fourth, when the controls enter additively rather than as a full set of cells, check the two representations in the next section: some implied weights can turn negative, and the weight on the treated group's term can sit far from the treated share. None of this changes the variance recipe: every aggregate in this chapter, including the population-share average, gets its standard error by rerunning the whole calculation on each of the 80 replicate weights.

## Going further: signed weights and group weights

The headline needs nothing beyond the cell weights above. Two further representations of the same kind of coefficient are worth knowing, because they apply when controls enter additively and because their labels invite confusion. Each weights something different.

| Representation | What is weighted | Model | Can a weight be negative? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell weights $$N_c\, p_c(1-p_c)$$ <Cite id="angrist1998" /> | cell contrasts | a full set of cell effects | no |
| Individual weights $$(D_i - \hat E[D_i \mid X_i])^2$$ <Cite id="aronow2016" /> | individuals' conditional contrasts | linear controls, with a treatment probability linear in them | no |
| Signed weights proportional to $$D_i - \hat\ell_i$$ <Cite id="chattopadhyay2023" /> | individual outcomes, treated against controls | any linear controls | yes |
| Group weights $$\omega_1$$ and $$\omega_0$$ <Cite id="sloczynski2022" /> | the two groups' average partial linear effects | additive linear controls | no, but the terms are not cell averages |

### When the weights turn negative

With a full set of cell fixed effects the weights are non-negative. With linear covariates they need not be. <Cite id="chattopadhyay2023" narrative /> show that an OLS coefficient on a binary treatment is a difference between a weighted mean of treated outcomes and a weighted mean of control outcomes, with the weight on person $$i$$ proportional to $$D_i - \hat\ell_i$$ (times $$w_i$$ in a survey-weighted regression), where $$\hat\ell_i$$ is the fitted value from a linear probability model of the treatment on the regression's controls. A commuter whose fitted probability is below zero enters the comparison with the wrong sign, as if counted among the home workers.

In Chapter 4's headline specification this does not happen: fitted probabilities of working from home run from <Fact id="ch5.lpm_min_head" /> to <Fact id="ch5.lpm_max_head" />, so every implied weight is positive. Add fixed effects for detailed occupation, industry, and state, as on the last rung of Chapter 4's ladder, and the additive linear probability model gives fitted values from <Fact id="ch5.lpm_min_occind" /> to <Fact id="ch5.lpm_max_occind" />. Then <Fact id="ch5.neg_commuters" /> of commuters, weighted by `PERWT`, carry negative weight (Figure 5.5). Their weights are small, together <Fact id="ch5.neg_mass" /> of the total absolute weight, but they are concentrated where home work is rare: in <Fact id="ch5.neg_top_occ" />, <Fact id="ch5.neg_top_occ_share" /> of commuters are counted with the wrong sign.<SideNote>Strictly, even the cell model's linear age and sex terms push a few fitted probabilities just below zero (the lowest is <Fact id="ch5.m5_lpm_min" />, affecting <Fact id="ch5.m5_neg_share" /> of commuters). The cell-level weights above are exact and remain non-negative.</SideNote>

### Smaller groups get larger weights

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Only <Fact id="ch5.sl_rho_wls" /> of workers work from home. A population average gives the contrast for home workers that share of the weight and the contrast for commuters the rest. How much weight do you expect the regression to give each?

</Callout>

<Cite id="sloczynski2022" narrative /> shows that an OLS coefficient on a binary treatment with additive controls can be written as follows; with weighted moments, the same holds for a survey-weighted regression:

$$
\hat\beta = \omega_1\, \ATT + \omega_0\, \ATU, \qquad \omega_1 = \frac{(1-\rho)\, V_0}{\rho\, V_1 + (1-\rho)\, V_0}, \quad \omega_0 = 1 - \omega_1 ,
$$

where $$\omega_1$$ is the implicit weight on the treated group's term (Słoczyński writes $$w_1$$, the symbol the table and ledger keep), $$\rho$$ is the share treated, and $$V_d$$ is the variance of the linear propensity score (the fitted values of a linear probability model of the treatment on the controls) among people with treatment status $$d$$. If the propensity score were equally variable in both groups, $$\omega_1 = 1-\rho$$: OLS would give the treated group's effect the weight that belongs to the untreated group, and vice versa. The smaller group gets the larger weight.

In the survey-weighted cell regression (Figure 5.6), <Fact id="ch5.sl_rho_wls" /> of workers work from home and the weight on the home-worker term is <Fact id="ch5.sl_w1_wls" />. The unweighted regression, in which <Fact id="ch5.sl_rho_ols" /> of records are home workers, gives <Fact id="ch5.sl_w1_ols" />, and Chapter 4's model with education only gives <Fact id="ch5.sl4_w1" />. That is the same pattern as Figure 5.3, where the regression sat next to the home-worker-share average.

Słoczyński's $$\ATT$$ and $$\ATU$$ are not the cell-based averages of Figure 5.3. They are average partial linear effects: within each group, log wage is projected on the propensity score by a straight line, and each term compares one group's mean with the other group's line evaluated at the first group's mean score. Here they are <Fact id="ch5.sl_att" /> for home workers and <Fact id="ch5.sl_atu" /> for commuters, implying an average partial effect of <Fact id="ch5.sl_ape" />, against a home-worker-share average of <Fact id="ch5.att" /> and a commuter-share average of <Fact id="ch5.atu" />. The two pairs disagree even on which group's contrast is larger.

They would coincide only if, within each group, mean log wage were a straight-line function of the propensity score. Across occupation-by-education cells it is not, and the commuter term is the most exposed: it evaluates the home workers' line, fitted mostly in cells where home work is common, at the low scores typical of commuters.

What the decomposition does establish is its weights. The decomposition itself is exact, and the build checks the implementation against the numerical example in Słoczyński's paper and against simulations with known answers. Its weights depend only on the share working from home and on how much the propensity score varies within each group. Applied to the cell-based averages, they give <Fact id="ch5.sl_hybrid" />, close to the fitted coefficient.

It does not establish a causal result. Reading the two terms as causal quantities would require unconfoundedness as well as linearity, and this descriptive analysis claims neither. Read Figure 5.6 for its weights, not for its ATT and ATU columns.
