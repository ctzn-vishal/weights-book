## Whose wage gap is it?

You ran the regression with the controls your field expects, and you are about to describe the coefficient as the average effect of the treatment, holding the controls fixed. Most readers will take it that way. But when the effect differs across the groups the controls define, and it usually does, the coefficient is a weighted average of the group contrasts with weights the regression chose and nobody reports. Survey weights do not choose them and cannot correct them. This chapter makes the weights visible in a case where they move the answer, and in a second case where they are just as unequal and do not.

A report on the 2024 American Community Survey wants one sentence: among full-time wage and salary workers, how much more do people who work from home earn than people who commute, holding occupation and education fixed? [Chapter 4](/ch04-regression) supplied the regression. Refine its education dummies into <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> occupation-by-education cells, keep the survey weights, and the coefficient is <Fact id="ch5.cellfe_wls" ci /> log points. The same <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> cells each have their own home–commute contrast, and those contrasts can be averaged by hand, each cell counted by its share of workers. That average is <Fact id="ch5.pate" ci />. Same workers, same `PERWT`, same cell contrasts, and the regression's number is <Fact id="ch5.proj_pate_ratio" /> times the population's.

Both numbers are defensible. The difference between them is a choice of weights, and nobody made it. Written out, a regression with a full set of cell effects is a weighted average of the cell contrasts in which each cell counts by its population times the variance of the work-from-home indicator inside it <Cite id="angrist1998" />. A cell where home workers and commuters are both plentiful counts heavily. A cell where almost everyone commutes barely counts, however many workers it holds. College-educated managers, <Fact id="ch5.top_cell_pop" /> of the workforce, receive <Fact id="ch5.top_cell_reg" /> of the regression's weight. Transportation and moving workers with a high school diploma, <Fact id="ch5.lowp_big_pop" /> of the workforce, receive <Fact id="ch5.lowp_big_reg" />. The ten most-weighted cells hold <Fact id="ch5.top10_pop" /> of workers and <Fact id="ch5.top10_reg" /> of the weight. Seen from the other side, in the decomposition of <Cite id="sloczynski2022" narrative />, home workers are <Fact id="ch5.sl_rho_wls" /> of the workforce and the term for their contrast carries <Fact id="ch5.sl_w1_wls" /> of the coefficient.

A reader should care for two reasons. The contrasts differ across cells, from <Fact id="ch5.tau_min" /> to <Fact id="ch5.tau_max" /> log points, and they tend to be larger where home work is common, so the cells the regression favors are the cells with the larger gaps. And the survey weights cannot fix this. They make each cell's population and home-work share estimates for the nation; they do not decide how the cells are combined. Chapter 4's coefficient, <Fact id="ch4.adj_wls" />, agreed with its unweighted twin, and the cell regression's unweighted twin, <Fact id="ch5.cellfe_ols" />, agrees with it as well. The weights that separate the regression from the population average are not survey weights. This chapter makes them visible and says what to report instead.

## Cells and their contrasts

The cells cross the <Fact id="ch5.n_og" /> occupation groups with four education levels. A contrast needs both home workers and commuters in the cell, so <Fact id="ch5.n_merges" /> thin cells were merged into a neighbor, leaving <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" />; no record is dropped. The model is Chapter 4's with the education dummies refined into cells:

$$
y_i = \beta D_i + \alpha_{c(i)} + z_i'\gamma + \varepsilon_i ,
$$

where $$y_i$$ is log hourly wage, $$D_i$$ indicates working from home, $$c(i)$$ is person $$i$$'s cell, and $$z_i$$ holds age, age squared, and a female indicator. The cell contrast $$\hat\tau_c$$ is the survey-weighted difference in mean log wage between home workers and commuters in cell $$c$$, after subtracting the fitted age–sex profile $$z_i'\hat\gamma$$. That profile is common to all cells and fitted on the whole sample, so every contrast in this chapter is net of it. The contrasts run from <Fact id="ch5.tau_min" /> to <Fact id="ch5.tau_max" />, and the share working from home from <Fact id="ch5.p_cell_min" /> to <Fact id="ch5.p_cell_max" />.

<Details summary="How the cells were built">

The base partition has <Fact id="ch5.n_base_cells" /> cells. Within each occupation group, a thin education cell is merged into its neighbor until every cell has at least 100 records and at least 30 workers on each side of the home–commute divide. All <Fact id="ch5.n_merges" /> merges were "less than high school" cells in occupations where few workers lack a diploma, folded into the high school cell. Because the age–sex profile is fitted once on the whole sample, the cell contrasts are not the contrasts that separate regressions within each cell would give.

## Which cells the regression listens to

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

The survey-weighted regression with cell fixed effects is an exact weighted average of the <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> cell contrasts. Before reading on: do the largest cells get the most weight? What happens to a large cell in which almost nobody works from home?

</Callout>

A comparison within a cell needs both kinds of worker. A cell where almost everyone commutes offers only a handful of home workers, so however large it is, it says little about the home–commute contrast, and a regression that compares only within cells leans on the cells where both groups are plentiful. With a full set of cell fixed effects, the coefficient equals

$$
\hat\beta = \frac{\sum_c \omega_c\, \hat\tau_c}{\sum_c \omega_c}, \qquad \omega_c = N_c\, p_c (1 - p_c),
$$

where $$N_c$$ is the cell's weighted population and $$p_c$$ its weighted share working from home <Cite id="angrist1998" />. A cell's weight is its size times the variance of the work-from-home indicator inside it, so the answer to the prediction is no: a cell where almost everyone commutes has $$p_c(1-p_c)$$ near zero, however large it is. <Cite id="aronow2016" narrative /> write the same weights at the level of individuals, $$\omega_i = (D_i - \hat E[D_i \mid X_i])^2$$; with cell effects alone, the individual weights times their survey weights $$w_i$$ add up to $$N_c p_c(1-p_c)$$.

The weights are never negative in this form, and survey weights enter them only through $$N_c$$ and $$p_c$$, making those two quantities estimates for the population rather than the sample. Survey weighting does not decide that the factor $$p_c(1-p_c)$$ belongs in the average; the regression does.

<Details summary="Why the identity is exact with age and sex in the model">

The age–sex coefficients $$\hat\gamma$$ come from the same least-squares fit as $$\hat\beta$$, so holding them at $$\hat\gamma$$ and refitting only the treatment and cell effects returns the same $$\hat\beta$$. That refit is a regression of the adjusted outcome $$y_i - z_i'\hat\gamma$$ on the treatment and the cell dummies alone, to which Angrist's algebra applies. The build recomputes the coefficient from the adjusted cell contrasts and weights and matches the regression to machine precision; [Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations#variance-weighting-in-ols-with-a-binary-treatment) gives the derivation, including this step.

</Details>

## One set of contrasts, several averages

Figure 5.3 sets the two cell regressions beside three averages of the same <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> contrasts: the *population-share average*, which weights each cell by its share of workers; the *home-worker-share average*, by its share of home workers; and the *commuter-share average*, by its share of commuters. Chapter 4's coefficient appears as a muted reference. The three averages copy the weighting schemes of the population average treatment effect, the effect on the treated, and the effect on the untreated, so the figure also labels them PATE-, ATT-, and ATU-analogues. The unweighted regression gives every record the same case weight, but it does not give every cell's contrast the same weight, or a weight in proportion to its sample size: its implicit weights are $$n_c \hat p_c(1-\hat p_c)$$. Across cells they are almost identical to the survey-weighted regression's (correlation <Fact id="ch5.reg_share_corr" />). Equal case weights do not imply equal weighting of heterogeneous conditional effects.

## The regression-effective sample

A survey-weighted regression can represent the population correctly in the design sense, every cell at its estimated population size, while placing highly unequal implicit weight on heterogeneous conditional contrasts. The implicit weights form the third of the booklet's four effective samples ([Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations#four-effective-samples)), a composition rather than a count. Figure 5.4 sums them within occupation groups and sets each group's share of the weight against its share of workers.

<Figure n="5.4" caption="Occupations where home work is common gain regression weight; large occupations where it is rare lose most of theirs.">
  <Chart id="ch5.effective_sample" />
</Figure>

The concentration in the opening runs through the whole distribution. The <Fact id="ch5.lowp_n_cells" /> cells where fewer than one worker in twenty works from home hold <Fact id="ch5.lowp_pop" /> of workers and <Fact id="ch5.lowp_reg" /> of the weight; cells where a quarter or more work from home hold <Fact id="ch5.hip_pop" /> of workers and <Fact id="ch5.hip_reg" /> of the weight. Measured as an effective number of equally weighted cells (the inverse of the sum of squared shares), the population's <Fact id="ch5.eff_cells_pop" /> shrink to <Fact id="ch5.eff_cells_reg" /> in the regression, and a worker in the most balanced cell counts <Fact id="ch5.per_worker_ratio" /> times as much as a worker in the least balanced one.

<Details summary="In code: the four averages from the cell table">

The implicit weights and the three averages can be reproduced from the cell data behind the interactive below.

```python
import numpy as np

share, p, tau = cells["pop_share"], cells["treat_share"], cells["effect"]
omega = share * p * (1 - p)                     # Angrist (1998) implicit weights

projection = np.sum(omega * tau) / np.sum(omega)    # = survey-weighted coefficient
pate = np.sum(share * tau) / np.sum(share)           # population-share average
att = np.sum(share * p * tau) / np.sum(share * p)    # home-worker-share average
atu = np.sum(share * (1 - p) * tau) / np.sum(share * (1 - p))  # commuter-share average
```

Every aggregate in this chapter, including the population-share average, gets its standard error by rerunning the whole calculation on each of the 80 replicate weights.

</Details>

## What the survey weights did and did not do

The opening's two numbers are both averages of the same <Fact id="ch5.n_cells" /> contrasts. The survey-weighted regression, <Fact id="ch5.cellfe_wls" />, weights each cell by $$N_c p_c(1-p_c)$$; the population-share average, <Fact id="ch5.pate" />, weights it by $$N_c$$. `PERWT` did its job in both: every cell's population and share working from home are estimated for the population, and the weighted and unweighted regressions agree, as in Chapter 4. What the weights cannot do is choose the average. With heterogeneous contrasts, a regression that compares within cells leans on overlap cells, here the college-educated managers, business and computer workers, and office staff among whom home work is common enough to compare. The population's average contrast is a different and smaller number, and survey weights will not turn a regression coefficient into it <Cite id="solon2015" />.

Four habits follow for anyone reporting a regression like this one.

1. **Name the target.** The population projection, a population-weighted average of conditional contrasts, and an average for the treated are three different numbers.
2. **If the population average is the target, estimate it directly.** Compute the contrast in each cell, or interact the treatment with the cells, and aggregate with population shares. This counts small cells, whose contrasts rest on few home workers, at full population weight, and it depends on how the workers were divided into cells.
3. **Report the regression-effective sample next to the coefficient.** The implicit weights by group against population shares, as in Figure 5.4 and the table inside the interactive, and the group contrasts beside them, since unequal weights matter only where the contrasts differ (Figure 5.6).
4. **When the controls enter additively rather than as a full set of cells, check the two representations in the next section.** Some implied weights can turn negative, and the weight on the treated group's term can sit far from the treated share.

## Going further: signed weights and group weights

The headline needs nothing beyond the cell weights above. Two further representations of the same kind of coefficient apply when controls enter additively, and their labels invite confusion. Each weights something different.

| Representation | What is weighted | Model | Can a weight be negative? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell weights $$N_c\, p_c(1-p_c)$$ <Cite id="angrist1998" /> | cell contrasts | a full set of cell effects | no |
| Individual weights $$(D_i - \hat E[D_i \mid X_i])^2$$ <Cite id="aronow2016" /> | individuals' conditional contrasts | linear controls, with a treatment probability linear in them | no |
| Signed weights proportional to $$D_i - \hat\ell_i$$ <Cite id="chattopadhyay2023" /> | individual outcomes, treated against controls | any linear controls | yes |
| Group weights $$\omega_1$$ and $$\omega_0$$ <Cite id="sloczynski2022" /> | the two groups' average partial linear effects | additive linear controls | no, but the terms are not cell averages |

### When the weights turn negative

With a full set of cell fixed effects the weights are non-negative. With linear covariates they need not be. <Cite id="chattopadhyay2023" narrative /> show that an OLS coefficient on a binary treatment is a difference between a weighted mean of treated outcomes and a weighted mean of control outcomes, with the weight on person $$i$$ proportional to $$D_i - \hat\ell_i$$ (times $$w_i$$ in a survey-weighted regression), where $$\hat\ell_i$$ is the fitted value from a linear probability model of the treatment on the controls. A commuter whose fitted probability is below zero enters with the wrong sign, as if counted among the home workers.

In Chapter 4's headline specification this does not happen: fitted probabilities of working from home run from <Fact id="ch5.lpm_min_head" /> to <Fact id="ch5.lpm_max_head" />, so every implied weight is positive. Add fixed effects for detailed occupation, industry, and state, as on the last rung of Chapter 4's ladder, and the additive linear probability model gives fitted values from <Fact id="ch5.lpm_min_occind" /> to <Fact id="ch5.lpm_max_occind" />. Then <Fact id="ch5.neg_commuters" /> of commuters, weighted by `PERWT`, carry negative weight (Figure 5.7). Their weights are small, together <Fact id="ch5.neg_mass" /> of the total absolute weight, but they are concentrated where home work is rare: in <Fact id="ch5.neg_top_occ" />, <Fact id="ch5.neg_top_occ_share" /> of commuters are counted with the wrong sign.<SideNote>Strictly, even the cell model's linear age and sex terms push a few fitted probabilities just below zero (the lowest is <Fact id="ch5.m5_lpm_min" />, affecting <Fact id="ch5.m5_neg_share" /> of commuters). The cell-level weights above are exact and remain non-negative.</SideNote>

### Smaller groups get larger weights

<Callout kind="predict" title="Predict first">

Only <Fact id="ch5.sl_rho_wls" /> of workers work from home. A population average gives the contrast for home workers that share of the weight and the contrast for commuters the rest. How much weight do you expect the regression to give each?

</Callout>

<Cite id="sloczynski2022" narrative /> shows that an OLS coefficient on a binary treatment with additive controls can be written as follows; with weighted moments, the same holds for a survey-weighted regression:

$$
\hat\beta = \omega_1\, \ATT + \omega_0\, \ATU, \qquad \omega_1 = \frac{(1-\rho)\, V_0}{\rho\, V_1 + (1-\rho)\, V_0}, \quad \omega_0 = 1 - \omega_1 ,
$$

where $$\omega_1$$ is the implicit weight on the treated group's term (Słoczyński writes $$w_1$$, the symbol the table and ledger keep), $$\rho$$ is the share treated, and $$V_d$$ is the variance of the linear propensity score (the fitted values of a linear probability model of the treatment on the controls) among people with treatment status $$d$$. If the propensity score were equally variable in both groups, $$\omega_1 = 1-\rho$$: OLS would give the treated group's effect the weight that belongs to the untreated group, and vice versa. The smaller group gets the larger weight.

<Figure n="5.8" caption="Home workers are a small minority, yet the home-worker term gets most of the regression's weight.">
  <Chart id="ch5.sloczynski" />
</Figure>

In the survey-weighted cell regression (Figure 5.8), <Fact id="ch5.sl_rho_wls" /> of workers work from home and the weight on the home-worker term is <Fact id="ch5.sl_w1_wls" />. The unweighted regression, in which <Fact id="ch5.sl_rho_ols" /> of records are home workers, gives <Fact id="ch5.sl_w1_ols" />, and Chapter 4's model with education only gives <Fact id="ch5.sl4_w1" />. That is the same pattern as Figure 5.3, where the regression sat next to the home-worker-share average.

Słoczyński's $$\ATT$$ and $$\ATU$$ are not the cell-based averages of Figure 5.3. They are average partial linear effects: within each group, log wage is projected on the propensity score by a straight line, and each term compares one group's mean with the other group's line evaluated at the first group's mean score. Here they are <Fact id="ch5.sl_att" /> for home workers and <Fact id="ch5.sl_atu" /> for commuters, implying an average partial effect of <Fact id="ch5.sl_ape" />, against cell-based averages of <Fact id="ch5.att" /> and <Fact id="ch5.atu" />. The two pairs disagree even on which group's contrast is larger, because within groups mean log wage is not a straight-line function of the propensity score across occupation-by-education cells. The commuter term is the most exposed: it evaluates the home workers' line, fitted mostly where home work is common, at the low scores typical of commuters.

What the decomposition does establish is its weights. They depend only on the share working from home and on how much the propensity score varies within each group, and applied to the cell-based averages they give <Fact id="ch5.sl_hybrid" />, close to the fitted coefficient. It does not establish a causal result. Reading the two terms as causal quantities would require unconfoundedness as well as linearity, and this descriptive analysis claims neither. Read Figure 5.8 for its weights, not for its ATT and ATU columns.

<Callout kind="note" title="A new case">

A large cell holds many workers, but almost none of them works from home, and its contrast differs from the other cells'. How does the population-share average treat that cell, how does the regression treat it, and which summary is wrong?

<Details summary="One answer">

The population-share average counts the cell at its full population share, so its contrast, which rests on the few home workers there and is imprecise, carries a lot of weight. The regression gives the cell almost nothing, because $$p_c(1-p_c)$$ is near zero. The two summaries therefore diverge, and neither is wrong: they answer different questions. The population-share average describes the whole workforce, including that cell's workers; the regression's summary mostly describes cells where home work is common. A report should say which question it answers and show the cell's contrast with its interval.

</Details>

- **A causal effect of working from home.** Who works from home, and in which jobs, is selected in ways the cells and the age–sex profile do not remove; the home-worker-share and commuter-share averages borrow only the weighting schemes of the ATT and ATU.
- **An average that does not depend on the cells.** The cells impose a common age–sex profile, <Fact id="ch5.n_merges" /> thin cells were merged, and a different partition, such as occupation by age, would give different contrasts and different weights.
- **Precise contrasts in small cells.** Small cells have imprecise contrasts, and the population-share average counts them at full population weight.
- **A telework contrast.** Chapter 4's construct caveat applies: `TRANWORK` records the usual means of getting to work, not any telework.
- **Słoczyński's ATT and ATU.** Here they are linear-projection summaries that disagree with the cell-based averages, not estimates of the causal quantities.
- **A discrimination estimate.** The gender contrast within cells is descriptive. Sorting into occupations, education, and home work is held fixed by the cells and controls, not explained, and the agreement of the two summaries says nothing about what the gap is a gap of.
