You have the file. The codebook lists a weight, and near it a stratum, a PSU, and perhaps eighty or a hundred and sixty more columns whose names end in numbers. Which of them go into `svyset`, or `svydesign`, or `svy.Design`, and what happens if you get it wrong? The file will not say, and neither will the software: it accepts most wrong declarations and prints a standard error. This chapter answers the question for the six surveys the book uses. It opens with the two ways a wrong declaration announces itself, silently and loudly, and each guide then states one number for what the wrong declaration would have cost.

Every September the Census Bureau publishes the share of Americans who went without health insurance for the whole of the previous year, estimated from the Current Population Survey's Annual Social and Economic Supplement, and every September someone sets out to reproduce it. The 2025 file gives them what IPUMS documents: the person weight `ASECWT` and its <Fact id="ch8.asec_n_reps" /> replicate weights. Ask `svy` (version `0.28`, the version tested here) for the share uninsured in calendar 2024 twice, once with the default estimation call and once with `method="replication"` and `variance_center="estimate"`. Both calls return <Fact id="ch8.asec_uninsured" />. Both look like a successful replication. The default call reports <Fact id="ch8.asec_df_default" /> degrees of freedom and a standard error of <Fact id="ch8.asec_se_default" /> percentage points; the replication call reports <Fact id="ch8.asec_df" /> degrees of freedom and <Fact id="ch8.asec_se_rep" />, <Fact id="ch8.asec_se_ratio" /> the default value. Only the second matches the Census Bureau's formula computed by hand. The first number gives the default call away: it ignored the declared replicate weights and linearized as if every person were an independent draw, so its degrees of freedom are the sample size minus one. The interval is <Fact id="ch8.asec_uninsured" ci />. Nothing warned. Declaring a design and using it are different steps, and a year-over-year change that clears the first standard error need not clear the second.

The second failure is loud. Declare BRFSS 2024 as CDC documents it, with the final weight, the strata `_STSTR`, and the PSU `_PSU`, and ask for any national prevalence. R's `survey` package stops, and `svy` raises a `SingletonError`: <Fact id="ch8.brfss_singletons" /> strata in the full national file hold a single respondent, and a stratum with one PSU has no within-stratum variation to measure. Code that silently skips such strata, as many hand-rolled formulas do, treats them as contributing no variance at all. The declaration is incomplete until it includes a rule for single-PSU strata.

The six guides below each declare one current release: what it is and whom it covers, which weight, which variance method with which constant and degrees of freedom, what to do about lonely PSUs and pooled years, where the series break, and the traps that catch careful analysts. Each opens with the question a researcher in one field brings to that survey, because the trap is usually hiding inside that question. Jump to a survey: [ACS](#acs-2024-1-year), [CPS ASEC](#cps-asec-2025), [NHIS](#nhis-2024), [ATUS](#atus-2024), [NHANES](#nhanes-august-2021august-2023), [BRFSS](#brfss-2024).

## How to read a guide

Each guide is a table with three columns: the item, the declaration, and its provenance. The build script generates the tables from the data pipeline's design manifests, the producers' documentation, and counts taken from the data, and provenance takes one of six forms: **design.json**, a machine-readable field of the dataset's design manifest; **design.json notes**, the manifest's free text, which is where most of the pipeline's datasets still keep their variance tier, lonely-PSU rule, pooling rule, and series breaks; **producer**, documentation from the Census Bureau, BLS, NCHS, CDC, or IPUMS, read on the date in the "Last verified" row; **computed from data**; **verified in this build**, a behavior the script confirmed by running code; and **book guidance**, an editorial recommendation where neither manifest nor producer states a rule.

Each guide carries its declaration in Python (`svy`), R (`survey`), and Stata, folded into a block below the table. The Python and R declarations for NHIS, NHANES, BRFSS, ATUS, and CPS ASEC were executed against the book's store and give the same estimates and standard errors; the ACS declarations were checked on a synthetic frame with the same column names, not on the three-gigabyte replicate file; the Stata declarations follow the producers' published examples and were not executed, because Stata is not installed on the build machine.

A variance tier is a property of the extract, not a choice. Replicate designs (ACS, CPS ASEC) and Taylor designs (NHIS, NHANES, BRFSS) support design-based uncertainty. The book's ATUS extract carries weights only, so a standard error computed from it is not design-based, and nothing in the extract says whether it errs high or low.

<Details summary="Variable names in the code">

The code mixes three kinds of name, and a copy has to keep them apart. In the ACS, CPS ASEC, NHIS, and ATUS snippets, UPPERCASE names are IPUMS variables. In the NHANES snippets they are NCHS names, except `CYCLE_YEAR`, which the book's store adds. The BRFSS snippets use the store's names: `xststr`, `xpsu`, and `xllcpwt` are CDC's `_STSTR`, `_PSU`, and `_LLCPWT`, and `iyear` and `Health` are harmonized store variables; `Health` has two levels, and `"Poor"` means fair or poor. Lowercase outcomes such as `uninsured`, `uninsured_ly`, `current_smoker`, and `htn_measured` are derived in the store, and `fairpoor` is built in the snippet itself; an extract of your own has to define them. Within each survey, the three languages use the same target, the same rule for missing outcomes, and the same variance method.

</Details>

## ACS 2024 1-year

An economist who needs a state-by-year panel of uninsured rates, or a wage regression with a million workers and occupation fixed effects, comes to the American Community Survey because no other survey is large enough, and brings a question the sample size seems to settle: with three million records, does the design still matter? The trap that catches careful analysts is the published margin of error. The public-use microdata are a subsample of the full ACS, so a correct replicate standard error from the PUMS will not reproduce the margin the Census Bureau publishes from the full sample, and an analyst who reads the gap as a failed replication goes looking for a bug that is not there.

The design matters at any sample size. [Lab 3](/ch03-lab-replicates) estimates the national uninsured rate among adults aged 19 to 64 from <Fact id="ch3lab.us_n" /> records; the replicate standard error is <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_us" /> the standard error that treats those records as independent draws, and the median state's is <Fact id="ch3lab.ratio_median" />. The silent failure of the opening recurs here: for Texas, `svy`'s default call reports a standard error of <Fact id="ch3lab.svy_default_se" /> percentage points with <Fact id="ch3lab.svy_default_df" /> degrees of freedom, and the replication call <Fact id="ch3lab.svy_rep_se" /> with <Fact id="ch8.acs_df" />.

The 2024 1-year sample holds <Fact id="ch8.acs_n_persons" /> person records in the IPUMS extract. Its public design lives entirely in replicate weights: <Fact id="ch8.acs_n_reps" /> person weights and as many household weights, built by successive difference replication. The variance is the constant <Fact id="ch8.acs_scale" /> times the sum of squared deviations of the replicate estimates from the full-sample estimate, with <Fact id="ch8.acs_df" /> degrees of freedom. There are no strata or PSUs to declare alongside them: IPUMS says to treat the sample as a single stratum. Person estimates use `PERWT`; household estimates use `HHWT` on the first person of each household. Figure 8.2 lists each item of the declaration with its provenance.

</Figure>

# Paths point at the book's store; adapt them to your own extract.
import polars as pl
import svy

keys = ["SAMPLE", "SERIAL", "PERNUM"]
acs2024 = pl.col("SAMPLE") == 202401  # IPUMS sample code of the 2024 1-year file
p = (
    pl.scan_parquet("ipums/analysis/usa/acs/part_2020_2024.parquet")
    .filter(acs2024)
    .select(keys + ["PERWT", "uninsured"])
)
reps = [f"REPWTP{i}" for i in range(1, 81)]  # by name, never a REPWTP* wildcard
r = (
    pl.scan_parquet("ipums/analysis/usa/acs_repwt/part_2020_2024.parquet")
    .filter(acs2024)
    .select(keys + reps)
)
d = p.join(r, on=keys, how="inner").collect()

rep = svy.SdrWgts(prefix="REPWTP", n_reps=80)
s = svy.Sample(d, design=svy.Design(wgt="PERWT", rep_wgts=rep))

assert est.to_dicts()[0]["df"] == 79  # the default call would silently report n - 1
```

## CPS ASEC 2025

A political scientist who has estimated turnout from the November supplement comes to the March file for the income, poverty, and coverage measures the voting file lacks, and meets <Fact id="ch8.asec_n_reps" /> columns the voting file never had. The Annual Social and Economic Supplement is the source of the official income, poverty, and health-insurance statistics; it is fielded in the first months of the year and asks about the year before, so the 2025 file describes calendar 2024. Its trap is the sign of the replicate weights. Some are negative, Stata refuses negative probability weights, and an analyst who meets the error is tempted to truncate or drop the offending replicates. They are legitimate for variance estimation, which is why IPUMS declares them as importance weights in Stata (Figure 8.3). The cost of ignoring them altogether is the opening's number: a standard error <Fact id="ch8.asec_se_ratio" /> too small for the uninsured share, and a sample-size degrees of freedom in place of <Fact id="ch8.asec_df" />.

The IPUMS extract has <Fact id="ch8.asec_n_persons" /> person records with a positive supplement weight. Its <Fact id="ch8.asec_n_reps" /> replicate weights combine successive difference replication in self-representing strata with modified half-sample replication elsewhere; the variance constant is <Fact id="ch8.asec_scale" />, and the degrees of freedom are <Fact id="ch8.asec_df" />.

Two `svy 0.28` behaviors were verified in the build. Once the replication method is requested, `svy` honors the `scale` and `df` arguments of its replicate-weight specification; only the default call ignores the specification, and it ignores all of it. ## NHIS 2024

A health economist who needs a national smoking or coverage rate by year, with a standard error a referee will accept, comes to the National Health Interview Survey and asks a question that sounds already answered: the file has `STRATA` and `PSU`, so is `vce(cluster psu)` enough? The NHIS is the principal source on the health of the civilian noninstitutionalized population: coverage, conditions, access, and behaviors such as smoking, reported quarterly. Its trap is in the PSU codes. There are only <Fact id="ch8.nhis_psu_codes" /> distinct codes, reused across strata, so a PSU is identified by the pair of stratum and PSU, and code that groups or clusters on the PSU code alone merges areas from different strata without a warning.

The 2024 public file holds <Fact id="ch8.nhis_n_adults" /> sample adults and <Fact id="ch8.nhis_n_children" /> sample children, each with a weight that sums to the adult or child population. The design has <Fact id="ch8.nhis_strata" /> strata and <Fact id="ch8.nhis_psus" /> PSUs, so the common rule of PSUs minus strata gives <Fact id="ch8.nhis_df" /> degrees of freedom. NCHS cautions that the rule is not directly applicable to the NHIS design and that it depends on the variable, and says a normal approximation may be adequate when a variable is spread across most clusters (Figure 8.4).

The cost of the PSU trap is measurable. For adult current cigarette smoking, <Fact id="ch8.nhis_smoking" ci />, the declared design gives a standard error of <Fact id="ch8.nhis_se_taylor" /> percentage points. A cluster-robust shortcut on the stratum-PSU key gives <Fact id="ch8.nhis_se_crv1" />, <Fact id="ch8.nhis_crv1_ratio" /> the design-based standard error, because it ignores the strata and so omits the stratification gain ([Appendix B](/appendix-b-derivations)). Clustering on the PSU code alone gives <Fact id="ch8.nhis_se_label" />, <Fact id="ch8.nhis_label_ratio" /> the design-based figure, and nothing about it looks wrong. `svy` nests PSU codes within the declared strata on its own, and R's `svydesign` stops with an error unless told `nest = TRUE`; both behaviors were confirmed by the build.

  <Chart id="ch8.guide_nhis" />
</Figure>

import svy

d = pl.read_parquet(
    "ipums/analysis/nhis/nhis_core/part_2015_2024.parquet",
    columns=["YEAR", "STRATA", "PSU", "SAMPWEIGHT", "ASTATFLG", "current_smoker"],
).filter(pl.col("YEAR") == 2024)  # one annual file is one design

est = s.estimation.mean("current_smoker", where=svy.col("ASTATFLG") == 1, drop_nulls=True)

```

Two more rules complete the declaration. Every stratum in the full 2024 file has at least <Fact id="ch8.nhis_min_psus" /> PSUs, so single-PSU strata arise only in small domains, where the manifest's rule (centering, equivalent to R's `adjust`) applies and PSUs retained should be reported beside the standard error. ## ATUS 2024

A sociologist studying the division of housework, or time with children, or the length of the working day, comes to the American Time Use Survey and asks whether a weighted mean of minutes describes people or days, and whether it can carry a standard error. The ATUS is the country's only continuous record of how people spend their days, and the source of the "how much do Americans work" estimands of [Chapter 1](/ch01-estimands). Its trap is the unit. The weight `WT06` sums to person-days, not persons, so an average over the weighted file is an average over days, and a standard error computed from the book's extract, which carries no design variables, is not a design-based standard error however carefully it is labeled.

The unit is not a nicety. Weekend diaries are <Fact id="ch8.atus_weekend_share_sample" /> of the 2024 sample and <Fact id="ch8.atus_weekend_share_weighted" /> of weighted person-days, so the unweighted mean of minutes worked per diary day is <Fact id="ch8.atus_work_unweighted" /> and the weighted mean per person-day is <Fact id="ch8.atus_work_weighted" />. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions, and only the second is the one the sociologist asked. Because the extract carries weights only, the chapter reports no standard error for either.

The survey interviews one person per household, drawn from households that have finished their final CPS interview, about a single designated day; the 2024 file has <Fact id="ch8.atus_n" /> respondents (Figure 8.5). The design assigns a quarter of the sample to each weekend day and a tenth to each weekday, and the weight returns weekdays to about five-sevenths of person-days. BLS and IPUMS supply <Fact id="ch8.atus_n_reps" /> replicate weights for each final weight; the variance is <Fact id="ch8.atus_scale" /> times the sum of squared deviations from the full-sample estimate, the four coming from replicate factors of $$1.7$$, $$1.0$$, and $$0.3$$. The declaration shows both the store's weights-only form and the replicate form to use once the replicate weights are added to the extract.

## NHANES August 2021–August 2023

An epidemiologist who wants the prevalence of hypertension from measured blood pressure, not from what people report, comes to NHANES and asks which of its several weights to use and how many degrees of freedom a few thousand examinees really carry. NHANES is the one national survey that measures rather than asks: it interviews people at home and then examines them in a mobile center, which is why the hypertension figures of [Chapter 3](/ch03-design) rest on blood-pressure readings instead of self-report. Its trap is the calendar. Two earlier files overlap, the 2017–2018 cycle and the 2017–March 2020 pre-pandemic file, and they cover the same field period with different participant numbers; an analyst who stacks them to gain sample size counts the same people twice.

The degrees of freedom are the second trap, and they can be priced. Among examined adults, <Fact id="ch8.nhanes_htn" ci /> have hypertension by measured pressure or medication; the declared design gives a standard error of <Fact id="ch8.nhanes_se_design" /> percentage points. Drop the pseudo-strata and pseudo-PSUs and keep the weight, as a regression with robust standard errors would, and the standard error falls to <Fact id="ch8.nhanes_se_wonly" />: the design figure is <Fact id="ch8.nhanes_wonly_ratio" /> the weights-only one, and the interval also widens because a t with <Fact id="ch8.nhanes_df" /> degrees of freedom replaces the normal (<Fact id="ch3.t_crit_df" /> in place of <Fact id="ch3.t_crit_z" />).

In the August 2021–August 2023 cycle <Fact id="ch8.nhanes_n_int" /> people were interviewed and <Fact id="ch8.nhanes_n_exam" /> of them examined, and the two stages have different weights. Examination variables, and laboratory variables measured on every examinee, use the exam weight `WTMEC2YR`, alone or combined with interview variables; interview-only analyses use `WTINT2YR`. Analytes measured on a subsample carry their own weights, such as the fasting subsample's `WTSAF2YR`, and this release adds a phlebotomy weight. NCHS's rule is to use the weight of the smallest subsample whose variables enter the analysis (Figure 8.6 lists the alternates). The public design is masked: <Fact id="ch8.nhanes_strata" /> pseudo-strata of two pseudo-PSUs each, <Fact id="ch8.nhanes_psus" /> in all, which leaves <Fact id="ch8.nhanes_df" /> design degrees of freedom however many people are examined.

This cycle is not a continuation of earlier ones. It dropped the oversampling by race, Hispanic origin, and income, selected everyone aged 0 to 19 or 60 and older in a sampled household, used a self-administered screener, and had much lower response rates; NCHS's assessment found no major bias that weighting did not address. Blood pressure has been measured with an oscillometric device since 2017, which reads lower than the earlier auscultatory method.

## BRFSS 2024

A health economist evaluating a state policy on self-reported health comes to BRFSS for its state sample sizes and asks two things: can the pooled estimate be called national, and with every respondent a PSU, is there any design left to declare? The Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System is a telephone survey run by the states, designed for state-level estimates of health behaviors and conditions, and pooled into the largest health survey in the country. Its trap is the word "national". Tennessee could not collect enough data to meet the minimum requirements in 2024, so an estimate from this file describes adults in participating jurisdictions and should be labeled that way; coverage changes from year to year, since the 2023 file omits Kentucky and Pennsylvania and the 2021 file omits Florida.

The second question has a measured answer, and it is the reverse of NHANES. The share of adults in fair or poor health is <Fact id="ch8.brfss_fairpoor" ci />, with a design standard error of <Fact id="ch8.brfss_se_design" /> percentage points. Drop the strata and PSUs and keep the weight, and the standard error is <Fact id="ch8.brfss_se_wonly" />: the design figure is <Fact id="ch8.brfss_wonly_ratio" /> the weights-only one, so the strata and PSUs change almost nothing. Drop the weight as well and the point estimate barely moves, to <Fact id="ch8.brfss_fairpoor_unw" />, but the standard error of a simple random sample of this size would be <Fact id="ch8.brfss_se_srs" />, <Fact id="ch8.brfss_srs_ratio" /> too small. For this outcome the weights carry the uncertainty rather than the estimate, and the declaration's strata matter mainly because they stop the software.

The 2024 public file has <Fact id="ch8.brfss_n" /> respondents in <Fact id="ch8.brfss_jurisdictions" /> jurisdictions: 49 states, the District of Columbia, Guam, Puerto Rico, and the US Virgin Islands. The weight is built in stages: a design weight from the telephone stratum, the adults in the household, and the household's landlines; a compositing adjustment for respondents reachable on both frames; truncation within regions; and raking to state population margins. The book's store keeps only the final weight; [Chapter 2](/ch02-weights) rebuilds the chain from the raw file. The variance design has <Fact id="ch8.brfss_strata" /> strata and <Fact id="ch8.brfss_psus" /> PSUs, one per respondent, which gives <Fact id="ch8.brfss_df" /> design degrees of freedom. BRFSS uncertainty comes from weighting and stratification, not from clustering: the opposite of NHANES (Figure 8.7).

## Three worksheets

Each item below is a decision to make and write down; the Analysis Ledger is where the answers go.

### Describing a population

- [ ] Name the target population and check the release's coverage against it: missing states, group quarters, the institutionalized, the months collected.
- [ ] State the unit and the denominator: persons, households, person-days, or examined adults.
- [ ] Choose the weight that matches the unit and the source of every variable: person or household, interview or exam, core or module version.
- [ ] Declare the whole design first: strata and PSUs, or the replicate weights with their method, constant, and centering.
- [ ] Estimate subgroups as domains of the full design, never by deleting rows; report PSUs retained and lonely strata beside the standard error.
- [ ] Decide the lonely-PSU rule deliberately and write it down.
- [ ] Report the estimate, standard error, design degrees of freedom, unweighted count, and coefficient of variation; suppress cells too small to support an estimate.
- [ ] If rates are compared across populations with different age structures, say whether they are crude or standardized, and to which standard.
- [ ] Before comparing years, check for series breaks and pooling rules.
- [ ] Compare with a published figure where one exists, and label it an external benchmark.

### Estimating an effect

- [ ] Write the estimand before the regression: a population average effect, the effect on the treated, a population projection coefficient, or a descriptive adjusted contrast.
- [ ] Say whether a causal reading is claimed, and if so, under what assumption; otherwise call the contrast descriptive.
- [ ] Ask whether selection into the sample depends on the outcome (endogenous sampling). If it does, weights are needed for consistency; if it depends only on the regressors, an unweighted fit of a correctly specified model is consistent, and weighting changes the estimand under heterogeneity rather than rescuing consistency.
- [ ] Run the regression weighted and unweighted, and treat a difference as a diagnostic of heterogeneity, misspecification, or endogenous sampling, not a verdict.
- [ ] Compute the implicit weights: which cells the coefficient averages over, and how they compare with their population shares.
- [ ] Check overlap: list the cells where almost everyone or almost no one is treated.
- [ ] If the target is a population average, model the heterogeneity and average the effects over the population distribution.
- [ ] Choose the standard errors with the next worksheet.
- [ ] Write down what the analysis does not establish.

### Choosing standard errors

- [ ] Name the repetition the standard error describes: resampling units, reassigning treatment, nonresponse, or a model.
- [ ] List every mechanism that induces common uncertainty: survey PSUs within strata, assignment clusters such as states or schools, repeated observations of the same unit.
- [ ] If the mechanisms nest, cluster at the coarsest relevant level; if they do not, consider multiway clustering.
- [ ] Ask whether the sample holds most of the clusters in the population; if it does, conventional cluster-robust standard errors can be conservative.
- [ ] Count the independent units. With few clusters, use the wild cluster bootstrap or a small-sample-corrected variance and t critical values with the right degrees of freedom.
- [ ] For survey data, prefer the declared design (Taylor linearization or the producer's replicate weights) to a cluster-robust shortcut, which omits the stratification gain and, keyed on PSU codes alone, can merge unrelated PSUs.
- [ ] With replicate weights, use the producer's method, constant, and centering, and assert the degrees of freedom the software reports.
- [ ] With weights only, do not present an interval as design-based: its error can run in either direction. Keep to point estimates and estimand lessons, or obtain the design information.
- [ ] Report the choice, the assumption behind it, and one alternative as a sensitivity check.

## From checklist to methods paragraph

Checklists help a reader recognize the pieces; a methods section has to state them. A paragraph of a familiar kind, invented for illustration:

> *An example, not quoted from any paper.* We analyzed NHANES data with the survey weights to obtain nationally representative estimates, and standard errors accounted for the complex survey design. Hypertension control was compared across periods with t-tests.

Every sentence leaves a decision unstated: which population, definition, weight, design variables, degrees of freedom, denominator, and how many comparisons. The same analysis, written for this book's Chapter 3 result:

> Hypertension was defined as a mean of up to three oscillometric readings at or above 130/80 mm Hg, or current use of prescribed blood-pressure medication, following NCHS Data Brief 511. The target population is U.S. civilian noninstitutionalized adults 18 and older, excluding pregnant women, in August 2021–August 2023. Control is the share of adults with hypertension whose readings were below 130/80; it was estimated as a domain of the full design with the examination weight `WTMEC2YR`. Standard errors use Taylor linearization with the masked strata and PSUs (`SDMVSTRA`, `SDMVPSU`), which leave <Fact id="ch3.nhanes_df" /> design degrees of freedom, and intervals use t with those degrees of freedom. Control was <Fact id="ch3.htn_control" ci />. Changes from the 2017–March 2020 sample were tested as differences between independent samples with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom; <Fact id="ch3.chg_sig_design" /> of the 24 period comparisons reached the 5 percent level, about as many as chance alone would produce. The medication question changed between the periods, and the estimates describe measured blood pressure, not diagnosed hypertension.

Each clause fills a field of the Analysis Ledger, and the referee's checklist below asks for the same fields.

## The tools: svy, R, and Stata

Four behaviors in this table were confirmed by running code: the `svy` estimation call ignores replicate weights unless the replication method is requested; its `where=` conditions reject 8- and 16-bit integer columns; R's `svydesign` stops when PSU codes repeat across strata unless `nest = TRUE` is set; and R stops on single-PSU strata under its default setting, which is the behavior to keep until the rule is chosen on purpose. The last row is a shortcut, not a declaration: its key must combine stratum and PSU, and even then it ignores the strata. The design-effect row compares with a with-replacement simple random sample in Python and R, the reference this book uses throughout; Stata's `estat effects` uses its own reference.

## A referee's and an advisor's checklist

Ten questions to ask of any survey-based result before it is submitted, each tied to the Ledger field that should already answer it.

| # | Question | Ledger field |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Whom is the result about, and does the release actually cover them (states, group quarters, months, the institutionalized)? | Target population |
| 2 | What is the estimand, in one sentence, with its unit (persons, households, person-days) and its denominator? | Estimand |
| 3 | What rule produced the number: a weighted mean, a ratio, a standardized rate, a weighted or unweighted regression? | Estimator |
| 4 | Which weight was used, and does it match the unit and the source of every variable (interview or exam, person or household, core or module)? | Explicit weights |
| 5 | Would dropping the weights change the answer, and if so, is that representation, misspecification, or a different estimand? | Explicit weights |
| 6 | With heterogeneous effects, which groups or cells dominate the coefficient, and do they resemble the population? | Implicit weights |
| 7 | Which repetition does the standard error describe: sampling, assignment, nonresponse, or a model? | Source of randomness |
| 8 | Does the variance estimator match the design: strata and PSUs nested correctly, the producer's replicate method and constant, clustering at the level of assignment? What are the degrees of freedom? | Variance estimator |
| 9 | Were subgroups estimated as domains of the full design, how many PSUs and lonely strata remain, and which rule handled them? | Variance estimator |
| 10 | What must hold for the interpretation (coverage, accurate measurement, ignorable nonresponse, a causal assumption), and what does the result not establish? | Assumptions |
